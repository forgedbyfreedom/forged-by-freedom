# How to Read Your Own Bloodwork: The Enhanced Athlete Reference Guide

Most bloodwork guides are written for sedentary patients whose biggest concern is whether their cholesterol is borderline high. If you're running a blast, managing a TRT cruise, running peptides, or coming off a cycle, that guidance is nearly useless to you. The ranges are wrong for your context, the markers they flag aren't the ones that matter most, and the guidance to "ask your doctor" lands hollow when most physicians have never managed an enhanced athlete in their life.

This guide is written for you — the person who actually needs to understand their labs.

---

## When to Pull Bloodwork

Timing matters as much as what you test. Here's the framework:

- **Pre-cycle baseline** — Before you touch anything. Establishes your personal reference points for everything below.
- **4-6 weeks into a cycle** — Catches E2 problems, hematocrit creep, and early liver stress while you still have time to intervene.
- **End of cycle** — Full picture of what the blast did. Informs whether you cruise or begin PCT.
- **During PCT** — Typically at weeks 4-6 of PCT. You're checking LH, FSH, and testosterone to see if the HPTA is waking up.
- **Post-PCT (8-12 weeks out)** — Confirms recovery. If LH, FSH, and total T are not back to your pre-cycle baseline by now, you have a problem worth taking seriously.

Don't skip the baseline. Without it, you're interpreting everything in a vacuum.

## Marker-by-Marker Breakdown

### Testosterone (Total and Free)

**What it is:** Total testosterone measures all circulating testosterone, both bound and unbound. Free testosterone is the biologically active fraction — unbound to SHBG or albumin.

**Standard lab range:** Total T: 264–916 ng/dL. Free T: 9–30 pg/mL (varies by assay).

**Enhanced athlete context:** On a blast, total T will be supraphysiological by design — 1,500 to 5,000+ ng/dL depending on dose. On TRT or a cruise, you're typically targeting 700–1,100 ng/dL. Free T matters more than most people realize; low SHBG can mean your free fraction is high even with modest total T.

**What moves it:** Dose and ester are the primary drivers. SHBG levels affect free T significantly. Liver stress elevates SHBG. Thyroid dysfunction also affects SHBG.

**When to intervene:** On cruise, if total T is below 500 ng/dL and you feel it, adjust dose. On blast, total T itself is rarely the concern — it's what the testosterone is converting to (E2, DHT) that creates problems.

### Estradiol (E2) — Sensitive Assay Only

**What it is:** The primary estrogen in men. Aromatase converts testosterone to E2, particularly in adipose tissue. Use the sensitive (LC/MS-MS) assay — the standard immunoassay is notoriously inaccurate for men.

**Standard lab range:** 8–35 pg/mL (sensitive assay for men).

**Enhanced athlete context:** On a testosterone blast, many athletes feel best in the 20–50 pg/mL range. Some tolerate higher. Low E2 is just as problematic as high E2 — the joint pain, libido crash, and mood depression from over-crashed estrogen are real and common from AI overuse.

**What moves it:** Testosterone dose drives aromatization. Body fat percentage matters — higher fat = more aromatase activity. Compounds like Deca and Dianabol increase estrogenic load. AIs drop it; if you crash it, you'll know.

**When to intervene:** Symptomatic high E2 (water retention, emotional lability, sensitive nipples) with confirmed high labs warrants AI adjustment. Symptomatic low E2 (dry joints, no libido, brain fog) with confirmed low labs means pull back on the AI. Don't dose by symptoms alone — test first.

### Hematocrit / Hemoglobin / RBC

**What it is:** Hematocrit is the percentage of blood volume composed of red blood cells. Hemoglobin is the oxygen-carrying protein within RBCs. Together these reflect blood viscosity and oxygen-carrying capacity.

**Standard lab range:** Hematocrit: 38.3–48.6% (men). Hemoglobin: 13.2–17.1 g/dL.

**Enhanced athlete context:** Testosterone and especially Equipoise (EQ) stimulate erythropoiesis aggressively. Hematocrit at 50–52% is common and manageable. Above 54% begins to carry real cardiovascular risk — thickened blood increases clot risk and cardiac workload.

**What moves it:** Testosterone dose, EQ, high-altitude training, sleep apnea, and dehydration all elevate hematocrit. Hydration status matters enormously on draw day.

**When to intervene:** Above 52%, increase hydration and consider a therapeutic phlebotomy. Above 54–55%, pull blood — donate or have it drawn therapeutically. Address root cause: if it's EQ or high-dose testosterone, that compound is the driver.

### LDL / HDL / Total Cholesterol / Triglycerides

**What it is:** LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol to tissues and is associated with plaque formation. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) is cardioprotective. Total cholesterol is the sum of all fractions. Triglycerides reflect dietary fat and carbohydrate metabolism.

**Standard lab range:** LDL <100 mg/dL (optimal), HDL >40 mg/dL, Total <200 mg/dL, Triglycerides <150 mg/dL.

**Enhanced athlete context:** Oral AAS — particularly DHT derivatives like Winstrol, Anavar, and Superdrol — trash HDL rapidly. Expect HDL in the 20s or lower on aggressive oral stacks. Injectable testosterone is more forgiving but still suppresses HDL. Any AAS use will shift lipids unfavorably to some degree.

**What moves it:** Orals are the worst offenders. Tren hits lipids hard. Diet quality matters; saturated fat load compounds the issue. Fish oil (4g EPA/DHA daily) and cardio provide partial mitigation. Niacin is occasionally used but GI tolerance is poor.

**When to intervene:** HDL below 25 mg/dL on a prolonged cycle, or LDL spiking above 160 mg/dL, warrants compound reconsideration. Shorten oral windows, drop the offending compound, increase cardio. A statin (rosuvastatin is commonly used) may be appropriate if lipids remain severely dysregulated off cycle.

### ALT / AST (Liver Enzymes)

**What it is:** Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are intracellular enzymes released when liver cells are damaged. They're the standard markers for hepatotoxicity.

**Standard lab range:** ALT: 7–56 U/L. AST: 10–40 U/L.

**Enhanced athlete context:** Resistance training itself elevates AST — it's also released by muscle. A slightly elevated AST alone, in an athlete who trained hard before drawing labs, is not necessarily hepatotoxicity. ALT is the more liver-specific marker. Both elevated together is more meaningful.

**What moves it:** 17-alpha alkylated orals (Dianabol, Anadrol, Superdrol, Winstrol, Anavar, Halotestin) are the primary offenders. Alcohol compounds hepatic stress significantly. High-dose injectable testosterone is generally well-tolerated by the liver.

**When to intervene:** ALT or AST above 3x the upper limit of normal on an oral cycle = pull the oral. Use TUDCA (500mg/day with food) and NAC (600mg twice daily) as liver support throughout any oral cycle. If enzymes do not normalize within 4-6 weeks off orals, get a physician involved.

### eGFR / Creatinine (Kidney Function)

**What it is:** Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) reflects how well the kidneys are filtering waste. Serum creatinine is a byproduct of muscle metabolism used to calculate eGFR.

**Standard lab range:** eGFR >60 mL/min/1.73m2 (normal), Creatinine: 0.7–1.3 mg/dL.

**Enhanced athlete context:** High muscle mass artificially elevates creatinine and can make eGFR appear lower than actual kidney function. Athletes with 220+ lbs of lean mass will commonly read creatinine at 1.3–1.6 mg/dL with no kidney pathology. Context matters.

**What moves it:** Dehydration, high creatine supplementation, very high protein intake, and NSAIDs (ibuprofen) all stress kidneys. Compounds like Deca that cause water retention can actually be nephroprotective in some contexts. Trenbolone is rumored to be nephrotoxic in animal models; data in humans is limited.

**When to intervene:** Trending creatinine elevation across multiple draws, especially with declining eGFR, is more meaningful than a single reading. Stay well hydrated, limit NSAID use on cycle, and investigate if eGFR falls below 60 consistently.

### PSA (Prostate-Specific Antigen) — For Athletes 40+

**What it is:** A protein produced by the prostate gland. Elevated PSA can indicate prostate enlargement, inflammation, or malignancy.

**Standard lab range:** <4.0 ng/mL (general), though age-adjusted ranges apply. Below 2.5 ng/mL is preferred for men under 50.

**Enhanced athlete context:** Androgens stimulate the prostate. High-dose testosterone and especially DHT-heavy compounds (Masteron, Proviron, Winstrol) elevate PSA. This is not inherently pathological, but it warrants monitoring.

**What moves it:** High androgen load, especially DHT-derived compounds. Recent ejaculation, prostate exam, or cycling can transiently elevate it.

**When to intervene:** PSA above 4.0, or a rapid rise of >0.75 ng/mL over 12 months, warrants urological referral. Running high-androgen compounds with an existing prostate concern is contraindicated.

### TSH (Thyroid-Stimulating Hormone)

**What it is:** TSH is produced by the pituitary to stimulate thyroid hormone production. It's an indirect marker of thyroid function — high TSH suggests hypothyroidism, low TSH suggests hyperthyroidism or exogenous thyroid use.

**Standard lab range:** 0.4–4.0 mIU/L.

**Enhanced athlete context:** Many enhanced athletes run T3 (Cytomel) or T4 for metabolic acceleration during prep. Exogenous thyroid hormone suppresses TSH to near zero. This is expected and not inherently concerning on cycle. The concern is rebound hypothyroidism when it's removed.

**What moves it:** Exogenous T3/T4, iodine deficiency, autoimmune thyroiditis, and significant caloric restriction.

**When to intervene:** If TSH is elevated (above 4.0) without thyroid medication use, get a full thyroid panel (free T3, free T4, TPO antibodies). Don't self-medicate thyroid without a full picture.

---

### IGF-1 (Insulin-Like Growth Factor 1)

**What it is:** The primary mediator of growth hormone's anabolic effects, produced by the liver in response to GH. It's the standard marker used to assess GH adequacy and exogenous GH dosing.

**Standard lab range:** Age-dependent. For men 30–50: approximately 100–300 ng/mL.

**Enhanced athlete context:** On pharmaceutical GH at 2-4 IU/day, IGF-1 will typically run 300–500+ ng/mL. This is the functional target for most bodybuilding protocols. Levels above 600 ng/mL with aggressive GH dosing are common in competitive prep but carry long-term mitogenic risk.

**What moves it:** Exogenous GH dose is the primary driver. Sleep quality, protein intake, liver health, and insulin sensitivity all affect IGF-1 production. MK-677 also elevates IGF-1 via GH secretion.

**When to intervene:** If IGF-1 is not responding to GH use, suspect underdosed or degraded product — this is one of the most counterfeited compounds in the market. If IGF-1 is above 600 ng/mL, consider reducing GH dose.

### FSH / LH (Pituitary Gonadotropins) — PCT Monitoring

**What it is:** Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) are pituitary hormones that drive testosterone and sperm production. Both are suppressed to near zero during AAS use.

**Standard lab range:** LH: 1.7–8.6 IU/L. FSH: 1.5–12.4 IU/L.

**Enhanced athlete context:** On cycle, both will be suppressed. During PCT, you're watching for these to come back online — it's the sign that the HPTA is recovering. LH typically recovers before FSH. Testosterone recovery follows gonadotropin recovery.

**What moves it:** Exogenous androgens suppress both via negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary. SERMs (Nolvadex, Clomid) stimulate LH and FSH release by blocking estrogen's negative feedback signal at the pituitary. HCG mimics LH at the testicular level but does not drive pituitary recovery.

**When to intervene:** If LH and FSH remain suppressed 8+ weeks post-PCT with confirmed low testosterone, recovery is incomplete. Prolactin elevation (from 19-nor use) can suppress LH/FSH independently — check prolactin if recovery is stalling.

Decongestants are your go-to for that blocked, stuffy feeling. They work best when a cold, sinus infection, or other invader has your nose bound up, but they won't treat the infection. Antihistamines are built to take on your allergy symptoms, like sneezing, runny nose, watery eyes, and itchiness triggered by pollen, dust, or pet dander. And how you use them matters. Decongestants are a short-term fix. You might take them for a few days when your sinuses are on the fritz. They're not meant for long-term use, especially nasal spray decongestants. Using those more than three days straight can actually make your congestion worse. Antihistamines, especially the non-drowsy ones, are often used daily during allergy season to prevent symptoms. Think of them as a strategy for managing your allergies for the long haul. But how do they work their magic? When you have a respiratory infection, the blood vessels in your nasal passage swell. That doesn't leave room for air to flow. Decongestants shrink those blood vessels to open up some space. Antihistamines work by blocking histamine. That's a chemical your body releases during an allergic reaction. But what if it's both? What if you have allergies and a cold? First, our condolences. But no worries. We got you. You can take both or use a combination medicine like Claritin-D or Zyrtec-D, which include an antihistamine and a decongestant. Just check with your provider first, especially if you have high blood pressure, glaucoma, or other health concerns. If you choose a decongestant, check labels before you buy. A medication called oral phenylepharin was once common in over-the-counter decongestants, but it's been judged ineffective by FDA advisors and is being phased out. The more you understand about what's causing your symptoms, the easier it is to find relief. When in doubt, don't guess. Ask a healthcare provider. They'll help you breathe easier. Literally.

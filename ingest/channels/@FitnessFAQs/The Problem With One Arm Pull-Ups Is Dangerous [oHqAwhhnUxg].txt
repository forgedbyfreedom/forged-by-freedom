My goal is to teach you how to get that one arm pull up or chin up the smart way with longevity in mind. Now if you're dealing with elbow pain from calisthenics that comes on gradually you're most likely dealing with a tendinopathy. Spamming chin ups can cause golfer's elbow. This is the pain on the inside of your elbow. Overdoing the pull ups can cause tennis elbow. This is pain on the lateral side of the elbow. The reason many people get injured when training one arm pull ups is because muscles they recover faster than tendons can adapt. You'll get DOMS in your back and biceps for a couple of days after a workout and you'll feel fresh to train hard once again. But those tendons they need 6 to 12 weeks just to begin adapting to resistance training due to their poor blood supply. Now if you're training for one arm pull ups pay close attention to the following problems. These are the ones that are going to sabotage your journey unless you fix them. Problem 1. Being too weak. If you're jumping into one arm pull up training without adequate strength it's a big no no. I used to think that if you could do 10 pull ups in a row you're probably good to go on those one arm progressions. I've changed my mind about this fact. Because if you don't have a really solid foundation with two arm pull ups, two arm chin ups with added weight, the body's going to break down eventually. I'd recommend simply building up your weighted two arm pull ups to around 50% of your body weight for 3 to 5 reps. Once you have this base level of strength you're going to find progressions like one arm eccentrics or assisted one arm pull ups much easier to recover from and actually progress. Besides, having a strong weighted pull up ensures that your back and biceps are actually strong enough to handle training rather than offloading all of that extra stress where? To the elbows. Problem 2. Poor technique. Using bad form is one of the biggest culprits for pain. This mistake is so common on all types of pull ups. Weighted, archer pull ups, assisted one arms, etc. Almost everyone does this when training low reps. After all, it's the optimal tempo to get best performance. What this looks like is dropping down quickly from the pull up and then rebounding with a bounce from that dead hang. You all know what this looks like. We're using that stretch reflex to slingshot ourselves up getting the chin over the bar. As good as this is for displaying our strength, it can be very taxing on the elbows. Given that plyometric movements place the most load on our tendons, we've got to be careful with how we use them. Instead, a more tendon friendly technique is as follows. Use a controlled negative from the top of the pull up and most importantly, control that final part into the hang, pause, then pull up. I highly recommend this technique for longevity on the one arm journey. Controlled negatives, they're something that I wish I started doing from day one. It's so simple and such an easy fix. Many people, they're not going to listen because it kills the ego. You lift less weight and do fewer reps, but health and longevity are more important than one good workout. Take it from someone who's been doing calisthenics for 16 years. Problem three, frequency. Training too often is a recipe for disaster with one arm pull ups. A frequency of four times per week on one arm pull ups or training every single day is just not sustainable long term. I'd recommend just do two pull workouts per week. One day focused on weighted pulls plus accessories and the other day focused on assisted one arm progressions plus accessories. The body needs a good 48 to 72 hours to recover between these hard elbow sessions. The stronger that you get, the more important restraint becomes because at an advanced level, a single workout can stress your body so much more that rest days become almost as important as training days. Calisthenics isn't about just doing more and more hoping for progress. It's about choosing the correct exercises and routine for your level. Problem number four is not listening to your body and I'm absolutely guilty of doing this myself. I'm very driven in my training. I want to progressively overload every session and beat my previous numbers. The problem is when you get early signs of overuse start showing up and you just push through them anyway, that's no longer discipline. That's a warning sign that you're ignoring. The most important red flags to watch out for when training one arm pull ups include pain and stiffness in the morning, even during basic tasks like opening a jar or turning a door handle. Pain at the start of the workout that improves as you warm up, but it worsens after training. Reduce grip strength is a telltale sign and this is your pain inhibiting you in your workouts. When they show up, the solution isn't just doing more or pushing through pain. It's load management. This means that you're getting that balance of work that you can actually recover and adapt from. Okay, so what's the best thing to do? Auto regulation. Simply listening to your body. Just temporarily lower the number of sets and reduce intensity. Then use symptoms as feedback for what to do next. Completely stopping training is one of the worst things that you can do for tendons. It's a common misbelief that you need just complete rest and it'll get better. You need load management. You need to find that sweet spot of what your body can actually handle, adapt from, and then you try and progress it slowly over time. It's just as much an art as it is science. If you don't measure your training, you can't manage it. This is the most common thing I see calisthenics bro do. They train without a structure and when pain shows up, they have no idea what caused it or how to fix it. That's why a structured program matters so much. By writing down your training, it lets you manage volume, intensity, and frequency and gauge your recovery so you can actually progress in a controlled way. Don't just nod and agree. Keep yourself accountable so that you can be healthy and get stronger. Your body is too precious to leave up to guesswork. Another problem is doing one-arm chin-ups on a bar with that supinated grip. This variation has cooked more elbows than I care to count, myself included. On a straight bar, your hand is locked into one position. This fixes your forearm rotation and it forces your elbow and shoulder to adapt to the bar. Even if that position doesn't suit your anatomy. Under one-arm loads, that restriction massively increases stress on the elbow tendons. To do these pain-free, you need exceptional strength, solid shoulder and forearm mobility, and a lot of patience with progression. Most people are missing at least one of those, often all three. Instead, I would use rings for all of your one-arm training. Rings allow that natural rotation through the wrist, elbow, and shoulder as you pull, which spreads more of the load across more tissue instead of concentrating it in one place, being the elbow. Simply changing the equipment is going to mitigate that injury risk quite a lot, while still building up the strength that you need for one-arm pulling.

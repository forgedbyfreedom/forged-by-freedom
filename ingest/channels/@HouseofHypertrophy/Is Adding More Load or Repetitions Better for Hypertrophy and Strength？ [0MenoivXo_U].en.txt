Progressive overload is necessary for making\h long-term hypertrophy and strength gains.\h\h Simply, it refers to progressing\h your training sessions in some way. Conceptualizing progressive overload as a cycle\h works well. A challenging training session evokes\h\h muscle and strength gains. These gains enable\h you to apply progressive overload, and in turn,\h\h this application of progressive overload\h sustains a challenging training session. Two popular ways to progressive overload are\h with all other training variables constant,\h\h either increasing the load you use or\h the number of repetitions you perform. However, how do these two progressive overload\h\h forms compare to one another for\h long-term hypertrophy and strength? To my knowledge, there hadn't been\h any studies addressing this. But,\h\h a new study by Nobrega et al. changes\h this. Let's thoroughly examine it. The researchers did not\h actually conduct a new study.\h\h Rather, they used data from two of their\h previous studies to create this analysis.\h\h The researchers justified this as the\h two studies were quite comparable. The data taken from study one\h involved 14 previously untrained men,\h\h while the data taken from study two\h involved 10 previously untrained men. In both studies, subjects trained the\h leg extension for 3 sets of repetitions\h\h to failure per session, with 2 minutes\h between sets, for a total of 24 sessions. In study one, which we'll hereafter\h call the subjects of this study the rep\h\h overload group, the total 24 sessions were\h achieved by training two times per week\h\h (on non-consecutive days) for 12 weeks. Furthermore, this rep overload group used an 80%\h one-rep max load on the leg extension in the first\h\h session and did not adjust this initial load at\h all throughout the full study. As subjects were\h\h performing repetitions to failure every\h set, this would have meant this group\h\h was increasing the number of repetitions\h performed per set across their session.\h\h That is, they progressively overload via\h increasing the number of repetitions performed. Now, I did kind of just lie. During the\h 6th week (corresponding to after the 12th\h\h training session), leg extension one-rep max was\h re-tested to return the rep overload subjects to\h\h training with a relative 80% one-rep max load.\h But this was only done once, and subjects kept\h\h this exact new load with no further adjustments\h for the remaining 6 weeks during which they\h\h would have again been simply progressively\h overloading via increasing repetition numbers. In study two, which we'll hereafter call the\h subjects of this study the load overload group,\h\h the total of 24 sessions was achieved\h by training three times per week\h\h (on non-consecutive days) for 8 weeks. Furthermore, this load overload group\h adjusted their load every set and across\h\h sessions to ensure they performed roughly\h 9-12 repetitions to failure every set.\h\h This means they would have predominantly been\h\h progressively overloading via increasing the\h load used to fall within the 9-12 rep range. As you probably noticed, although both groups\h ultimately performed 24 training sessions,\h\h they did not do so identically. Training\h frequency and total study duration differed.\h\h This is a limitation that\h will be returned to later. Nevertheless, before and after\h the 24 training sessions,\h\h one-rep max on the leg extension\h and vastus lateralis cross-sectional\h\h area at approximately 50% of the\h thigh bone length were measured. Additionally, total volume load (calculated\h as the product of sets x repetitions x load)\h\h accumulated by the end of\h as well as volume load progression across\h the 24 training sessions was measured. By the end of the study, total volume\h load, despite being numerically greater\h\h on average for the load overload group, was not\h statistically different between the two groups.\h\h However, progression of volume\h load across the 24 sessions was\h\h statistically greater for the load overload\h group compared to the rep overload group. PAUSE After adjusting the data based on baseline\h values via an analysis of covariance,\h\h increases in relative and absolute\h leg extension one-rep max were not\h\h statistically different between the two groups. Note, relative increases can be\h thought of as percentage changes.\h\h With this data specifically, the rep\h overload group saw an average 31.4%\h\h increase in leg extension one-rep max, while the\h load overload group saw an average 38.3% increase. Absolute increases can be thought\h of as measurement unit changes.\h\h overload group saw an average 14kg leg\h\h extension one-rep max increase, while the load\h overload group saw an average 15.3kg increase. PAUSE Moving forward, also after adjusting for\h baseline values via an analysis of covariance,\h\h vastus lateralis cross-sectional\h\h area was statistically greater for the load\h overload group versus the rep overload group. So overall, this data potentially\h suggests for increasing one-rep max,\h\h there may be no difference between progressively\h overloading via increasing load or repetition\h\h numbers. But for hypertrophy, increasing load\h may be superior to increasing repetition numbers. Quite interesting, when combining both group’s\h data, although the researchers did not find a\h\h significant correlation between the total volume\h load accumulated by the end of the 24 sessions\h\h and one-rep max or vastus\h lateralis cross-sectional gains,\h\h a significant correlation was\h found for volume load progression\h\h across the 24 sessions and gains in one-rep\h max and vastus lateralis cross-sectional area. As the load overload group saw greater\h progressions in volume load across the\h\h 24 sessions, this led the authors\h to suggest this greater volume\h\h load progression may explain the greater\h hypertrophy experienced for this group. Having detailed all of this, there are\h some extremely important limitations\h\h and counterevidence. Let’s explore this\h to derive some more informed conclusions. Firstly, recall this study combined and\h analyzed two independent studies together,\h\h and as such it was not a\h randomized controlled study. Secondly, it’s interesting that both\h groups saw similar one-rep max gains.\h\h Yet, it’s more than likely all subjects\h trained suboptimally for strength.\h\h Both groups trained with loads that\h enabled 8 or more repetitions per set.\h\h Yet, the current evidence indicates training with\h loads that result in you performing 5 or fewer\h\h maximum repetitions is probably more favorable\h for one-rep max gains. For example, Schoenfeld\h\h et al. found with the back squat and bench press,\h adjusting loads every set and across sessions to\h\h maintain 2-4 repetitions to failure produced\h larger bench press and back squat one-rep max\h\h gains versus adjusting loads every set and across\h sessions to maintain 8-12 repetitions to failure. One question arising from this is what if both\h groups simply started with a 2-4 rep max load,\h\h with one group progressively overloading\h via adding reps from hereafter,\h\h and another group simply adjusting loads every set\h and across sessions to maintain 2-4 repetitions,\h\h would both still be similarly\h effective for promoting one-rep\h\h max gains? Hopefully future\h research explores this. Thirdly, we know that although both groups\h ultimately performed 24 training sessions, they\h\h did not do so identically. The rep overload group\h performed 3 leg extension sets 2x per week for 12\h\h weeks, meaning they performed 6 leg extension sets\h per week. The load overload group performed 3 leg\h\h extension sets 3x per week for 8 weeks, meaning\h they performed 9 weekly leg extension sets. However, using other data, it’s actually\h difficult to say this is a true limitation. The evidence indicates when performing 10 or\h fewer weekly sets for a muscle or exercise (as\h\h both groups were doing), training frequency does\h not seem to matter for strength or hypertrophy.\h\h That is, whether you perform these 10 or fewer\h weekly sets for a muscle all in one session,\h\h or across two or more days does not\h really matter. So neither training two\h\h or three times per week are inherently\h superior to each other in this regard. However, the load overload group did\h perform 9 weekly sets on the leg extension,\h\h while the rep overload group only\h performed 6 weekly leg extension sets.\h\h Based on the current data, it’s\h probably likely that on average,\h\h performing 9 weekly sets for an exercise or muscle\h is better than fewer for hypertrophy and strength.\h\h Thus, on a per week basis, the load\h overload group was likely gaining more.\h\h However, remember the load overload group only\h trained for 8 weeks to reach the 24 sessions,\h\h while the rep overload group trained for\h 12 weeks to reach the 24 total sessions,\h\h thus these study's duration differences likely\h cancel out the per week growth differences.\h\h Ultimately probably meaning that use of different\h training frequencies may not be a major problem. Having said all of this, it’s important to\h note muscle and strength gain are not constant.\h\h Generally, each week an individual would\h incrementally experience fewer gains.\h\h This throws another curveball and\h makes the comparisons pretty confusing. Therefore, overall, it’s truly\h difficult to comment on how these\h\h difference in training frequency and\h study durations between the two groups\h\h impacts the results. It would have been much\h better to have controlled these variables. The fourth limitation is that\h the sample sizes were small,\h\h as is most commonly the case\h with resistance training studies.\h\h 14 subjects were in the rep overload group,\h and 10 subjects in the load overload group. Small sample sizes increase the\h chance of errors. Let me explain. Different individuals, despite training\h with exactly the same training variables,\h\h can have very different outcomes. Expanding on this, we know the Nobrega data found\h the load overload group experienced higher vastus\h\h lateralis cross sectional area gains versus the\h rep overload group. There’s quite a bit of data indicating\h loads between 30% and 80% one-rep max,\h\h so long as repetitions are performed very close\h or to failure, produce similar muscle hypertrophy. One of the reasons for this is likely because\h when performing repetitions very close or to\h\h failure within this loading zone, you’re going to\h be evoking quite high mechanical tension levels.\h\h More precisely, muscle fiber recruitment and\h force production by the individual muscle fibers\h\h is going to be quite high (these are two parts of\h mechanical tension). The reason this matters is\h\h because mechanical tension is currently the\h best categorized stimulus for hypertrophy. If you start with a given loading, let’s say 80%\h one-rep max, and progress via increasing your\h\h repetition numbers over sessions, the relative\h load you’re training with will technically\h\h decrease.

CTS might be increasing cancer rates. CTS are an important and frequently used diagnostic tool, but it might be worth considering the consequences of radiation exposure before sending your next patient into the donut of truth. In 2023, there were 93 million CT scans performed on 62 million patients. These scans are projected to account for over 100,000 future cancers. This study, which was published in JAMAMA Internal Medicine, estimated future cancer risk using the National Cancer Institute's radiation risk assessment tool. Lung cancer was the most common type of predicted cancer in adults, followed by colon cancer and leukemia. In children, thyroid, lung, and breast cancer were the most common predicted types. Abdomen and pelvis CTs were estimated to contribute the largest number of projected cancers in adults, accounting for over 40% of predicted cancers. These exams are multi-phase scans, which result in higher radiation exposure. In children, head CTS contributed the largest number, 53% of predicted cancers, even if each CT scan confers only a small cancer risk to the individual. This risk is still applied over millions of scans performed every year, which could still result in a large number of future cancers. CT remains an indispensable tool, and responsible use of CT imaging should involve balancing diagnostic benefit and radiation risk.

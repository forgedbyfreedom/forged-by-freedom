Can drinking coffee or tea lower your risk for dementia or cognitive impairment? A new study in JAMA followed over 130,000 people for up to 43 years. Every 2 to four years, participants would fill out a food frequency questionnaire, noting how often and how much of a particular food they ate. Caffeine intake was calculated by how much coffee, tea, sodas, and chocolate they consumed. The study's primary outcome was dementia. Cognitive decline was a secondary outcome. The study found that higher caffeinated coffee and tea intake was significantly associated with lower dementia risk and modestly better cognitive function. Drinking two to three cups of caffeinated coffee or 1 to two cups of tea per day was associated with the most pronounced differences. There were no additional benefits associated with drinking more than two to three cups per day. As the study notes, this pattern is also consistent with prior studies on coffee consumption and cardiovascular disease risk in which moderate intake was associated with the greatest benefits. However, the authors note that previous studies typically haven't differentiated between caffeinated and decaffeinated beverages. While this study suggests that it is caffeine that specifically may be the primary neuroprotective agent, it's important to keep in mind that this was an observational study, not a randomized clinical trial. So, it doesn't necessarily mean that drinking coffee or tea prevents dementia. And the study had important limitations. For example, relying on self-reported dietary patterns. It also didn't differentiate between types of tea, black, green, or herbal, and coffee characteristics, dark versus light roast, or preparation technique.

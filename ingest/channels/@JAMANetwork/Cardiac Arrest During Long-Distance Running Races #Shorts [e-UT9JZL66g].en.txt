Rate of cardiac arrest during long-distance races has stayed consistent since the year 2000, but the chances of surviving a cardiac event on race day has improved over the last decade. The popularity of long-distance running has continued to grow over the last quarter century with millions participating in marathons and half marathons annually. While long-distance running has its benefits, there is a potential risk of cardiac arrest for those with underlying heart conditions. From 2000 to 2009, among nearly 11 million marathon and half marathon participants in the US, 42 cardiac arrests resulted in death with 17 cardiac arrest survivors. While from 2010 to 2023, among more than 29 million marathon and half marathon participants in the US, 59 cardiac arrests resulted in death with 117 cardiac arrest survivors. So in the earlier period 71% of cardiac arrests resulted in death but more recently 34% of race related cardiac arrests resulted in death. The rate of cardiac arrest didn't change between 2000 and 2023 but the survival rate from cardiac arrests has improved by almost 50% since 2010. It's likely that a major factor in reducing race day mortality is related to better emergency action planning including access to automated external defibrills and CPR trained personnel. 2000 to 2009 data showed that 58% of runners experiencing cardiac arrest during the race received CPR and only 48% received onseen AED use while 2010 to 2023 data show that all runners experiencing cardiac arrest received CPR and there was near universal use of AEDs.

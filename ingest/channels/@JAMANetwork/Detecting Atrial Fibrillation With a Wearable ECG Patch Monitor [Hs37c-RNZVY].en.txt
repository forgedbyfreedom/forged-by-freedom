Atrial fibrillation, a type of disorganized heart rhythm, increases the chances of stroke. Because AFIB can come and go or cause no symptoms, some guidelines recommend screening for it in apparently healthy people. Researchers have now tested that strategy in a randomized trial of a small wireless patch that patients can apply and wear at home. The patch stores heart rhythm information gathered over as many as 14 days, after which patients mail in the patch for analysis. At four months, people randomized to monitoring with the device were diagnosed with AFIB more than unmonitored controls, and were more likely to be prescribed anticoagulants. The effort demonstrates the way new, convenient digital technologies can facilitate detection of dangerous heart rhythms. The actual effect of the device on patient health outcomes like stroke remains to be determined.

>> So today we're going to talk about a study about multiple myeloma that ASCO has chosen to highlight and that you're excited about. So tell us what caught your eye? >> So a theme that's really featured this year at ASCO is prevention. And this study is a great example of a new emphasis and focus on prevention. Smoldering myeloma is a precursor condition before patients get myeloma. It's asymptomatic. People often don't know they have it until they have a blood test. It's usually identified based on abnormalities in the blood. What these authors did is a randomized trial to see if they could prevent the condition known as "smoldering myeloma," if they could prevent it from progressing to full blown myeloma. >> OK. >> That's important because full-blown myeloma is not curable. The current standard of care for smoldering myeloma is observation. They used a drug called Lenalidomide, and they randomized patients to receive the drug or not receive the drug. Patients who received the drug, 91% did not progress to full blown myeloma in a three-year period. >> Wow. >> In the group that did not receive the drug, in fact received placebo, 66% did not progress to myeloma. Now, it's not that simple. The drug is toxic. And a good proportion of patients stopped the drug due to toxicity and side effects. But it's an important new option. It builds on prior work. A study published in 2015 from Spain that showed when you combine Lenalidomide with steroids you could prevent progression, but this study adds, it dropped the steroids making the regimen considerably less toxic. So that's a distinct advantage. Will all patients with smoldering myeloma want to take Lenalidomide? No. They'll look at the side effects. But they have a new option and from what we can tell from the presentation of this randomized trial, the data are very compelling.

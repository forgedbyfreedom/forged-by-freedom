The Autism Spectrum Disorders are remarkably heterogeneous in terms of their clinical presentation as well as the underlying genetics. And we estimate that there are a hundred different types of genetic and genomic variance that can predispose a person to the development of an autism spectrum disorder. And while we don't know all of these, there's been tremendous progress made in identifying them over the last five years because of the introduction of [inaudible] forms of sequencing. Our objective was to look at the diagnostic yield of two relatively new forms of genome-wide testing in a sample of children with Autism Spectrum Disorder. The study sample consisted of 258 unrelated children with Autism Spectrum Disorder from Newfoundland. We recruited them consecutively from the two developmental pediatric clinics here that perform multi-disciplinary assessments for ASD. Each child had a very careful physical examination looking for minor physical anomalies. These are slight morphologic differences that occur in about less than 5% of the population. Many of us have one, two, or possibly even three of these. But when there's more of that number, we start to wonder whether there could be a genetic difference in that person that expressed itself when the person was developing in utero. We classified the children into three groups of increasing morphologic severity. The three groups were essential equivocal and complex. We found that about 20% of our sample fell into the complex group. When both tests were applied, the micro ray and the whole exome sequencing, we found that 16% of the children had an ASD-related molecular finding and that the chance of a positive genetic test was highest in the morphologically most complex group where the diagnostic yield was over 35%. We were really encouraged by the results in the sense that, OK, we found 16% of the children with a positive genetic test and really what we consider to be a very high number in that complex group. Going forward, our hope is to, that eventually we and others will get to the stage where most of the genetic and genomic variation that underlies susceptibility to ASD will be known.

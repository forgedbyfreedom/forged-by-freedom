Pericardiocentesis  is performed when there is concern for pericardial tamponade and is also often pursued when specific etiologies such as bacterial, tuberculous, or malignant pericardial effusions are suspected. The most common anatomical approaches for pericardiocentesis are subxiphoid, apical, and parasternal. Imaging guidance with ultrasound or fluoroscopy should be used whenever available. In an emergency when imaging guidance is not available, a subxiphoid approach is preferred. Outside of this setting, the optimal approach is determined by the location where pericardial fluid is most easily accessible with the lowest risk of damaging other vital structures. This video shows an apical approach to pericardiocentesis. With ultrasound, a pericardial effusion is noted apical and lateral to the left ventricle, with no intervening lung between the skin and pericardial fluid. A wire is inserted through a needle into the pericardial effusion, with a trajectory directed away from the heart as much as possible. The wire can be directly visualized during the pericardial effusion. A catheter is then inserted and left in the pericardial space to ensure complete drainage.

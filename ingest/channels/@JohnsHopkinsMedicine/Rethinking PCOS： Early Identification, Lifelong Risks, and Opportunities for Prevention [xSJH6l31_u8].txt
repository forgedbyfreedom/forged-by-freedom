PCOS is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive age women and affects about 5-10% of women globally. Despite its prevalence, it's frequently underdiagnosed or diagnosed years after symptoms begin. Traditionally, PCOS has been viewed primarily as a fertility disorder. However, we now understand that it's a lifelong metabolic and endocrine condition with implications far beyond reproduction. Many patients begin experiencing symptoms of PCOS during adolescence or early adulthood. These may include irregular or absent periods, acne, excess hair growth, or difficulty maintaining weight. Unfortunately, these symptoms are often attributed to stress, puberty, or lifestyle factors. Because of this, diagnosis may be delayed by several years. The most widely used diagnostic framework is the Rotterdam criteria. Based on the Rotterdam criteria, we diagnose PCOS if someone has two of the three following criteria. With number one being irregular cycles or having ovulatory dysfunction. Number two, if someone's presenting with symptoms of elevated androgens, including facial hair growth or acne, or having elevated androgens on their blood check. Third criteria is having polycystic appearing ovaries on the ultrasound. So, as I mentioned, we diagnose PCOS if someone has two of these three criteria, but that is if we have excluded all other causes, including thyroid disorders, adrenal gland disorders, some pituitary disorders, and also androgen producing cysts of the ovary. It is so important to know that PCOS is a heterogeneous condition, meaning patients may present with different combinations of this symptom. The biology of PCOS involves multiple interacting pathways. insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia play a central role contributing to increased androgen production and disruptions in hypothalamic pituitary ovarian signaling. These pathways reinforce each other and help explain the chronic nature of PCOS. So, PCOS is not just a reproductive disorder. Patients face higher risk of type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, and early onset cardiovascular disease. They also have an increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer of the uterus due to chronic inovulation. Recognizing PCOS early provides an opportunity to address these risks. One of the most important opportunities in PCOS care is prevention. Early diagnosis allows early intervention, which includes lifestyle modification and optimization, such as healthy nutrition, regular physical activity, and weight management. Lifestyle modifications are still the cornerstone of PCOS treatment. Even a small weight loss of approximately 5-10% of body weight can significantly improve metabolic and reproductive outcomes. We have medications and medical therapies that can address both metabolic and reproductive aspects of PCOS, one of which include metformin. Metformin is commonly known as a diabetes medication, and it can improve insulin sensitivity and metabolic parameters and results in regular ovulation in some women. Hormonal therapies, such as the combined oral contraceptives or progesterone-only measures, can be used to regulate menstrual cycles and protect the endometrium. Antiandrogen therapies help treat hirsutism and acne. More recently, the GLP-1 receptor agonists have shown promise in the PCOS population to help with weight management, metabolic optimization, and resuming regular cycles. PCOS also affects mental health. Patients experience higher rates of anxiety, depression, and body image concerns.

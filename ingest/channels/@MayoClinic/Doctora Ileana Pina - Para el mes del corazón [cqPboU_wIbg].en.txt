What I want to convey to the public, as well as to doctors, nurses, and other people who treat heart problems, is that heart problems are the number one cause of death in women. It's not breast cancer, it's not uterine cancer, it's heart disease. And the risk factors we know about in men are exactly the same: high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and lack of activity, which unfortunately is very prevalent among Hispanic women. We want everyone to be aware, and we want women to use their power to go to their doctor and ask, " What is my cholesterol? What is my blood pressure? What is my morning blood sugar?" Knowing your risks, knowing the factors that can lead to heart problems, is crucial. The symptoms in women may be a little different from those in men. If a woman is expecting that severe chest pain that doubles her over, it might not happen. It could be a discomfort, a burning sensation, a pressure. I've had patients who tell me it goes to their back, or it goes to their neck, even their jaw and teeth. They might experience fatigue and tiredness. Fatigue, a lack of motivation, accompanied by symptoms like sweating (especially cold sweats) and a rapid pulse, can all be signs of a serious heart problem. 911, the number we call in emergencies, remains crucial for women. Let's not ignore these symptoms; let's ask questions and take action ourselves. It's vital for healthcare providers to understand the risk factors in their patients and address them as aggressively as they do with men. For example, we know that Black African American and Hispanic women are more likely to be overweight, while those of Mexican descent have more diabetes. These two groups, as I mentioned before, are less likely to exercise, and lack of physical activity is a significant risk factor. Maintaining a healthy weight throughout life and staying active are essential.

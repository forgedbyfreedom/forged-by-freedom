Catnapper, take note. Your break should be quick. A short nap strikes that balance between allowing you to be refreshed without giving you that hangover effect from the sleep. Dr. Eric Olsen is a sleep specialist at Mayo Clinic. He says longer naps take you into deeper stages of sleep and with that then it's harder to get going when you awaken. Doctors call it sleep inertia. Keeping your nap under 30 minutes can help to avoid it. Dr. Olsen says the need for a longer nap may be a sign of nighttime sleep trouble. Your schedule, diet, or bedtime routine could all be causes. Or is there suspicion of a sleep disorder that's interfering with what seems like should be an adequate amount of time in bed? A sleep study can identify a disorder and lead to treatment. And sleep specialists can suggest improvements to bedtime habits. Getting help with your sleep can make napping simply refreshing instead of required. For the Mayo Clinic News Network, I'm Jeff Olsson.

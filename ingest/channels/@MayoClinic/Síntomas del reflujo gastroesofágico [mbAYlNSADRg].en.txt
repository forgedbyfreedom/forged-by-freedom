The symptoms of gastroesophageal reflux are characterized by two cardinal or fundamental symptoms: food reflux and the patient's perception of heartburn (or acid reflux). The medical term is pyrosis, which refers to the sensation of acid flowing back into the stomach. These are the most common symptoms typical of the condition. These symptoms usually respond to or improve with the use of medications such as antacids, particularly when the patient has had the condition for a long time. Sometimes complications can arise. For example, if the patient begins to have difficulty swallowing food, we are concerned that a complication such as esophageal stenosis or narrowing has occurred, and this is an important symptom for us to investigate. I would also like to mention that in patients who have had reflux for a long time (more than 5 years), we are also concerned about the possibility of a lesion called Barrett's esophagus. Barrett's esophagus is a precancerous lesion that is important to detect precisely because it may have an increased risk of developing esophageal cancer. The typical symptoms of gastroesophageal reflux are twofold: the sensation of heartburn or acid reflux in the retrosternal area, that is, in this part of the sternum in the central chest, which feels like a burning sensation. We commonly call this heartburn or acid reflux, and the backflow of liquid from the stomach to the mouth. These are the fundamental symptoms of reflux. When a patient has these symptoms for a prolonged period of time (5 years), or the symptoms are frequent, or they affect their lifestyle, we consider it important not only to treat them but also to evaluate them. What we aim to ensure is that patients who have reflux for a sustained period of time, for example, more than 5 years, undergo evaluation and screening tests to look for a precancerous condition that occurs with this reflux, called Barrett's esophagus. Barrett's esophagus is simply a complication of chronic reflux in which the epithelium, that is, the typical normal cells of the esophagus, transform.  Cells with a characteristic of premalignant type cells are important to detect due to their practical importance in relation to esophageal cancer. The patient at highest risk of complications from reflux is a subject who has had the disease for more than 5 years. Specifically, in this type of patient, we want to look for the possibility of Barre's esophagus. But the alarming reflux symptoms that make us worry and seek to investigate the patient more closely are, firstly, those patients who are not responding to the usual treatment, and secondly, those patients who have new symptoms such as the onset of difficulty passing food, swallowing, or swallowing. We see many patients; personally, I have a particular interest in the area of ​​pain and reflux. I see many patients who present to the cardiologist because they have pain whose central characteristics in the chest resemble pain originating in the heart, or angina pectoris, or even a myocardial infarction. Once these patients are objectively studied with careful cardiological tests, we find that a significant percentage, almost on the order of Thirty to forty percent of patients with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) are not actually heart disease and have GERD as the source of their pain. This is important because the management of these patients is radically different from managing those with heart or coronary artery disease. However, I want to emphasize that before we, as gastroenterologists, see these patients, we must ensure that their heart is indeed functioning properly. Clinically, it is very difficult to distinguish between pain originating from the heart and pain originating from the esophagus. Esophageal pain does not cause death, while cardiac pain can. Therefore, it is essential that these patients be carefully evaluated to rule out heart disease. If they do not have heart disease through objective testing, then we can certainly provide significant assistance in their effective treatment management.

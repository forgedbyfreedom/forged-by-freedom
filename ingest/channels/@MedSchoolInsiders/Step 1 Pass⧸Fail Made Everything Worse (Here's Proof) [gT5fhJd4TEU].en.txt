For decades, step one was the single most dreaded exam in all of medical education. You thought the MCAT was brutal. Step one made it look like a warm-up. For many students, preparation started nearly a year in advance. Months of steady buildup that eventually culminate in 2 to 3 months of intense full-time studying. 12 hours a day, 6 days a week. Your future compressed into a three-digit number. The toll was enormous. Students mental health deteriorated. Burnout was rampant. and the calls for change grew louder and louder. Then in 2020, the announcement came. Step one would go past fail. The announcement sparked an immediate and heated debate. Online medical forums erupted. Twitter threads multiplied. Students, residents, and physicians argued that making step one pass fail would finally address burnout, improve mental health, and fix a broken system. Deans, and institutions echoed similar hopes. students exhaled for the first time in years. Except there were a few voices, ours included, saying, "Hold on. You're confusing the symptom with the cause. Step one wasn't the disease. It was a thermometer measuring a much deeper problem. And removing the thermometer doesn't cure the fever." 3 years later, the data are in, and it tells a very different story than the one everyone expected. So, was turning step one pass fail a mistake? Dr. Jubal, medschoolinsiders.com. To understand how this went wrong, you need to understand what step one actually did. Before it went past fail in 2022, step one was a single highstakes exam taken between second and third year. It tested foundational medical knowledge, but more importantly, it functioned as a sorting tool. A higher step one score shaped which specialties you could realistically pursue, which programs would consider your application, and how early you could gauge your own competitiveness. For better or worse, you knew where you stood. Residency programs face overwhelming application volume. They need fast standardized filters just to function. Step one filled that role for decades. When critics argued that too much weight was being placed on a single test, the logic behind pass fail seemed straightforward. Remove numeric scores and reduce pressure. But as we argued when the pass fail change was announced, the pressure wouldn't disappear. It would shift to other signals. The intention behind pass fail was to reduce stress. But the debate missed a bigger question. What happens to the rest of the system when you remove one of its primary filters? You can actually check out those videos and articles from back in 2020 linked in the description. Making step one pass fail didn't change the stakes or how competitive programs are. It threw away your backup score. Mess up step one in the past, you could make real improvement on step 2 CK. Mess up step 2 CK now, that's your only shot. So, what actually happened? Pass rates dropped by about 5 to seven percentage points with a larger decline among DO students. Before pass fail, US MD students had pass rates around 95 to 97%. By 2023 and 2024, that dropped to roughly 89 to 91%. That means about 1 in 10 MD students now fail step 1 compared to roughly 1 in 20 before. For DO students, the decline was steeper. Pass rates fell from the low 90s into the mid to high 80s, a drop of about 6 to 8 percentage points. What changed? Lower stakes change behavior. When an exam feels less impactful, preparation becomes more variable. Some students still overprepare, others pull back, and that variability increases the risk of failure at the margins. Removing the incentive to excel also remove the buffer against failing. And there's another factor most students don't consider. On the exact same day step one went pass fail, the USMLE raised the minimum passing score from 194 to 196. So students were preparing less intensely for an exam that had actually become harder to pass. Less preparation, higher bar, same day. The result was predictable. But what's worse is that the stress didn't disappear. Recent studies of students approaching step one in the past fail era still show high levels of anxiety and poor sleep. Even without a numeric score, step one remains a major psychological stressor. So, the experience didn't become calm or low pressure. It just changed shape. There were two major pressure shifts. The first is the most obvious. Step 2 CK has become the primary numeric test score differentiator in residency selection. Scores didn't suddenly rise faster after 2022. They were already trending upward. What changed is how heavily programs depended on them. The timing of step two didn't change either. Step 2 CK is still taken after your third-year clinical rotations, usually with a short dedicated study period. But that means much of your studying is overlapping with long work hours, evaluations, and residency applications. That concentrates more pressure into a single exam during one of the busiest periods of medical school. When step one became pass fail, many students reallocated their time. Some pulled back on step one prep and spent more time on research and extracurriculars. For some students, that shift meant less consistent step one preparation, increasing the risk of failing. Meanwhile, residency programs didn't stop needing filters. They still have to sift through thousands of applications. Removing one numeric metric didn't eliminate the need for sorting. It meant programs leaned more heavily on the remaining ones. Now there's more pressure on a single exam with no backup test to offset a weak step to performance. This translates into fewer opportunities to recover and more downstream consequences from a single bad outcome. Knowing what matters isn't the same as knowing how to prepare for it. Many students hit a plateau, let's say 230, 235, 245, grinding through more and more practice questions and content review, hoping their score will eventually climb. It won't, not without changing the approach. When step one was still numerically scored, most students didn't spend months studying for step 2 CK. Dedicated periods were often only a few weeks long. So what was the next major pressure shift? Research didn't suddenly matter after step one went past fail. It was already increasing, but the slope changed dramatically. When step one scores were removed, residency programs didn't suddenly become holistic. They still needed ways to filter thousands of applications. They had fewer early objective signals. So research output became one of the remaining measurable differentiators. As a result, incentives shifted. Research became more heavily weighted and the system began rewarding the countable output of research items including abstracts, presentations, and publications over depth alone. These specialties were already in a different tier. But after 2022, the gap widened fast. If you're aiming for one of these fields, the research expectation isn't optional. It's table stakes. Now, some of you might be thinking, didn't RAS just change how research is reported? Starting with the 2027 cycle, RAS is replacing the traditional publication section with a new scholarly work section. It introduces clearer categories, lets applicants designate select items as most meaningful, and is designed to shift emphasis from sheer volume to substance and depth of contribution. A lot of students are hearing this and assuming research item counts are going to drop. I don't think that's what's going to happen. By the time I graduated from medical school, I had more than 60 research items, including 29 publications. Even with the new RAS structure, if you're doing research properly and strategically, your numbers shouldn't actually decrease. There are ways to maintain and even increase your output while also meeting the new emphasis on meaningful contributions. The students who understand how the system works will adapt. The ones who don't will fall behind. Because here's what hasn't changed. Residency programs aren't reading every paper. They use their own algorithms to assign each candidate a research score. They're using research items as proxies for academic engagement and productivity. Authorship position, publication type, journal relevance, and impact factor and other variables are all factored in. It's a fast way to sort large applicant pools, especially now that step one is past fail and more weight has shifted to step 2 CK and research. The new format may change how that information is presented, but it doesn't change the underlying incentive. The original promise was reduce stress, but burnout and anxiety persist because burnout was never primarily about step one. What actually changed was how students are evaluated, not the competitiveness of residency. There's less clarity on where students stand, fewer objective benchmarks, and less time to adjust once signals appear. The real issue is a structural bottleneck. And this gets to the core issue. Competitive things are competitive. And if you change something to pass fail, that doesn't make the end goal of residency any less competitive. It just changes the metrics by which you play the game. Residency didn't become less competitive when step one went past fail. The competition shifted to different signals. ones that show up later carry higher stakes and are harder to recover from. When we talk about databacked competitiveness, the most competitive specialties consistently cluster at the top. Dermatology, plastic surgery, orthopedic surgery, neurosurgery, and ENT stand clearly above the rest in terms of applicant pressure, score expectations, and research volume. Other specialties like radiology, radiation oncology, and anesthesiology are competitive, but they're not in the same tier as those top five. when you look at objective match data. Meanwhile, specialties like family medicine and pediatrics generally face less intense screening pressure because their supply demand dynamics differ fundamentally. This distinction matters because competition in medicine isn't abstract. It's driven by incentives. Where demand far exceeds supply, programs will always rely on hard filters. Regardless of whether step one is scored or pass fail, programs must filter. application volume makes a detailed review impossible. And when one signal is removed, others take its place. A lackluster step 2 CK score is now much harder to recover from. Research output carries far more weight and programs lean more heavily on clerkship grades, letters of recommendation, and institutional familiarity to sort applicants at scale. With all that said, what should students do now? Take step one seriously, even though it's past fail. It's all about finding the right balance. Investing too much energy into step one puts you at a disadvantage because while you're hyperfocused on step one, other students are gaining research experience and publications. Aim for a score that puts you well beyond the danger zone. But don't overprep at the expense of the rest of your application. Step 1's change to pass fail didn't ease stress. It changed how students are filtered. Early clarity was replaced with later, higher stake signals. The pressure didn't disappear, it concentrated. Treat step 2 CK as the primary test score in residency screening. Be intentional with research and start early. Focus on experiences that actually mean something, not just checking boxes.

 Imagine the heartache of a spouse who shares decades of memories with their partner, now a stranger in moments of forgetfulness, or the distress of a child, explaining to their parent who they are. Picture the emotional turmoil of a family as they adjust to the new reality of providing care, while mourning the loss of the person they once knew. Dementia is a very challenging illness. It not only affects a person who develops it, but also has a very huge emotional toll on their loved ones, leading to grief, frustration, and helplessness over family and friends. According to the World Health Organization, more than 55 million people worldwide live with dementia, and more than 10 million new cases are diagnosed every year. And while Alzheimer's disease is the most common reason of dementia, it's not the only cause. The purpose of this video is to educate you about what dementia really is, and what are the early signs of dementia that usually go unnoticed. Understanding these early indicators is the first step towards early detection, which can significantly influence the course of action of their illness. Now let's start and talk about five of these early signs of dementia. 1. Certain changes in short-term memory Now having a bit of trouble with memory is very common and happens with everyone, but when it becomes frequent and repetitive, it can be a very important early sign of dementia. The changes are often subtle and tend to involve the short-term memory. You may be able to remember events that took place years ago, but might find it difficult to remember what you ate for breakfast. A person with dementia may also display other changes in their short-term memory, such as misplacing objects and being unable to recall where they kept them, or having a hard time remembering why they walked into a particular room, and not being able to keep track of what tasks or plans they had set for the day. Now some of these things happen to everyone now and then. However, as mentioned previously, when these incidents start to occur regularly in everyday life, it's a signal to become alert and perhaps seek further advice. 2. Difficulty in communication Another early symptom of dementia is difficulty with communicating thoughts. You may have a hard time explaining something or finding the right words to express yourself. You might end up using a related word or describing the object instead of remembering the exact term for an object. For example, you might say, the thing you sit on instead of a chair. You may also stop in the middle of a sentence and not know how to continue. You might start off with a clear direction, but suddenly lose your train of thought unable to recall what you were discussing. This situation can lead to frustration and sadness. You might feel embarrassed or distressed about your difficulties, potentially leading to social withdrawal. Having a conversation with a person who has dementia can be very challenging, and it may take longer than usual for them to express their thoughts or feelings. 3. Disorientation in time and place It's one thing to briefly forget what day it is or why you entered a room. It's entirely another to lose a sense of time more deeply or to feel lost in places that should feel like home. You're disorientation with time can become severe, affecting your grasp on the passage of time. You might not only forget the day, but could also be confused about the season or even the year. This level of confusion can disrupt your daily routine and make planning or remembering future events a struggle. For you, time might seem to meld together, blurring past, present and future, and complicating everyday life. People living with dementia can become lost on their own street, not knowing how they got there or how to get home. You may head out to the grocery store, a friend's house or even just to the mailbox, and suddenly find yourself unsure of where you are or how to get back home. This loss of spatial awareness is not only frightening for you, but also for your loved ones. It signifies a move from normal age-related memory laps to more serious cognitive impairment. This disorientation occurs because dementia impacts a brain's ability to process and recall information accurately. Parts of your brain responsible for memory, spatial orientation, and understanding time become impaired. As these areas deteriorate, so does your ability to interpret and navigate the world as you once did. Difficulty in performing familiar tasks Difficulty in performing familiar tasks is a significant early sign of dementia. It's more than just forgetting a minor detail here and there, it's when everyday activities become confusing challenges. For instance, you might forget the steps to prepare your favorite dish or the correct order to wear your clothes. Preparing a meal, for example, involves not only just the art of cooking, but also planning what to make, remembering recipes, and managing the time of different components. When dementia enters the picture, these once automatic processes can become confusing mazes. Person might forget critical steps in the recipe, struggle to use kitchen appliances correctly, or be unable to recall why they started cooking in the first place. The inability to carry out familiar tasks can lead to feelings of frustration, sadness, and loss of independence. This change can also affect self-esteem and contribute to social withdrawal, as individuals might become embarrassed about their difficulties and opt out of activities they fear they no longer can manage. Apathy Apathy describes an absence of feeling or emotion. This indifference can affect your motivation and leave you feeling detached from the world. You might stop caring about everyday tasks, hobbies, or personal interests, struggle to show interest and enthusiasm in your personal relationships, feel indifferent to spending time with others, or experience very little emotion or changes in mood. Most people experience apathetic feelings from time to time, especially during times of stress. But persistent apathy can affect your relationships, everyday life, and overall well-being. This change can strain relationships, as friends and family might feel rejected or misunderstood, not realizing that this detachment is part of the disease. You may also notice a stark decrease in the expression of emotions. The highs and lows that characterize a typical emotional spectrum might flatten out. This doesn't mean someone with dementia no longer feels emotions, but the expression of those emotions can become muted. Recognizing apathy as a symptom of dementia is crucial because it can significantly affect quality of life. It can lead to increased isolation and accelerate cognitive decline due to reduced mental engagement. If you have noticed any of these early signs of dementia, please consult with the healthcare professional. Early detection makes a huge difference, and there is support available to help manage this condition.

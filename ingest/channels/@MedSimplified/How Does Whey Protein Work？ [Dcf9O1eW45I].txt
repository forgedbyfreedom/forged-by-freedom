It takes 200 liters of milk to make just 1 kg of whey protein. That's a lot of milk. Now milk is made of two main types of proteins. Casein, which doesn't dissolve very well in water. And whey, which dissolves in water easily. When milk is processed to make cheese, it undergoes separation into two components, curds and liquid. The liquid leftover from this process contains whey protein. This liquid gold, once processed further, is transformed into the whey protein powder we all know and love. The production of whey protein can be divided into five main phases. Pasteurization, separation, filtration, drying and final processing. The journey of making whey protein begins with pasteurization of milk. Pasteurization is a process by which all the bacteria and other germs in the milk are killed. Milk is first heated to over 100 degrees Celsius and then rapidly cooled to 4 degrees. Like I told you earlier, milk contains two main types of proteins. Casein and whey. Usually they are in a ratio of 80 to 20. Meaning 80% casein and 20% whey. During the separation phase, the aim is to separate casein from whey. To achieve this, an enzyme known as chymosin is added to the milk. The casein proteins in the milk are present in the form of micelles. Which look something like this. Chymosin breaks the chemical bonds in these micelles which destabilizes its structure. This causes clumping of these micelles which aggregate together and start to settle down at the bottom. The liquid part that is left on the top is the one we are mainly concerned with and it is none other than the whey protein. This liquid is separated and sent to the next stage of production which is phase of filtration. Basically, the liquid whey that we just separated also contains other components like carbohydrates, fats and additional solids which need to be filtered out so as to get pure whey protein. Now there are two main mechanisms that are used for filtration. Membrane filtration and ion exchange. Let's simplify these. Membrane filtration process uses special membranes with small pores to separate different components in whey based upon their size. Depending upon the pore size of the membranes, we can have microfiltration, ultrafiltration and nanofiltration. When liquid whey is passed through the microfiltration membranes, most fat and lactose from the liquid are removed and the resulting product is the base of whey protein concentrate. The process of ultrafiltration is done if whey protein isolate has to be produced. This removes all the lactose and makes the protein content of the whey highly concentrated. Nanofiltration reduces the mineral content of the whey, especially sodium and potassium chlorides. The filtration phase is crucial for obtaining high-quality whey products with desired characteristics. It removes impurities, concentrates valuable components like proteins and achieves the desired composition of whey. The other method used for filtration is the ion exchange method, in which the pH levels of the liquid whey are first changed by adding a chemical agent. And then different components of the whey are separated depending upon their surface charge, rather than their size. First, chemical agents such as hydrochloric acid or sodium hydroxide are added, which adjusts the pH and the electrical charge on the proteins, making them negatively or positively charged. The liquid whey is then passed through a tube-like structure, which contains an anionic membrane and a cationic membrane. The anion exchange membranes are positively charged, so all the negatively charged proteins in the whey like beta-lactoglobulin, alpha-lactalbumin, and bovine serum albumin get attached to the anionic membrane. Similarly, the opposite membrane is known as the cationic membrane, which is negatively charged and attracts lactoferrin and lactoperoxidase, since they are positively charged proteins. If you don't understand all these names, don't worry yet, we will discuss about these sub-fractions of whey later in this video. So, when the liquid whey is passed through this tube containing these ion exchangers, the protein sticks to these membranes, but the carbs and fats pass through. What is left in the end is a product which is very high in protein with little to no fats or carbs. The ion exchange process is relatively inexpensive as compared to membrane filtration, but it has its drawbacks. After filtration comes the next phase of drying. The high protein liquid whey is put into an air dryer, and under the influence of hot and cold air, the water is rapidly removed. After this, we get the raw whey protein powder which moves to the next step of final processing where flavoring agents and other nutrients are added. So, this was how whey protein is made.

It is\h the same in Latin, with some extra details.\h In Latin, adjectives are formed in a way that\h is very similar to nouns. Just like nouns,\h\h they will show a specific case, a number and\h gender, although they don’t actually have a\h\h specific one of their own. They simply change\h their endings in order to AGREE with the word that\h\h they modify in case, gender and number. In other\h words, if there is only one apple, the adjective\h\h will be declined into singular. If there are many\h apples, the adjective will change to plural. If\h\h the apple is a subject, as in “The apple is red”,\h and therefore found in the nominative case, so\h\h will the adjective. If the noun is a direct object\h and thus accusative, as in “I eat the red apple”,\h\h the adjective will need to agree with that\h instead. Of course let’s not forget that all\h\h nouns in Latin have gender, while the adjective\h will only have it when it agrees with a noun.\h In addition we should say that adjectives\h in Latin may be used in three ways:\h 1. as an attributive adjective. This means\h the adjective is placed right next to the\h\h noun adding some kind of information to the\h noun it modifies. I love beautiful flowers.\h 2. as a predicative adjective. This happens\h when the noun and the adjective are separated\h\h by the verb “to be”. Flowers are beautiful. 3. as a substantive adjective.

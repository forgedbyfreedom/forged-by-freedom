Now that we’ve discussed the stresses that affect\h Earth’s rocks, let’s go into detail about the\h\h different layers of Earth. On the surface, Earth\h may seem like a relatively stable place, with the\h\h occasional earthquake or volcanic eruption, but\h our experiences are limited to the uppermost part\h\h of Earth, the crust. Earth’s crust comes in two\h varieties: continental and oceanic. Continental\h\h crust is felsic, or rich in silica, and is around\h 20 to 70 kilometers thick. Oceanic crust is mafic,\h\h or rich in iron and magnesium, and is around 5 to\h 10 kilometers thick. However, the most important\h\h difference between oceanic and continental crust\h is their density. Oceanic crust is more dense\h\h than continental crust, therefore when a piece\h of oceanic crust collides with continental crust,\h\h the oceanic crust slides beneath the continental\h crust in a process that we’ve mentioned,\h\h called subduction. The subducted oceanic crust\h will then sink deeper and deeper into the mantle\h\h until eventually reaching the bottom of\h the mantle. Oceanic crust is constantly\h\h being subducted and therefore destroyed. There\h are large areas of ancient, 4-billion-year-old\h\h continental crust called cratons, but the oldest\h oceanic crust is only 340 million years old.\h Below the crust is the mantle, a solid, but\h malleable layer with temperatures reaching\h\h 3500 degrees Celsius and pressures up to a million\h times greater than atmospheric pressure. Although\h\h the mantle is solid, over millions of years,\h it convects like the world’s largest lava lamp,\h\h as earth’s primordial and radiogenic heat is\h transferred to the surface and radiated to space.\h\h Mid-ocean ridges and rift zones\h form beneath large areas of hot,\h\h upwelling mantle, while subduction zones\h form beneath areas of cold, sinking mantle.\h\h When subducting oceanic crust sinks into\h the mantle, it creates a suction-like force\h\h in its wake, which pulls the subducting slab\h further into the mantle. This slab-pull force\h\h is driven by buoyancy, due to oceanic crust\h becoming more dense with age. Eventually it\h\h becomes more dense than the mantle beneath it,\h which is when subduction can begin. According\h\h to the current scientific consensus, slab pull\h is the main force that causes plate tectonics.\h The mantle extends from 70 to 2900 kilometers\h below the surface and is mainly composed of an\h\h ultramafic rock called peridotite, which is mainly\h composed of the mineral olivine, or MgFeSiO4.\h\h Geologists have found xenoliths, or strange rocks,\h of peridotite in massive volcanic eruptions.\h\h By analyzing the minerals in peridotite,\h scientists can determine what depth the peridotite\h\h came from. Diamonds, for example, are formed\h at depths of about 150 kilometers or deeper,\h\h and are primarily found in another\h ultramafic xenolith called kimberlite.\h At the bottom of the mantle lies a very\h mysterious, but geologically important\h\h layer called D’’. This layer is marked by a large\h thermal gradient and an increased heterogeneity.\h\h The exact nature of this layer is unknown, but\h there are some hypotheses. Some believe that\h\h the D’’ layer is the “slab graveyard”, or the\h place where subducted slabs eventually settle.\h\h Some believe that mantle plumes, or cylindrical\h areas of hot, rising mantle, initiate from within\h\h D’’ in areas where there is increased heat flux\h from the core. Mantle plumes have caused some\h\h of the largest volcanoes in Earth’s History,\h with modern examples being located in Hawaii,\h\h Iceland, and Yellowstone. The accumulation of\h subducted slabs at D’’ also has implications\h\h for triggering magnetic field reversals; but\h before we get there we must discuss the core.\h At 2900 kilometers, the D’’ layer gives way to\h a liquid outer core composed mainly of iron and\h\h nickel. Here, temperatures reach 5500 degrees\h Celsius, and pressures exceed 300 million times\h\h the atmospheric pressure on the surface. Since\h this layer is a liquid, it can easily convect,\h\h carrying heat from the inner core into the\h mantle through the D’’ layer. The rotation\h\h of Earth on its axis further modifies the\h flow and creates large, cylindrical helices.\h\h Since iron is an electrically conductive material,\h when it swirls through Earth’s magnetic field it\h\h creates an electric field, which in turn creates\h an additional magnetic field, strengthening the\h\h original field. This is called the geodynamo,\h and it is how earth maintains its magnetic field.\h\h The geodynamo does require a “seed” magnetic\h field to get going, which was likely provided\h\h during the T-Tauri stage of the early sun. At 5100 kilometers, the pressure becomes so great\h\h that iron is forced into the solid phase. This\h boundary is called the Bullen discontinuity,\h\h and it marks the division between the liquid\h outer core and the dense, solid inner core. The\h\h inner core is about the size of the moon and has a\h temperature equivalent to the surface of the sun.\h\h Though the inner core does not generate a magnetic\h field, it does help lock-in the field lines and\h\h stabilize the magnetic field generated in\h the outer core. The inner core also rotates\h\h faster than Earth as a whole. This phenomenon is\h called super-rotation. It is thought to be caused\h\h by rotating magnetic fields in the outer core\h exerting a magnetic torque on the inner core,\h\h similar to how an induction motor works. Magnetic\h fields induce electric fields in conductors,\h\h and the created electric field manifests an\h additional magnetic field that interacts with\h\h the original magnetic field. In an electric\h motor, the stator windings produce a rotating\h\h magnetic field, and the rotating magnetic\h field induces an electric current in the rotor,\h\h which induces a secondary magnetic field that\h interacts with the original rotating field causing\h\h the rotor to spin, as it is “dragged” along\h by the rotating magnetic field of the stator.\h\h Earth’s magnetic field undergoes periodic\h reversals where the field strength drops to\h\h near zero, and the poles switch.

The third of the periods into which Egyptologists\h have divided the long course of ancient Egyptian\h\h history is the New Kingdom, which is famous\h for the rule of kings like Tutankhamun and\h\h Ramses. This is due to its possible connections\h with the story of Exodus told in the Bible;\h\h for its architectural and artistic achievements;\h and for the vast extent of its territory, which\h\h has led scholars to call it the “Egyptian Empire”. The New Kingdom began with the Eighteenth Dynasty,\h\h which was founded around 1540 BCE by Ahmose I, who\h was a relative of the last king of the previous\h\h dynasty. Following up on his predecessor’s\h expulsion of the Hyksos from the northeast,\h\h he conquered part of southern Canaan itself. He\h also acquired territory in Nubia to the south,\h\h which he assigned to a new official,\h called the “king’s son of Kush”,\h\h who was second in power only to the vizier. One of the features of this era was the growing\h\h power of the cult of the god Amun. Though this had\h begun with the Middle Kingdom ruler Mentuhotep II,\h\h who ruled from the god’s city of Thebes,\h it became even more important during the\h\h New Kingdom. Spoils taken from military\h campaigns were dedicated to its temples,\h\h and new ones were built in the neighborhood\h of Thebes. This concentrated a great deal of\h\h the country’s wealth in the hands of Amun’s\h priests; and with one important exception,\h\h which we’ll return to in a moment, the kings\h paid great respect to this cult. In fact,\h\h the title “God’s wife of Amun” was applied\h to the sisters of kings, whom they had begun\h\h to take as their wives, or to their mothers. Around 1500 BCE, Ahmose’s son Amenhotep, which\h\h means “Amun is satisfied”, extended the southern\h border even farther into the territory of the\h\h Kingdom of Kush, which had attacked southern Egypt\h in the past; and he may have had some encounters\h\h with Mitanni in Syria. Amenhotep was followed in\h 1493 by one of his generals, who took the name\h\h Thutmose I. The Kushites immediately rebelled,\h and in response Thutmose invaded, killed the king,\h\h and made Kush a permanent part of his empire.\h He also campaigned against the cities of Syria,\h\h and reached the city of Carchemish across\h the Euphrates River in Mesopotamia.\h When Thutmose died around 1480, he was\h briefly succeeded by his son, Thutmose II;\h\h and when he died, leaving his own son who was too\h young to rule, the state was governed in his place\h\h by Thutmose II’s widow, Hatshepsut. Not long\h after that, a coalition of Egypt’s vassal cities\h\h in Syria and Palestine, led by the city of Kadesh,\h fought a battle with Thutmose’s forces at the\h\h town of Megiddo in northern Palestine, which is\h described in detail in a hieroglyphic inscription\h\h in the temple of Amun in Thebes. The coalition was\h defeated, and its rulers were replaced with chiefs\h\h chosen by Thutmose. They were also required to\h send their sons to be educated in the Egyptian\h\h court, and to pay an annual tribute; and military\h garrisons were placed in the region to ensure\h\h their compliance.

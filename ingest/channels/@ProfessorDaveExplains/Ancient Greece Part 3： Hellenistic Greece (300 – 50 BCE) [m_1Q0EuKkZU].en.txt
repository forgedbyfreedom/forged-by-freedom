We’ve been learning about the history of Ancient\h Greece, and the final era of this time period is\h\h called Hellenistic Greece. “Hellenistic” is a\h modern term that was coined to describe a set\h\h of connected political, cultural, and social\h changes in the Mediterranean world which took\h\h place after the end of the classical period,\h and lasted for several hundred years. This\h\h transition from classical to Hellenistic began\h with the reign of the Macedonian King Philip II,\h\h who defeated a Greek alliance led by Thebes\h and Sparta in 338 BCE. From that time on, the\h\h rulers of Macedonia were involved in the affairs\h of the Greek mainland. Philip formed many of its\h\h independent city-states into an alliance called\h the League of Corinth, and his son, Alexander the\h\h Great, led them in a series of military campaigns\h in the East, to conquer the Persian Empire. In the\h\h wake of these campaigns, a new civilization arose\h that spanned the Mediterranean from Asia Minor\h\h to Afghanistan, becoming a fusion of Greek and\h non-Greek cultures; and common forms of economic,\h\h social, and political life emerged in what would\h now be called the oikumenē, the inhabited world.\h Philip had been planning an expedition in order to\h free the Greek cities of Ionia from Persian rule\h\h when he was assassinated in 336 BCE. Alexander\h then took up his plans, and in 334 BCE he crossed\h\h the Hellespont, the strait that separated\h Europe from Asia, with about 50,000 soldiers.\h\h Over the next eight years, his army campaigned\h across the Persian provinces of Asia Minor,\h\h Egypt, Palestine and Syria, Mesopotamia, and\h Central Asia. It then pushed even farther east,\h\h reaching the northwestern part of India.\h By the time of his death in 323 BCE,\h\h Alexander was starting to reorganize all of\h these territories as part of his own empire,\h\h though he left in control many of the same\h officials, called satraps, who had governed them\h\h on behalf of the Persian king. Though his ultimate\h intentions are not clear, and many theories about\h\h them were proposed by later historians,\h he may have been trying to create a joint\h\h Greek-Persian empire with its capital at Babylon.\h In fact, the educated elites of the Roman Empire,\h\h who lived in a fused Greco-Roman civilization,\h came to see Alexander as a visionary.\h Because Alexander did not leave behind a\h clear successor, his own generals fought\h\h among themselves over sections of his empire, and\h one of them, Antigonus the One-Eyed, attempted to\h\h conquer all of it and declare himself king. But\h he was defeated at the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BCE,\h\h and the others reached a settlement which divided\h the empire into three large kingdoms. There was\h\h Macedonia itself, along with a large part of\h Greece, which was ruled by Antigonus’ descendants;\h\h Egypt, which was ruled by Ptolemy; and a\h kingdom consisting of Asia Minor, Syria,\h\h and Mesopotamia, which was ruled by Seleucus.\h Though we tend to think of the Greeks as living\h\h in proudly independent city-states, these men\h chose to become kings because, like Alexander,\h\h they were from Macedonia to the north of the Greek\h mainland. Though the Macedonians also spoke Greek,\h\h they were a kingdom ruled by a hereditary dynasty;\h so when Alexander’s successors came to power,\h\h they based their own regimes on this model, and\h expected both Greeks and non-Greeks to regard them\h\h as their rightful rulers, and even as divine. But\h because they did not have the royal lineage that\h\h Alexander did, they felt the need to justify their\h authority by using propaganda, like the images on\h\h the coins they minted, to portray themselves as\h the men whom Alexander had entrusted to take his\h\h place. In fact, Ptolemy did them one better by\h hijacking Alexander’s corpse and placing it in\h\h a huge mausoleum in the center of his capital\h of Alexandria. The successors also tried to\h\h portray themselves, as Alexander had, as the\h favorites of the particular gods they worshiped.\h In addition, these successors built new cities\h across the region that became centers of Greek\h\h life in places where it had not existed before;\h and they named them after Alexander or after\h\h themselves. People, goods, and ideas\h could move freely between these cities;\h\h so even though the oikumenē was politically\h divided, it amounted in other respects to\h\h a single Hellenistic society. The capital of\h the Ptolemaic kingdom was Alexandria in Egypt,\h\h which was built on the Mediterranean coast and\h was therefore much more closely connected with\h\h the rest of the Greek world than it was with\h Egypt itself. It was the home of the very first\h\h library in the sense of a systematic and organized\h collection of books, and a center for scholarship\h\h called the Museum or “temple of the Muses”, where\h scholars like Eratosthenes and Aristarchus studied\h\h astronomy and geography, and made the first forays\h into literary and textual criticism. Another major\h\h center was Pergamon in Asia Minor, which was\h actually an independent city-state that became\h\h a hub of literature and art. Just as Alexandria\h took advantage of the fact that papyrus, one\h\h of the most important writing materials of the\h ancient Mediterranean, was made from the reeds\h\h that grew along the Nile, Pergamon, which was\h in the mountains where livestock was pastured,\h\h used parchment made from animal skins. Alexandria was essentially a Greek city\h\h in every way, and Cleopatra VII, who was the last\h Ptolemaic monarch, was said to have been the first\h\h to bother to learn Egyptian. But at the same time,\h the Ptolemaic and Seleucid kingdoms adapted their\h\h policies to suit the diverse reality in which they\h now found themselves. The Ptolemies, for example,\h\h created an elaborate bureaucracy like that of the\h native kings of Egypt, who had exercised tight\h\h control over the country’s people and its limited\h natural resources. They adopted the practice of\h\h brother-sister marriage that had been used by the\h Pharaohs to emphasize their divinity, and were\h\h represented as the Pharaohs had been in Egyptian\h art and architecture. They also established\h\h a cult of the god Serapis, which combined the\h anthropomorphic imagery familiar to Greeks with\h\h aspects of the Egyptian gods Osiris and Apis.\h Their administrative decrees were published\h\h in three scripts: Greek, hieroglyphics, which\h was not a very practical system of writing, but\h\h had always been used for official purposes, and\h Demotic, which was the script that was developed\h\h to write Egyptian for everyday purposes. This is\h why scholars in the 19th century were able to use\h\h the Rosetta Stone, which recorded one of these\h decrees, to decipher the hieroglyphic script.\h The Seleucids, on the other hand, associated\h themselves with the Greek cults of Apollo and\h\h Zeus, while maintaining relationships with the\h powerful priesthoods of Babylonian temples,\h\h which were essentially small states of their\h own. These temples were allowed to continue to\h\h manage their affairs, and to collect revenues for\h their own use, though a king might occasionally\h\h confiscate treasure from a temple if he needed the\h funds. They also took part in Babylonian religious\h\h festivals like the Akitu which was celebrated\h every spring. The 3rd century BCE king Antiochus I\h\h rebuilt the temple of the god Nabu in the city of\h Borsippa, and this deed was recorded in cuneiform\h\h script on a clay cylinder just as Assyrian\h and Babylonian kings had done for thousands of\h\h years. Another cuneiform tablet records Antiochus’\h worship of the Babylonian moon-god Sin. And just\h\h as the Ptolemies used Egyptian, the Seleucid\h administration continued to use Aramaic, the\h\h common language of the Persian Empire, alongside\h Greek. Just as in Egypt, the cities founded by\h\h the Seleucids, such as Antioch in Syria, which\h was one of the largest in the Hellenistic world,\h\h remained Greek in both theory and practice, while\h many of the older cities of Mesopotamia and Persia\h\h continued on as they had before, though some of\h them, including Jerusalem, were made into poleis\h\h or city-states. The relatively laissez-faire\h policy of the Seleucids was itself inspired\h\h by the Persian style of administration, which had\h been a matter of necessity, since Persia - unlike\h\h Ptolemaic Egypt - was a patchwork of many cultures\h and beliefs that would not easily be forced to\h\h conform. Besides, Hellenism as an idea seems\h largely to have been an accidental byproduct\h\h of the new world that had been opened up to\h Greeks, rather than a creed that wanted to\h\h change the hearts and minds of the non-Greeks\h who lived in that world. On the other hand,\h\h we don’t have that many examples of what it was\h like to be a non-Greek in the Seleucid Empire;\h\h and the one example we do have demonstrates\h anything but a live-and-let-live attitude.\h This example was the Maccabean revolt of 167\h BCE, when a family led by Judah Maccabee rebelled\h\h against the Seleucid authorities of Palestine,\h eventually establishing a Jewish state that lasted\h\h for more than a hundred years. We know a lot about\h this revolt from the Jewish literature of the\h\h time, especially the Books of Maccabees, which are\h included in some Christian versions of the Bible;\h\h and these books portray it as a reaction to King\h Antiochus IV, who wanted to ban Jewish practices\h\h and beliefs and replace them with pagan Greek ones\h like sacrificing pigs and setting up a statue of\h\h Zeus. But these books were written after the\h rebellion by members of the Hasmonean court,\h\h and were clearly meant to paint it as a\h culture war between righteous Jews on one side,\h\h and immoral Greeks and their Jewish supporters\h on the other. The picture is also complicated\h\h by the fact that the Jews seem to have lived\h peacefully enough under the Ptolemies who ruled\h\h their country before the Seleucids conquered\h it in 200 BCE; and the Hasmoneans themselves\h\h went on to behave like Hellenistic\h kings just like other local rulers did.\h In any case, Antiochus’ actions don’t seem to\h have had many parallels among other Seleucid\h\h or Ptolemaic kings, who did not generally try to\h force their beliefs on their non-Greek subjects;\h\h and though Hellenism as a phenomenon is often\h described as the interaction between Greek\h\h and non-Greek cultures, it tended to go in\h one direction only. It was Egyptians, Jews,\h\h Phoenicians, and Babylonians who learned the\h Greeks’ language and took part in their cultural\h\h life, rather than the other way around.\h But this did not mean that they wanted to\h\h discard their own ethnic backgrounds. The Jewish\h philosopher Philo of Alexandria, for example,\h\h wrote in Greek and applied Greek ideas to his\h interpretation of Biblical stories and characters;\h\h but he did this in order to show the\h philosophical and ethical value of those\h\h texts. But the Greeks\h\h of the mainland did experience changes in their\h political life, as their independent city-states\h\h came into conflict with the Macedonian monarchy\h that was constantly trying to control them,\h\h whether directly or indirectly, even as it claimed\h to respect their freedom. The\h most important of these were the Achaean League,\h\h which included the cities of the Peloponnese,\h and the Aetolian League in central Greece. By the middle of\h the 2nd century BCE, both Macedonia and the Greek\h\h mainland had been reduced to Roman provinces. The military history of the Seleucid kingdom\h\h was even more chaotic, as by the middle of\h the 3rd century, many of its territories in\h\h Persia and Central Asia had broken away and\h become independent states. Around 200 BCE it\h\h was able to take from Egypt the territories\h of Palestine and Syria; but then in the 180s,\h\h it lost a lot of its holdings in Asia Minor to the\h states of Pergamon and Rhodes in a war they fought\h\h with Rome. From that point on its kings tended\h to fail in their military ambitions, and in the\h\h 160s BCE they lost control over Palestine when it\h became an independent Jewish state. By the end of\h\h the second century the Parthian Empire had taken\h most of the territory of the Seleucid Empire,\h\h which had been reduced to a small state in Syria,\h which was made into a Roman province in 63 BCE.\h The Ptolemaic kingdom of Egypt, on the other\h hand, remained generally undisturbed until\h\h the end of the first century BCE. The Ptolemies\h were not interested in acquiring the territories\h\h of the other kingdoms, and instead built\h up Alexandria as an international center\h\h of commerce and culture. They also became a\h valuable ally of the Roman Republic as the\h\h main grain supplier for the city of Rome, which\h was growing so large that it could not depend on\h\h its own farmland to supply its needs. But Egypt\h finally became a province in 31 BCE when Octavian\h\h defeated Marc Antony and his ally Cleopatra\h in the civil wars that ended the Republic.\h Throughout the Hellenistic period and into\h the Roman Empire, places like Alexandria,\h\h Pergamon, and the island of Rhodes produced\h art and literature that is now described as\h\h quintessentially Hellenistic. When it comes to\h literature, this can mean two very different\h\h things, a combination of Greek forms and ideas\h with the values and traditions of other cultures;\h\h or a group of genres and ideas that, while Greek\h in itself, was not typical of the classical\h\h period. Hellenistic poets like Callimachus,\h and playwrights like Menander, were much more\h\h concerned than classical writers had been with\h individuals, their desires, and their anxieties;\h\h and instead of assuming that their readers were\h the members of small communities that gave them\h\h their identity and made clear what it expected of\h them, they saw them as people who were were trying\h\h to find happiness in a suddenly much larger and\h confusing world where change was the order of the\h\h day.

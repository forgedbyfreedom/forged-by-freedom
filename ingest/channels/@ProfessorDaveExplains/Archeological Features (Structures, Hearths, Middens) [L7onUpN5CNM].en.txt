We just spent a few tutorials examining\h artifacts, so let’s move on to features.\h\h A feature in archeology refers to immovable\h objects, such as the remains of houses, walls,\h\h or pyramids. Knowing how to identify a feature\h in an excavation is critical in interpreting and\h\h recording a site. While some features are made of\h stone, many others are made of organic artifacts,\h\h such as a house made of wooden posts in the\h ground, or a trash pile. While the organic\h\h material has long rotted away, it often leaves\h discolored spots in the earth. A discoloration\h\h in the soil in an archeological context is\h called a stain. The exact color of the stain\h\h is very important in differentiating it from the\h surrounding soil and identifying its cause. How\h\h can archeologists communicate exact coloration?\h “Brown” has many different shades, and photo color\h\h looks different depending on the lighting\h and camera. Archeologists use a Munsell to\h\h quantitatively describe soil color. Think of the\h paint strips you see at hardware stores. Munsells\h\h are books of certain paint strips with each\h color having a specific notation. That way, all\h\h archeologists agree on what a specific color is.\h Munsells are used to describe stains, but also the\h\h color of stratigraphic layers to quantify layer\h changes rather than leaving it to interpretation.\h The first feature type we will look at is a\h structure. A structure is simply the remains of a\h\h building. This ranges from shades with four stakes\h in the ground to entire palaces. Many structures\h\h have stone foundations, which help preserve their\h footprint. Linear raised portions on foundations\h\h would usually be identified as walls. Mapping\h these walls lets archeologists create maps of\h\h large structures and entire towns Structures with\h stone foundations but wooden or organic walls are\h\h also common. The walls can usually still be\h identified and mapped from linear stains on\h\h the stone foundation. Keep in mind, structures\h are commonly built on top of other structures,\h\h especially on sites with long occupations.\h Structures without stone foundations are\h\h trickier to find but still possible. Wooden posts\h can usually be identified by their staining in\h\h specific patterns. However, if the site was\h occupied for a long time and several wooden\h\h structures are built in the same place, the\h footprint of the structures can get muddled. To\h\h help with this, we can look for areas swept clean\h of refuse. Human activities make a lot of trash,\h\h but it is usually swept from the floor of houses.\h No one wants to step on lithic flakes or ceramic\h\h sherds. Therefore, we can look for areas swept\h clean and bounded by postholes to define wooden\h\h structures. Mounds and pyramids are another\h type of structure. While pyramids are usually\h\h defined as raised stacks of stone, and mounds\h as raised piles of earth, most don’t fit neatly\h\h into these categories. For example, Mayan pyramids\h are piles of rock and earth capped with a layer of\h\h rock, and a building usually sat atop it. Each\h region in archeology defines pyramids and mounds\h\h differently. Mounds and pyramids are the easiest\h way to elevate a structure above other ones, which\h\h is why they’re so common in the ancient world.\h However, this elevation is steeped in ritual,\h\h political, and social meanings that varied\h wildly from people to people. For example,\h\h Egyptians built pyramids for dead pharaohs,\h pyramids in Mayan society served as temples,\h\h and in the Mississippian civilization\h of North America they were used as the\h\h residences of elite members of society. Fire is a universal tool used by humanity.\h\h We commonly find remains of fires in residential\h homes. Places where humans lit fires are called\h\h hearths in archeology. Hearth features are common\h in residential structures; used for light, heat,\h\h and cooking. Hearths can be indoor or outdoor\h features. Hearths are usually identified from\h\h dark grey or black stains containing charcoal.\h Hearths can give us lots of information about\h\h the people who used them. As discussed earlier,\h charcoal is easy to radiocarbon date. We can\h\h also identify the species of wood the charcoal\h is, letting us know what trees in the area they\h\h were collecting for firewood. This can help in the\h reconstruction of past environments. Burned food\h\h is also commonly found in residential hearths.\h Archeologists can scoop the ash and charcoal and\h\h place it in a flotation machine, which causes\h burnt organic material like bone and grain\h\h kernels to float to the surface. We can even\h get carbonized pollen from flotation machines,\h\h further helping in environmental reconstruction. The last feature we will discuss are middens.\h\h Middens are areas where refuse was dumped.\h Middens can be a single type of artifact,\h\h such as how a large pile of lithic refuse would\h be a lithic midden, or they can contain a variety\h\h of artifacts. Middens can get so large that\h they form artificial platforms and mounds,\h\h and are incorporated into the architecture of\h the site. For example, on flat plains, sites can\h\h sometimes be identified from “village middens”,\h the refuse that slowly built up over centuries\h\h under a town. In North America, shell middens\h were used like mounds, having buildings atop them.\h\h Middens are often targeted by archeologists\h due to the wealth of artifacts within them.\h\h Many other features exist such as plazas,\h roads, defensive walls, and many more,\h\h all of which are important in interpreting a\h site.

As we discussed in the previous tutorial,\h Aristotle cemented the foundation which would\h\h establish logic as its own proper discipline for\h millennia to come. Many elements can be identified\h\h in other regions around his time, but this was\h the first time an explicit, comprehensive, and\h\h dedicated theory was established. Logic itself,\h though, is not a science, but rather a tool to\h\h be used in science and other areas of inquiry. It\h represents the strongest form of argumentation,\h\h since a valid conclusion needs to be, beyond any\h doubt, a necessary consequence of its premises.\h\h And to further avoid any traps language may offer,\h Aristotle proposed that placeholder symbols such\h\h as a single letter should substitute proper words\h in the analysis – what we know today as variables,\h\h a concept only introduced to mathematics\h almost 2000 years later in the 16th century.\h Let’s return to our original syllogism. As we\h saw, it contains two kinds of sentences: premises,\h\h which were the first two lines, and a conclusion,\h which is inferred from the premises. Each of these\h\h sentences also has three different elements akin\h to grammatical elements: the subject, which is the\h\h main term being defined; the predicate, which says\h something about the subject; and the operators,\h\h which quantify and qualify the others. The\h repeated term which connects both premises,\h\h allowing for the conclusion, is called a middle\h term. Aristotle names ten kinds of predicates\h\h in his Categories, which in Greek also literally\h meant “predicates”. Those are substance, quantity,\h\h quality, relation, location, time,\h position, possession, doing, and undergoing.\h\h Regarding their relationships with the subject, or\h “predicables” he named four: of definition, genus,\h\h unique property, or accidental property. And as\h we can see with the Socrates example, subjects\h\h and predicates can be a combination of several\h different things, so we can substitute some of\h\h these words for the aforementioned variables as\h follows: “Every ‘A’ is ‘B’/’C’ is ‘A’/Therefore,\h\h C is B.” This is a valid argument, and a valid\h inference, since it’s simply formulaic in that\h\h if both premises are correct, the conclusion\h will also necessarily be correct. This doesn’t\h\h mean, of course, that it will be true. This is an important distinction. Validity\h\h is a property of the format of the argument, while\h truth is a property of every individual sentence.\h\h For example, consider this argument. “Every dog\h is a bird/Dave is a dog/Therefore Dave is a bird”.\h\h Everything here is false, but it’s still a valid\h argument, since it follows a proper structure,\h\h and the conclusion does follow the premises,\h even though they are false premises.\h\h Or consider this. “Every cat is a mammal/My dog\h is a mammal/Therefore, all dogs are mammals”.\h\h Each of these statements are true individually,\h however, the conclusion does not follow from its\h\h premises, and thus it’s an invalid argument.\h Therefore, an argument is true if and only if\h\h every sentence composing it is true and they’re\h valid, in that the argument: 1) has at least two\h\h premises and a conclusion, 2) the conclusion is\h a necessary inference from the premises, and 3)\h\h the conclusion is different from the premises. We can see in all of these examples two kinds of\h\h sentences, depending on their operators. There are\h universal ones such as “Every YouTuber is mortal”,\h\h which refer to every member of a given category\h being part of some other category, and particular\h\h ones such as “Dave is a YouTuber”, which refers\h to a single or limited number of members belonging\h\h to a given category. And both these kinds of\h sentences can be either positive, in that they’re\h\h affirming something, or negative, meaning they\h are denying it. “No YouTuber is immortal”, for\h\h example, would be a universal negative. This leads\h us to the following board, where we have all four\h\h kinds of sentences in Aristotelian logic. We can\h categorize each of these sentences by a letter:\h\h “A” for universal affirmatives, “E” for universal\h negatives, “I” for particular affirmatives and\h\h “O” for particular negatives. These sentences\h have clear relationships between themselves,\h\h For example A and O would be clear\h contradictories, as in “Every YouTuber\h\h is mortal/Professor Dave is immortal”, much like\h E and I. Additionally, I and O are subalterns of\h\h A and E, respectively, since they only represent\h a part of their generalized versions. Finally,\h\h A and E are contraries, since they exclude each\h other, as all members of a class either are or\h\h are not something. For example “YouTubers” cannot\h be mortal and immortal at the same time. This goes\h\h back to another concept in his Metaphysics, that a\h thing cannot be and also not be at the same time:\h\h the principle of non-contradiction. I and O, on\h the other hand, are subcontraries, as they can\h\h coexist. Aristotle also used name mnemonics to\h refer to certain kinds of syllogisms. If one is\h\h composed of three universally positive sentences,\h for example “All YouTubers are mortal/All humans\h\h are mortal/Thus, all YouTubers are human”,\h we could call them an “AAA” sentence, and\h\h its mnemonic would be Barbara, with each of its\h vowels representing one sentence of the syllogism.\h\h Other examples would be “Darii” for AII, which\h describes our original example, and “Baroco”\h\h for AOO, as in “All YouTubers are mortal/This\h rock is not mortal/This rock is not a YouTuber”.\h Another interesting pattern arises as we\h analyze Aristotle’s understanding of the\h\h proper ordering of sentences: the premises\h must have a certain direction in order for\h\h a conclusion to be their result, a\h direction from the more “general”\h\h premise to a “less” or at least equally general\h conclusion. This is what we call a deduction.\h\h A deduction is the opposite of an induction,\h where a more general conclusion is reached\h\h by more particular premises. Aristotle only\h briefly referred to induction when speaking\h\h of its importance in cognitive processes\h regarding scientific knowledge. However,\h\h as he was focused on classifying argumentation\h itself in every field of knowledge when writing\h\h on logic, it is understandable that he focused\h on the more objective and certain deduction.\h\h Inductive reasoning depended on more practical\h and methodological observation and categorization,\h\h and thus wasn’t susceptible to a “pure\h form analysis” like deductive reasoning\h\h is. Nevertheless, we will see later in the series\h that inductive reasoning still plays a big role\h\h when we think about the scientific method. Finally, Aristotle was also the first to\h\h veer into what would be known today as “modal\h logic”. “Modal” because it analyzed the degree\h\h of possibility of a sentence, instead of it being\h simply “assertoric”, or affirming nothing but its\h\h truth or falsity, as with our previous examples.\h For example, “Dave is a YouTuber” is simply\h\h asserting some information. However, if someone\h doesn’t know me or what I do for a living, they\h\h could say “Dave is probably a YouTuber” or “Maybe\h Dave is a YouTuber”. These would be “contingent”,\h\h or “accidental”, “possible” and not “necessary”,\h and would have different relationships with their\h\h assertoric counterparts. But Aristotle’s\h formulations were problematic and largely\h\h abandoned soon afterwards, so we’ll not go farther\h into his perspective.

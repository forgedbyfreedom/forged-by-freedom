What did they\h\h eat? What did they do at home? Material culture\h is found as much in our garbage as it is in our\h\h temples and tombs. Analyzing artifacts is critical\h\h to creating typologies and a variety of\h other research methods that archeologists\h\h utilize. Let's take a look at how to identify and\h analyze some of the most common artifact types.\h Stone tools are some of the most common artifact\h types since they preserve well. In archeology,\h\h stone tools and artifacts made when\h creating them are called lithics,\h\h coming from the Greek word for stone. While stone\h tools have been used throughout human history,\h\h we will mainly be looking at them in a prehistoric\h context. Lithic studies is an entire subfield of\h\h archeology, so we’ll just go over the basics\h here. One of the most prominent types of\h\h lithic artifact is a point. While commonly\h called “arrowheads” by the general public,\h\h this term isn’t used in archeology. This is\h because it implies something about the tool,\h\h the idea that it was the head of an arrow.\h However, many of the points called arrowheads\h\h are spear or dart points. Archeologists simply\h refer to lithic artifacts that come to a point\h\h as “points”. Points that would have been placed\h on a shaft of wood and shot or thrown are called\h\h projectile points. The process of creating a point\h is called “knapping”. Not just any rock can be\h\h knapped, actually the vast majority of rock types\h don’t meet important criteria. Most importantly,\h\h a rock must be able to easily break in\h predictable ways when a force is applied.\h\h The rock also must be hard enough to withstand\h being used as a point, instead of breaking before\h\h it's able to lodge itself into a target. By\h far the most common rocks used in knapping\h\h contain microcrystalline quartz, namely chert\h and flint. Another rock used is obsidian.\h To knap, a knapper will strike a large piece of\h flint with a percussive tool, historically made\h\h from antler. This large, unworked piece of flint\h is called a core. A single core often produces\h\h several points. Pieces are broken from this\h core, which are called flakes. These flakes\h\h taken from the core, called primary flakes, are\h further worked. Flakes are broken off of primary\h\h flakes until the general shape of the point is\h reached. At this stage, the point is often worked\h\h with a sharp tool to serrate and thin the edge.\h The making of a single point will often produce\h\h more lithic refuse than the point itself. While\h points can be easy enough to identify as a point,\h\h let's learn to identify lithic refuse, as large\h quantities of lithic refuse are one of the best\h\h ways to diagnose prehistoric settlements. Flint\h and other knappable stones crack and break into\h\h smaller pieces through a variety of natural\h phenomena, so how could we differentiate it\h\h from this? Flakes have several characteristics\h because of how they were broken which lets us\h\h identify them. At the top of the flake will be a\h flattened area. This is the “striking platform”,\h\h where the knapper applied the percussive force\h to break the flake off the point. One side of\h\h the flake will be mostly smooth, where the\h flake was against the point or core. On this\h\h smooth side directly below the platform is a\h bump called the bulb of percussion. This is\h\h created by the force of the striking platform\h being hit. Several other factors exist to help\h\h determine if a piece of flint is an artifact,\h but these are the ones that we will discuss.\h Now that we know how to identify flakes and\h points, let's learn some basic analyses of\h\h them. First, as with all artifacts, knowing where\h in the world the lithic artifact was found is the\h\h single most important datum. Points can be broken\h down into two broad categories, spear points and\h\h projectile points. Projectile points need to\h be attached to a shaft and must be aerodynamic\h\h enough to fly through the air. Projectile points\h can be further broken down into two categories,\h\h dart points and arrow points. Dart points were\h attached to throwing spears, or “darts”. These\h\h were either thrown just as a spear or with the\h assistance of a tool called the atlatl. The atlatl\h\h was an ice age invention that let people throw\h darts much harder and farther. Arrow points are\h\h projectile points that were attached to an arrow\h and shot from a bow. The main way to distinguish\h\h between these two is size. Lithic arrow points\h are usually no larger than three centimeters,\h\h as larger points would weigh down an arrow. Projectile points can be further described\h\h based on two characteristics: notch and stem.\h The notch refers to large grooves near the base\h\h of a projectile point where it was tied to the\h shaft. Where this notch is on the point can be\h\h described as well, dividing them into groups like\h side notched, corner notched, and basal notched.\h\h Stemmed points can be identified by a skinny\h extension of material on the base of the point.\h\h A stem can be described as being contracting,\h straight, or expanding. It’s also possible that\h\h a point has neither a notch nor a stem, and this\h is an important descriptive characteristic for\h\h projectile points. Let’s analyze a real point.\h Suppose the point was found in Tennesee, USA,\h\h and measures five centimeters in length. Notice\h the cut areas near the bottom. This makes the\h\h point notched, rather than stemmed. The notches\h aren’t in the base or corner of the point, so\h\h it’s a side-notched point. We now know it was created\h\h around 5,000-10,000 BP and is part of a knapping\h tradition that spans several states in the eastern\h\h United States. These identification\h and analysis methods have given us a good\h\h idea of how to spot a lithic point and the\h basics of descriptive analysis. However,\h\h many other lithics exist besides points.

In the previous tutorial we examined lithic\h artifacts. Another type of artifact widely used\h\h in the past that preserves very well is ceramic\h material. Often called pottery in vernacular\h\h terms, archeologists refer to any hardened clay\h artifact as a ceramic. The addition of other\h\h materials to the clay makes ceramics vary quite a\h lot. They range from china plates to Greek vases\h\h to neolithic bowls. It’s very rare for an entire\h ceramic piece to be found whole in the ground,\h\h it is much more likely to find small fragments\h of a larger piece. A ceramic fragment is called a\h\h sherd. Ceramics can also be put into typologies\h to better date and identify them. To do this,\h\h we need to be able to describe a ceramic. Paste refers to the clay and additives used to\h\h make the ceramic. Other materials must be added\h to raw clay to help it hold together, otherwise\h\h it wouldn’t survive the firing process. Materials\h added to a paste are called temper. Common tempers\h\h include shell, sand, bone, and many others.\h Identifying a ceramic temper can be difficult,\h\h and sometimes requires a microscope to inspect the\h ceramic. Often, a culture will have a signature\h\h paste with consistent levels of temper across all\h their ceramics that can be used to identify them.\h\h Another attribute of ceramics is treatment. This\h refers to how it was fired and any glazes used\h\h to finish the ceramic. Usually, a higher heat\h treatment will result in a smoother and glossier\h\h outside. Different pastes also change color based\h on the temperature that was used in firing the\h\h piece. Finally, a piece can be described by its\h decoration. This includes coloring, patterning,\h\h writing, and artwork. Different cultures around\h the world have very distinct art styles, which\h\h manifests in their ceramics. With these three\h attributes, pottery can be distinguished from one\h\h another to be put into typologies, which we can\h attribute to certain cultures and time periods.\h As new technologies were discovered around the\h globe, some cultures began moving away from lithic\h\h and ceramic use, replacing both with metal.\h All cultures still used lithics and ceramics,\h\h but metal became more and more important to these\h groups. While historians have long used “ages”,\h\h such as Copper Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age,\h to describe the appearance of metalworking\h\h and their dates, this is only applicable to\h Europe and surrounding areas. For example,\h\h while Europe was transitioning from the Copper Age\h to the Bronze Age, Africa was beginning its Iron\h\h Age. Many other societies practiced metallurgy,\h but didn’t rely on it for tools. For example,\h\h the Aztec Empire made many bronze\h ceremonial artifacts but preferred\h\h using obsidian and ceramics for everyday use.\h Metal artifacts are also grouped into typologies,\h\h from bronze swords to silver coins. One of\h the most important characteristics of metal\h\h artifacts is identifying the type of metal used\h in its creation. Metallography can reveal the\h\h exact proportions of different elements used\h in an alloy. Like ceramics, many cultures had\h\h signature blends we can look for to attribute the\h artifact. Shape, color, and decoration are other\h\h describable characteristics of metal artifacts. Lithics, ceramics, and metals are the most common\h\h artifact types in the archeological record.\h However, many others exist and are important\h\h in archeological interpretations. Bones, both\h animal and human, are critical to archeology.\h\h We will discuss human burials in greater\h detail a bit later. With animal bones and\h\h charred plant remains, we can get a view of what\h people were eating at a site. Another important\h\h artifact is fabric and textiles.

Those are Ustilaginomycotina,\h Pucciniomycotina, and Agaricomycotina.\h\h Let’s first examine fungi within the\h subphylum, Ustilaginomycotina. Generally,\h\h fungi within this subphylum are known\h as smuts, or fungi that cause diseases\h\h in plants. These smuts fall into the class\h Ustilaginomycetes, then the order Ustilaginales.\h\h These fungi mainly infect plants in the\h Poaceae family, also known as the grass family.\h\h This means that some smuts can adversely affect\h economically important cash crops such as barley,\h\h wheat, sugar cane, or potatoes. Smuts attack\h plant fruits and seeds causing them to swell\h\h into structures called galls. These galls\h are full of spores and burst open when fully\h\h matured. Although many smuts cause issues for\h us, the corn smut, Ustilago maydis, is an edible\h\h smut that infects corn. It causes the kernels\h of corn to swell into galls. In Mexico, these\h\h galls from Ustilago maydis are used for fillings\h in tacos, quesadillas, and several other dishes.\h Corn smut has an exciting life cycle consisting\h of two phases: a single-celled haploid yeast phase\h\h and a dikaryotic mycelia phase. As we recall from\h our study of biology, haploid means the cells each\h\h have one nucleus and one set of chromosomes.\h Dikaryotic means there are two nuclei in each\h\h cell, one from each of the parent fungi, a\h feature that is unique to the fungi kingdom.\h\h We must not confuse dikaryotic with diploid.\h In diploid organisms the nuclei from parents\h\h fuse into one, whereas dikaryotic fungi have\h two separate nuclei that do not fuse into one.\h\h Starting at the yeast phase, the cells have two\h genetic alleles that determine if another yeast\h\h cell is a compatible mate, the “a” locus and\h “b” locus. If the two yeasts have different\h\h alleles at the “a” locus, conjugation tubes\h form on the cells and grow toward each other,\h\h fusing at the conjugation tube tips. This\h connection will allow the cells’ respective nuclei\h\h to be transported from one to the other. However,\h this is conditional upon the “b” locus. If the “b”\h\h locus differs from one cell to the other, the\h conjugation tubes will transport the nuclei and\h\h form a dikaryotic cell, which can grow into a\h filamentous dikaryotic mycelium. This dikaryotic\h\h mycelium will insert itself into the plant\h tissue and form galls that release the spores.\h Originally, species of fungi within the\h subphylum Ustilaginomycotina were generally\h\h known to be plant pathogens called smuts\h and fall into the class Ustilaginomycetes.\h\h Ustilago maydis is considered a Ustilaginomycete.\h Recently, there have been other unique divisions\h\h of the fungal lineage that fall into this\h subphylum. Most species within\h\h this subphylum are plant pathogens in the form\h of rusts, but many species act as parasites that\h\h act on mushrooms, also called mycoparasites.\h We’ll discuss the rust fungi, which generally\h\h constitute the class Pucciniomycetes\h and fall into the order Pucciniales.\h\h Rusts get their name from the powdery red\h fruiting bodies that form on parts of plants.\h\h Wherever there are plants, there are also rusts\h which can infect a diverse number of plants\h\h from a wide range of ecosystems. Many rust fungi\h have coevolved with their host plants, which has\h\h led the order to become quite diverse, with about\h 7,000 described species. A notable rust fungus is\h\h Gymnosporangium virginiana, or Cedar Apple rust,\h which infects both Juniper trees and Apple trees\h\h to complete its life cycle. When a fungus requires\h two host organisms to complete its life cycle,\h\h it is considered heteroecious. Rust fungi are\h known for having a heteroecious life cycle.\h\h Fungi that need only one organism to complete\h their life cycle are considered autoecious.\h\h Rust fungi can have quite an interesting\h life cycle with up to five stages and five\h\h different types of spores. A rust is considered\h macrocyclic if it produces all spore types.\h\h These are urediniospores, teliospores,\h basidiospores, pycniospores, and aeciospores.\h Let’s describe the life cycle and spore types\h of the well studied Puccinia graminis, or stem\h\h rust. Puccinia graminis is heteroecious and has\h a primary host where asexual reproduction occurs\h\h and an alternate host where sexual reproduction\h occurs. Its primary host is wheat and its\h\h alternate host is the barberry plant. Barberry is\h considered the alternate host because the rust can\h\h reproduce with or without its presence. We’ll\h begin the life cycle during the summer months\h\h by focusing on the stem of its primary host,\h wheat. Hyphae have penetrated the wheat stem, and\h\h at the places where the red rust emerges, there\h are structures called uredinium that are filled\h\h with urediniospores. The urediniospores allow\h for the rust to asexually reproduce and infect\h\h other plants. As the temperature gets colder,\h and the fungus recognizes that winter is coming,\h\h the fungus forms a telium instead of a uredinium.\h The telium houses the production of teliospores,\h\h which are black, thick-walled, and diploid. These\h teliospores are quite durable and are stable until\h\h spring, when they germinate and transition from a\h diploid spore to a haploid spore through meiosis.\h\h These new haploid spores are known as\h basidiospores, and they allow the fungus\h\h to infect its alternate host, barberry leaves.\h The basidiospores germinate inside the leaves and\h\h produce a haploid mycelium. From this mycelium,\h structures called pycnia produce receptive\h\h mycelium and haploid pycniospores, which are found\h in a sticky substance that attracts insects. The\h\h insects will transport pycniospores from one\h pycnia to another, allowing sexual reproduction.\h\h Once a pycniospore fuses with receptive hyphae,\h new hyphae produce a mycelium having both nuclei\h\h instead of just one. This new, double nucleus\h mycelium, known as a dikaryotic mycelium,\h\h grows through the barberry leaf and produces a\h structure called an aecium which houses dikaryotic\h\h aeciospores. These spores can only infect the\h primary host, wheat. Aeciospores are separated\h\h from urediniospores because the spore shape\h and production process is quite different.\h\h Once aeciospores reach the wheat plant, they\h penetrate and begin producing a mycelium.\h\h Soon, that mycelium will produce dikaryotic\h urediniospores, and the cycle starts over again.\h So that covers two subphyla of the\h phylum basidiomycota.

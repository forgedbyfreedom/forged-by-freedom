We’ve examined a handful of biotechnology concepts\h in previous tutorials, but now it’s time to\h\h introduce what is undoubtedly the most promising\h technique in biotechnology of the past decade. The\h\h CRISPR-Cas9 system represents genome editing\h technology that has revolutionized molecular\h\h biology, due to its precise and site-specific gene\h editing capabilities, which essentially allow for\h\h an unprecedented level of control in manipulating\h the genetic information of a living organism.\h\h How does this work mechanistically, and what are\h its applications? Let’s get a closer look now,\h\h starting with some historical context. In 1987, Atsuo Nakata and his team of\h\h researchers from the Osaka University in\h Japan first reported the presence of Clustered\h\h Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats,\h abbreviated as CRISPR, in the Escherichia coli\h\h genome. These refer to short, repeated sequences\h of DNA nucleotides found within the genome of\h\h prokaryotes. These sequences are the same\h when read from 5' to 3' on one strand of DNA\h\h and from 5' to 3' on the complementary strand, and\h are therefore described as palindromic repeats,\h\h just the way that we refer to words like racecar\h or kayak as being palindromes, because they are\h\h the same whether read forwards or backwards. This\h was further reported in both Gram-positive and\h\h Gram-negative bacteria, along with archaea,\h leading to the obvious question regarding\h\h the relevance of CRISPR to these organisms,\h which drove research for some time. Later on,\h\h in the mid 2000s, the functionality and importance\h of CRISPR was first realized in prokaryotes. As it\h\h turns out, the CRISPR system is a key component\h of their adaptive immunity, which protects\h\h these prokaryotes from attack by viral DNA,\h bacteriophages, and plasmids. That’s right, it\h\h may seem incredible, but even unicellular bacteria\h have a very basic immune system. Recall from our\h\h studies in the immunology series that adaptive\h immunity refers to the immunity that an organism\h\h acquires after exposure to an antigen, either\h from a pathogen or vaccination. Vaccination,\h\h for example, results in a form of adaptive\h immunity in humans, since the body is exposed to\h\h antigens, and forms antibodies in response, which\h contribute to the development of the immunity.\h The way this works for bacteria is as follows. The\h unique sequences that are nestled in between the\h\h palindromic repeats, which are called spacers, are\h bits of DNA that are foreign, and do not belong to\h\h the bacterium, but instead originate from mobile\h genetic elements, or MGEs, such as bacteriophages,\h\h transposons, or plasmids that have\h previously infected the prokaryote.\h\h This was revealed by sequencing the spacers found\h in the CRISPR system, which led to the hypothesis\h\h that this could be a defense mechanism employed\h by bacteria to recognize foreign DNA elements.\h\h During a viral infection, bacteria acquire\h a small piece of the foreign viral DNA,\h\h and integrate it into the CRISPR locus to\h generate CRISPR arrays. These consist of\h\h duplicate sequences, which are the palindromic\h repeats belonging to the bacterial genome,\h\h flanked by variable sequences, or spacers, which\h again are from the foreign genetic elements.\h\h In this way, bacteria retain a memory,\h so to speak, of a past infection.\h So although it was initially revealed as a\h genomic component of bacteria and archaea,\h\h CRISPR has inspired a method of genome editing\h that can be applied to various eukaryotic\h\h species. But before we get there, we first have to\h understand the function of CRISPR in prokaryotes,\h\h because understanding the mechanism of its natural\h function will be necessary in order to understand\h\h the way it is exploited to achieve genome editing\h capabilities in humans and other organisms.\h Let’s take a look at a particular Streptococcus\h bacterium which is being attacked by a\h\h bacteriophage. Once the viral DNA is injected into\h the cell, a section of it can be incorporated into\h\h the bacterial genome, and as we mentioned, it\h will be inserted between the repeated palindromic\h\h sequences. This will now be called a spacer.\h So here we can see three different spacers,\h\h potentially from three different viruses,\h sandwiched in between the repeated palindromic\h\h sequences. Now we have what is called a\h CRISPR array. This CRISPR array can undergo\h\h transcription, to form CRISPR RNA, abbreviated\h as crRNA, although this longer strand is called\h\h pre-crRNA. Then the protein Cas9 gets involved.\h Cas refers to CRISPR-associated nuclease protein,\h\h and as we know, nucleases are enzymes\h that are capable of cleaving DNA\h\h at specific nucleotide linkages, kind of\h like a pair of scissors. In particular, Cas9\h\h is one of the nucleases found in Streptococcus\h pyogenes, which is one of the most extensively\h\h researched and characterized CRISPR-associated\h nuclease proteins, so this is the one we will be\h\h looking at here inside this bacterium. Now along with Cas9, there are also\h\h molecules of tracrRNA. These have sections\h that are complementary to and therefore\h\h can anneal to the palindromic repeats. So for\h each spacer and palindromic repeat, we end up\h\h with a complex consisting of that segment of\h pre-crRNA, a tracrRNA, and a Cas9 protein.\h\h Then another enzyme called ribonuclease three,\h or RNase III, will cleave the strand in between\h\h these complexes, leaving us with individual crRNA\h complexes which we can call effector complexes.\h\h With these effector complexes formed, the cell\h is now ready to defend against the invader\h\h whose genome produced that crRNA. If this\h complex encounters a section of viral DNA\h\h that has a sequence which is complementary to this\h crRNA, the nuclease enzyme will coordinate, and\h\h if it recognizes a short sequence unique to the\h viral genome called a protospacer adjacent motif,\h\h or PAM, then it will snip both strands of the\h DNA, just a few base pairs upstream from the PAM.\h\h In doing so, it will neutralize the\h virus, because its genome can no\h\h longer be transcribed properly to create more\h viral particles, so infection is impossible.\h So that gives us a reasonable understanding of\h how CRISPR is employed by prokaryotic organisms\h\h as a natural defense. Now it’s time to understand\h how this phenomenon came to serve as the basis for\h\h biotechnological application. This begins in\h 2012, when Jennifer Doudna, a molecular biologist\h\h from the University of California, Berkeley\h along with French microbiologist Emmanuelle\h\h Charpentier, were the first to propose\h that the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 system\h\h could be used as a programmable toolkit for\h genome editing in humans and other animal species,\h\h and they eventually received the Nobel\h prize in chemistry for their work, in 2020.\h So how can genome editing be achieved using this\h method? The first thing we need to understand\h\h is that in bacteria, the crRNA and tracrRNA are\h separate molecular entities. The first major\h\h breakthrough arrived when it was realized that\h the roles of these molecules could be combined\h\h into a single molecule by fusing them together\h with a linker to generate something called\h\h single guide RNA, or sgRNA, which\h can be synthesized in the lab.\h\h If the sgRNA complexes with a Cas9 protein, this\h two-component system will be able to cleave DNA\h\h just as the three-component system does in\h bacteria. What this means was that it was then\h\h possible to determine any sequence of about 20\h base pairs as a target for editing, and all that\h\h has to be done is to synthesize the appropriate\h sgRNA with the complementary sequence, and insert\h\h that into a cell along with the Cas9 protein which\h has been sourced from Streptococcus pyogenes.\h\h The complex will form, read the DNA until it\h finds the appropriate sequence along with a\h\h PAM sequence, binding will occur, and DNA will\h be cleaved at precisely the desired location.\h\h Cas9 has two domains, and each one\h will snip one of the DNA strands.\h After the incision is made, the natural DNA\h repair mechanism is enacted for the target DNA.\h\h The cleaved dsDNA can undergo repair via two\h routes. Either by homology-directed repair,\h\h abbreviated as HDR, or by non-homologous end\h joining, abbreviated as NHEJ. The NHEJ pathway\h\h repairs double-strand breaks in DNA by directly\h ligating without the need for a homologous\h\h template, which means a DNA strand with similar\h sequence that can act as a template. The NHEJ\h\h mechanism can also introduce insertion or deletion\h of specific sequences at the joining ends,\h\h thus creating what are referred to as indels.\h Indels are DNA strands with either an insertion\h\h or deletion of nucleotide sequences. Thus, NHEJ\h produces DNA strands with non-uniformity in size.\h The other route of repair, the HDR pathway, is\h commonly found in bacterial and archaeal cells,\h\h while the NHEJ pathway we just discussed is more\h common in a eukaryotic domain. The HDR process,\h\h although more complex than NHEJ, uses a homologous\h DNA template. The homologous DNA template has\h\h homology to the adjacent sequences surrounding the\h site of cleavage to incorporate new DNA fragments.\h\h The template guides the repair process, and\h lowers the possibility of errors. Since there is\h\h no insertion or deletion of nucleotide sequences,\h the HDR pathway maintains uniformity in the size\h\h of the resulting dsDNA, unlike NHEJ. So that covers the mechanism of CRISPR\h\h genome editing technology. Now we move on\h to the potential applications, which have\h\h only expanded ever since Doudna and Charpentier\h suggested the possibility of using CRISPR for\h\h genome editing in humans and other animals. The\h potential scope of application of CRISPR is vast,\h\h and includes its use as a genetic screen\h to identify genes in different cells.\h\h One of the most prominent applications is in\h cancer immunotherapy. In this practice, immune\h\h T cells, which are a type of white blood cell that\h fights against a disease, are genetically modified\h\h using CRISPR technology. Specifically, these\h T cells are extracted from the patient’s body\h\h and modified to make them more specialized in\h recognizing cancer cells and killing them once\h\h they are reintroduced into the patient’s body. Similarly, CRISPR has also found its application\h\h in therapeutic management of acquired\h immunodeficiency syndrome, or AIDS, which is\h\h caused by human immunodeficiency virus, also known\h as HIV, as we covered in the microbiology series.\h\h Conventional anti-retroviral therapies are\h capable of suppressing viral replication. With so many invigorating possibilities\h\h for this exciting new technology, it will be\h fascinating to see which of these major diseases\h\h and issues will be solved first, signaling\h the dawn of a new era in molecular biology.

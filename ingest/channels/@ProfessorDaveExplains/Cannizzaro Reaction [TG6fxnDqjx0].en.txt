Continuing our study of name reactions,\h let’s investigate another oldie but goodie,\h\h the Cannizzaro reaction. This reaction\h involves the disproportionation\h\h of two equivalents of a non-enolizable aldehyde,\h where disproportionation means that one molecule\h\h is oxidized, and the other is reduced. So as we\h can see here, two equivalents of the aldehyde\h\h will produce one equivalent of the corresponding\h alcohol and one equivalent of the carboxylic acid.\h\h The reaction was discovered by Italian chemist\h Stanislao Cannizzaro at the University of Palermo,\h\h in Sicily, who published his results in 1853.\h In its original formulation, two molecules of\h\h benzaldehyde react in an aqueous sodium hydroxide\h solution to yield one molecule of benzoic\h\h acid and one of benzyl alcohol. Given that the\h reaction occurs under strongly basic conditions,\h\h the product is actually sodium benzoate, which\h is water soluble, and therefore benzyl alcohol,\h\h which is neutral, can be separated by extraction\h into ether. Dilute hydrochloric acid is then used\h\h to protonate the benzoate to give benzoic acid. There is an enduring joke among synthetic\h\h chemists, which says Cannizzaro was so poor that\h he could only afford two chemicals for his lab,\h\h sodium hydroxide and benzaldehyde. He mixed them\h together and thusly discovered his name reaction.\h\h Of course this is indeed just a joke, but\h the reaction illustrates how empirically\h\h and haphazardly organic reactions\h were discovered back then.\h The reaction is quite general for aldehydes that\h cannot enolize. If the aldehyde is enolizable,\h\h meaning that it has alpha hydrogens, then enolates\h tend to form, followed by aldol reactions.\h\h The synthetic utility of the reaction is limited,\h because there are better ways to oxidize an\h\h aldehyde to a carboxylic acid or reduce it\h to an alcohol. It is, however, important\h\h to be aware of this reactivity when planning\h reactions of aldehydes under basic conditions,\h\h such as with aldol chemistry. For example, when\h using formaldehyde under basic conditions, a very\h\h important side reaction will be its Cannizzaro\h reaction to yield methanol and formic acid,\h\h which in many cases can participate in further\h side reactions. As we will see in a moment,\h\h clever applications of the Cannizzaro\h chemistry have been devised, and therefore\h\h the reaction also has synthetic significance. The mechanism of the reaction is a bit complex.\h\h This is demonstrated by the fact that the rate\h equation contains two terms. As we can see,\h\h the reaction is second order in aldehyde, which\h confirms that there are two molecules reacting\h\h with each other in the rate-determining step, but\h the order in hydroxide ion is somewhere between\h\h one and two, revealing a more complex role\h for the base. Let’s see how this works now.\h Beginning with this generalized aldehyde and\h under strongly basic conditions, the hydroxide\h\h ion attacks the carbonyl to form this species, at\h equilibrium. Under sufficiently basic conditions,\h\h this species can be deprotonated to form this\h dianion, again at equilibrium. Although the\h\h main species in solution is the previous one, the\h dianion is much more reactive, and it is believed\h\h that the key step proceeds via this species.\h The key step is the irreversible expulsion of\h\h a hydride from the dianion. The hydride is passed\h along to a second molecule of neutral aldehyde,\h\h forming the corresponding carboxylate and\h alkoxide. In this way, the aldehyde oxidizes\h\h while reducing another molecule. Finally a water\h molecule will protonate the alkoxide to give the\h\h alcohol. We should note that reaction through\h to the dianion involves the intervention of\h\h two hydroxide ions, which explains why the rate\h equation is partially second-order in hydroxide.\h Now that we understand the mechanism, let’s check\h out some applications. A Cannizzaro reaction may\h\h be observed when carrying out crossed-aldol\h reactions. These reactions are very successful\h\h when an enolizable, substituted aldehyde is\h reacted with a non-enolizable, reactive aldehyde.\h\h In this way a homo aldol reaction cannot\h occur. Let’s take a look at an example of\h\h this. If cyclohexyl carboxaldehyde is reacted\h with formaldehyde under basic conditions,\h\h only one enolate can form, because formaldehyde\h has no alpha-hydrogens. At the same time,\h\h the enolate of cyclohexyl carboxaldehyde will\h react preferentially with the much more reactive\h\h formaldehyde and not with itself, to yield the\h crossed-aldol product in high yield, which is this\h\h compound here. However, now the crossed-aldol\h product is also a non-enolizable aldehyde,\h\h given that this alpha carbon is now a quaternary\h center, so this can be involved in a Cannizzaro\h\h reaction with formaldehyde or with itself. If the\h chemist carrying out this procedure is not careful\h\h and does not control the reaction, they may\h end up obtaining only the Cannizzaro products.\h Apart from these situations involving unwanted\h side products, there are actually quite a few\h\h clever synthetic applications of this chemistry.\h We cannot list them all, but just to give you\h\h an idea of what chemists can do with this\h reaction, consider the following substrate.\h\h If a molecule contains two carbonyl groups and\h yet is not enolizable, as is the case here,\h\h one might wonder if it is possible to induce\h an intramolecular hydride transfer. Remember\h\h that the key step in the mechanism involves\h hydride transfer from one aldehyde to another,\h\h and there is no reason why this could not occur\h with adjacent carbonyls on the same molecule,\h\h which in this case would yield mandelic acid.\h Indeed, this can be done very cleanly, and the\h\h hydrogen on the aldehyde group can be transferred\h onto the ketone carbonyl. Chemists have been at\h work to make this reaction enantioselective.\h\h To do this, one needs some kind of chiral\h template to guide the reaction, ideally a\h\h catalyst, so as to use only a tiny amount.\h Also, this catalyst must be very effective\h\h to overshadow the uncatalyzed reaction, which\h would lead to a racemate. This is also quite\h\h possible. To illustrate this transformation,\h we will highlight a study by Xiaoming Feng from\h\h Sichuan University in China, published in 2015. This brilliant work highlights several facets of\h\h the Cannizzaro chemistry. First, it can occur\h intramolecularly. If the aldehyde hydrogen is\h\h isotopically labeled as deuterium, all of\h it gets transferred to the adjacent ketone.\h\h Second, metals catalyze the reaction, and this\h application is predicated on the ability of iron\h\h three salts to promote the hydrogen transfer,\h although a discussion of the exact mechanism is\h\h too complex for this tutorial. Given that this is\h the case, designing a chiral ligand to bind both\h\h iron three and the substrate has the possibility\h of inducing enantioselectivity, although obviously\h\h not just any ligand will do. Attack of this base onto the aldehyde\h will ultimately lead to an ester of mandelic acid.\h\h Obviously, all sorts of new, substituted mandelic\h acids can be made by this methodology as well.\h This is just one of the many applications of the\h Cannizzaro reaction which creative chemists have\h\h been able to invent. In this way, the old\h chemistry of supposedly destitute Professor\h\h Cannizzaro is alive and well almost\h 170 years since its initial report.

In an earlier tutorial we briefly discussed\h the difficulty of hydrogenating alkenes.\h\h Although exergonic in most cases, and\h therefore thermodynamically favorable,\h\h it remains problematic from the standpoint\h of kinetics, such that hydrogen gas and an\h\h olefin will essentially never react on their own,\h requiring some kind of catalysis. For some time\h\h this has been and still is a fascinating topic\h of research, as demonstrated by the number of\h\h Nobel prizes awarded to hydrogenation chemists. As we also mentioned previously, an early solution\h\h to this kinetic problem was found in 1897 by\h French chemist Paul Sabatier, who discovered\h\h that gaseous mixtures of ethylene and hydrogen\h could be converted to ethane by action of a\h\h nickel surface under extreme conditions. For this\h process, Sabatier received the 1912 Nobel prize in\h\h chemistry. Nickel is still used, industrially as\h well as in the laboratory, to hydrogenate alkenes.\h\h Chemists now use a special grade of finely\h divided material, called Raney Nickel in honor\h\h of its inventor, American engineer Murray Raney. Supported palladium is widely used as well, often\h\h as palladium on charcoal. Rhodium and platinum\h are other surfaces commonly used to hydrogenate\h\h alkenes. Industrially, these processes are\h important in the petroleum industry, as alkanes\h\h are better and cleaner fuels than unsaturated\h hydrocarbons. The same goes for the food industry,\h\h where unsaturated vegetable fats are often\h hydrogenated to yield more saturated products,\h\h like the well-known margarine. From a stability\h standpoint, saturated hydrocarbons are preferred\h\h products, because they do not easily react with\h oxygen, forming unstable and toxic hydroperoxides.\h The mechanism of these hydrogenations\h is understood in its gross features,\h\h as shown in the following scheme. In the solution\h or gas phase, both the alkene and the hydrogen\h\h molecules independently diffuse towards the active\h metal surface. The olefin is chemically adsorbed\h\h onto the surface, forming a loose pi bond with the\h metal, whereas the hydrogen molecule is reversibly\h\h cleaved to form two M-H bonds. If the alkene\h is bound at the site vicinal to a newly formed\h\h M-H bond, a metal alkyl complex is formed as the\h hydrogen is transferred to one of the carbon atoms\h\h in the olefin. This step is reversible. We know\h this because with incomplete reactions we observe\h\h alkene cis/trans isomerization, which means the\h metal-alkyl moiety has time to rotate around the\h\h C-C bond and redeposit the beta-hydrogen onto the\h surface. For the final step, the atomic hydrogen\h\h must migrate next to the metal center bound to the\h alkyl for the reductive elimination to take place,\h\h thus releasing the alkane product. This\h is the energetically favorable step of the\h\h catalytic cycle, and it is irreversible. Alkynes are hydrogenated to alkanes by a\h\h double hydrogenation mechanism similar to the\h one we just described. However, the first step\h\h is usually faster than the second one, and it\h is possible, under select conditions, to stop\h\h at the alkene stage. Alkynes are hydrogenated to\h alkenes most commonly using Lindlar’s catalyst,\h\h which is a special grade of palladium metal\h supported on barium sulfate and “poisoned”\h\h with quinoline. This reaction, which occurs\h with stereoselectivity for the Z alkene, should\h\h be familiar from our study of organic chemistry. Industrial hydrogenations are complex to develop\h\h due to the gradual fouling of the catalytic\h surface, with increasing deposits of carbonaceous\h\h material altering the rate and selectivity of the\h reaction. For simple industrial substrates like\h\h petrochemicals or fatty acids, these difficulties\h are tolerated. For more value-added products,\h\h such as fine chemicals and pharmaceuticals, a\h milder and more selective form of hydrogenation\h\h is used, such as homogeneous hydrogenation. The catalysts are usually rhodium, iridium,\h\h or ruthenium complexes which are quite\h soluble in organic solvents. They work by\h\h a qualitatively similar mechanism as the one we\h just discussed for heterogeneous hydrogenation,\h\h with a few added complications. First, the\h metal is coordinated to a number of ligands,\h\h and ligand dissociation acquires a special\h importance. Second, we have seen that alkenes\h\h and hydrogen diffuse and react with a metal\h surface independently. In homogeneous catalysis,\h\h the order is important. There are “hydrogen first”\h catalysts and “olefin first” catalysts. Out of\h\h sequence coordination usually inhibits catalysis. The prototypical “hydrogen first” catalyst is\h\h Wilkinson’s catalyst, developed by English chemist\h Geoffrey Wilkinson and acknowledged with the\h\h Nobel Prize for chemistry in 1973. This rhodium\h catalyst reacts under extremely mild conditions,\h\h often room temperature and atmospheric\h pressure of hydrogen gas, and displays\h\h excellent chemoselectivity. If a polyunsaturated\h substrate is present, the chances are excellent\h\h that the less substituted double bonds will be\h selectively saturated. This means that mono and\h\h disubstituted olefins can easily be hydrogenated,\h but the more substituted substrates do not react\h\h readily and are left untouched. There is only\h one puzzling feature to this selectivity trend,\h\h and that is the position of ethylene, the least\h substituted olefin and yet the most difficult to\h\h hydrogenate. How could this be the case? To answer\h this question we need to look at the mechanism.\h First of all, let us look at the structure\h of the catalyst: it is a rhodium one species,\h\h so it is a d8 species. Four two-electron donors\h make Wilkinson‘s catalyst a 16 electron species.\h\h We have seen that many compounds of rhodium one,\h iridium one, palladium two, and platinum two, all\h\h 16 electron species, are very stable in a square\h planar geometry, so a bit counterintuitively,\h\h binding of a fifth ligand to reach the usual 18\h electron quota is not favorable. The reason for\h\h this is the high potential energy of the b1g\h orbital, which makes bonding at the two axial\h\h positions thermodynamically labile. As a result,\h in many catalytic cycles the intermediates are\h\h 14 electron species, which means the reaction is\h L-dissociative. This is the case with our rhodium\h\h catalyst, and hydrogenations are inhibited by\h added triphenylphosphine. We consider the empty\h\h coordinating position loosely occupied by the\h solvent, if the solvent has any donicity. For the\h\h electron count, however, we consider this position\h empty, given that solvents like dichloromethane,\h\h benzene, or methanol, are poorly coordinating\h toward rhodium. So consider this just a formality.\h Let us follow the rest of the mechanism. The\h first species in the cycle is I-1, which is\h\h electron-poor with 14 electrons, and therefore\h eager to acquire ligands. Oxidative addition\h\h with hydrogen gives I-2, now a 16 electron species\h and also featuring a higher oxidation state than\h\h before, rhodium three. At this point, the alkene\h coordinates through its pi cloud, to yield I-3.\h\h Hydrometalation follows, with the syn H migrating\h to one of the carbons in the alkene. Now I-4\h\h needs to isomerize to yield I-5 for the reductive\h elimination to take place. The hydrogen and carbon\h\h involved in the reaction need to be close to each\h other, so the reaction cannot occur in I-4 because\h\h the two substituents are trans. There are several\h mechanisms for isomerization at the rhodium three\h\h center, but it is a bit too complex to discuss\h them here. We must simply know that isomerizations\h\h in these species are quite easy to achieve,\h and so I-5 is formed readily at equilibrium.\h\h Reductive elimination can thus generate the alkane\h product and I-1, which initiates the cycle again.\h\h Most of these steps are reversible, and there\h are other intermediates in the scheme which we\h\h have left out for simplicity, but what we have\h highlighted is the basic mechanism for a number\h\h of “hydrogen first” transition-metal catalysts. With the mechanism now understood, we can explain\h\h why ethylene does not readily get hydrogenated.\h Species I-1 reacts almost irreversibly with\h\h ethylene to form the off-cycle species OC-1. This\h is not an intermediate in the hydrogenation as it\h\h does not react with hydrogen gas, and therefore\h ethylene acts as a poison, permanently occupying\h\h this coordination site and rendering the catalytic\h complex inactive. More substituted alkenes bind\h\h poorly and reversibly to I-1 so their inhibitory\h effect is therefore small or non-existent.\h\h Thus the catalytic cycle may occur as intended. Now that we understand hydrogen first catalysts,\h\h how do “olefin first” catalysts work? The basic\h steps are the same as what we just learned,\h\h but they occur in a different order. If the\h catalyst is an M-H species, the hydrometalation\h\h can occur before the oxidative addition of\h H2, which occurs later, right before the\h\h reductive elimination. This is exemplified by the\h hydrogenation catalyst with the following formula,\h\h consisting of a rhodium center, one carbonyl\h ligand, three triphenylphosphines which we can\h\h represent as L for ligand, and a hydrogen. First,\h one of the triphenylphosphines will dissociate\h\h to yield this square planar complex. If the\h catalyst then coordinates with an alkene moiety,\h\h in this case one that is monosubstituted, this is\h not a dead-end species like it was in the previous\h\h cycle we examined, but instead can insert via\h the pre-existing rhodium-hydrogen bond. It is\h\h at this point that oxidative addition of hydrogen\h will occur, pushing the carbonyl ligand down. And\h\h lastly reductive elimination can occur with\h the newly saturated alkyl fragment and one\h\h of these hydrogens to yield the alkane product and\h regenerate the square planar 16 electron catalytic\h\h intermediate, thus perpetuating the cycle. So\h the interesting thing about this mechanism is\h\h that the hydrogen involved in the hydrometalation\h was already present on the catalyst, and molecular\h\h hydrogen will oxidatively add after this step\h to replenish this hydrogen and also perform the\h\h final reductive elimination. Many additional\h hydrogenation catalysts can be described\h\h qualitatively by one of these two cycles we just\h discussed, whether hydrogen first or olefin first.\h To summarize, we have seen how industrial\h applications of heterogeneous hydrogenation are\h\h carried out under harsh, biphasic conditions.\h For more complex, multifunctional molecules,\h\h like pharmaceuticals, chemists prefer homogeneous\h catalysts. We have seen that the success of these\h\h catalysts rests on the ability of the metal to\h easily switch between two alternating oxidation\h\h states, to coordinate reactants in a specific\h order, and to be able to associate and dissociate\h\h spectator ligands like triphenylphosphine when\h needed, to make room in the coordination sphere\h\h for the reactants. It is therefore evident\h that creating a truly effective catalyst is\h\h not an easy task, and requires much empirical\h and theoretical work, especially because metals\h\h like palladium, platinum, rhodium, and\h iridium are extremely rare and expensive,\h\h and the requirement is for these catalysts to be\h robust and have a high turnover number. Alkene\h\h hydrogenation is still one of the most important\h areas of catalysis research, so we will continue\h\h to discuss this topic in the next tutorial\h which introduces an aspect of stereochemistry.

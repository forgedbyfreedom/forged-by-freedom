The primary difficulty is withstanding\h\h the high pressures that result from the\h weight of up to 11 kilometers of water,\h\h yielding pressures of more than 1000 atmospheres.\h Though the deepest areas of the ocean, trenches,\h\h only represents a small area of the ocean,\h its average depth is still a substantial 3.5\h\h kilometers. But, as we know from studying Earth’s\h layers, we do not have to visit an area of the\h\h Earth to know something about it. Oceanographers\h use methods like sonar, which reflects\h\h sonic waves off the ocean floor, to determine its\h topography and create detailed bathymetric maps.\h\h Instruments called echo sounders emit a sound\h pulse, and then measure the amount of time it\h\h takes the pulse to reach the ocean floor,\h bounce off of it, and return to the sounder.\h\h Since the speed of the pulse can vary with ocean\h water’s salinity, pressure, and temperature,\h\h engineers developed multibeam echosounders that\h send out hundreds of sound pulses and can measure\h\h the angle of return from each pulse. With the high\h angular resolution of the multibeam echosounder,\h\h scientists can measure ocean floor features\h from multiple sides and create a more realistic\h\h picture of what these features look like. So, what type of landforms are on the ocean floor?\h\h For the most part, the very bottom of the\h ocean floor, called the abyssal plain,\h\h is flat and featureless. At the center of an ocean\h basin lies a mid-ocean ridge, where new oceanic\h\h crust is being created. On either side of\h an ocean lies a continental margin, which is\h\h simply the transition zone from land to sea. The\h continental margin is composed of three areas,\h\h the continental shelf, which is a gently sloping\h surface that makes up all shorelines, the\h\h continental slope, a steep drop-off that marks the\h edge of the continental crust, and the continental\h\h rise, which is an area where sediment from the\h continental slope piles up during underwater\h\h avalanches called turbidity currents. The abyssal\h plane, which is probably what you imagine when you\h\h think of the ocean floor, is located between\h the mid-ocean ridge and continental margin.\h\h The abyssal plane ranges in depth from 3 to 6\h kilometers and covers 70% of the ocean floor.\h\h As we mentioned, it is predominately flat\h and featureless, with the occasional trench\h\h near areas experiencing subduction, or volcanic\h seamount above hotspots, breaking up the monotony.\h Lying atop the oceanic crust is a thick layer of\h sediment, blanketing the ocean floor and providing\h\h a habitat for many types of burrowing organisms.\h Sediment thickness varies based mainly on the\h\h age of the crust and the distance from land, with\h the thickest sediments occurring closest to land,\h\h where both the sediment supply is greatest and\h oceanic crust is oldest. The largest sediment\h\h deposits in today’s oceans are in the Bay of\h Bengal and Gulf of Mexico, where they approach\h\h 20 kilometers thick in some spots. For comparison,\h atop the youngest crust near mid-ocean ridges,\h\h sediment may only be millimeters thick. As\h sediment is progressively buried in basins,\h\h the weight of the overburden causes the\h sediment to compact and minerals such as\h\h quartz and calcite begin to precipitate from the\h mineral-laden pore waters, cementing sediment\h\h grains together to form a sedimentary\h rock. This combined process of compaction\h\h and cementation is called lithification. To fully understand sedimentary rocks, we need\h\h to understand sediments, where they come from,\h how they are transported, and what happens to them\h\h during transport. There are two types of sediment\h found in the ocean: terrigenous and biogenic.\h\h Terrigenous sediment is made up of minerals and\h small rocks that eroded from continental crust,\h\h or more simply, the land. Wind and water, though\h mainly water, carry sediment from the mountains\h\h to the oceans. The reason that the Bay of Bengal\h has such a thick sediment layer is that several\h\h large river systems are dumping millions of tons\h of eroded sediment from the rising Himalayas.\h\h In a similar manner, the Mississippi river carries\h loads of sediment from the eroding Rockies and\h\h Appalachians into the Gulf of Mexico. This is\h terrigenous sediment. Most terrigenous sediment\h\h is transported to the oceans during large storms\h and floods when river flow is fastest. This is\h\h the same reason why you probably shake your orange\h juice before pouring a glass. Shaking it increases\h\h its velocity, allowing the juice to lift the pulp\h into suspension. Once the sediment reaches the\h\h open ocean, flow velocity drops, and it begins\h to settle out by mass. Sand settles out first,\h\h followed by silt, then mud, with clay being the\h last to settle out, sometimes remaining suspended\h\h in the ocean for hundreds to thousands of years.\h Since the largest grains settle out first, sand\h\h and silt are only found near the shoreline,\h with mud accumulating further offshore, and\h\h only the smallest clay particles reaching the open\h ocean, slowly accumulating on the abyssal plane.\h Biogenic sediments, or “oozes”, are made\h of materials created by living organisms.\h\h There are two categories of biogenic\h sediment that accumulate on the ocean floor:\h\h calcareous and siliceous. Calcareous biogenic\h sediments are comprised of the calcium carbonate\h\h skeletons from single-celled marine organisms like\h foraminifera. However, since calcium carbonate\h\h dissolves readily in colder, deeper water,\h calcareous oozes are primarily found in warm,\h\h shallow seas. The depth at which calcium\h carbonate starts to dissolve in ocean water\h\h is called the carbonate compensation depth,\h abbreviated as CCD. Since the CCD is a function\h\h of temperature and seawater chemistry, its exact\h depth varies, being deeper in equatorial regions\h\h and shallower near the poles. By mapping this\h depth across the ocean, scientists created the\h\h lysocline, which shows how the CCD changes with\h location. Siliceous biogenic sediments are made\h\h of the silica skeletons from single-celled marine\h organisms like diatoms and radiolarians. Siliceous\h\h oozes are essentially the opposite of calcareous\h oozes in that the silica tends to dissolve\h\h rapidly in the shallow ocean, whereas carbonate\h rapidly dissolves in deep water, as we mentioned.\h\h Given the high solubility in seawater,\h siliceous oozes only form in areas where\h\h diatoms and radiolarians are thriving,\h and sedimentation can exceed dissolution.\h Unlike the calcareous, siliceous oozes are likely\h to be found in the cold, deep water at the poles\h\h or in areas where upwelling occurs. Upwelling\h is a process where strong winds and the Earth’s\h\h rotation cause shallow waters to diverge, allowing\h deep, cold water to rise to the surface, filling\h\h the space created by the diverging surface waters.\h Upwelling tends to occur along western shorelines\h\h where winds blow from the North, which\h when modified by the Coriolis Force,\h\h cause an ocean current that moves away from\h land, or 90 degrees to the right of the wind.\h\h This movement of water at right angles to the\h prevailing winds is called Eckman Transport.\h\h The Coriolis Force deflects moving objects\h to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and\h\h to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. This\h applies to all moving fluids on Earth’s surface,\h\h including the atmosphere.

Up until the 20th century, ethical theories\h were predominantly derived from one of the\h\h three main traditions: Virtue Ethics, based on\h the Aristotelian school which focused on character\h\h development toward being virtuous; Deontology,\h associated with Kant and Plato, encompassing\h\h all moral theories focusing on principles and\h values; and finally, Consequentialism, based on\h\h the utilitarian model of the best results for the\h greatest number of people. But in the early 1900s,\h\h George Edward Moore, one of the founders of the\h analytic tradition alongside Bertrand Russell,\h\h while debating and defending his consequentialist\h theory, inadvertently developed a novel manner to\h\h deal with ethics in philosophy. Not another school\h of thought intending to define what is right or\h\h wrong, but rather an attempt to identify how we\h can talk about ethical subjects in general: the\h\h field of metaethics. Much like metaphysics, which\h studies the existential structure of the objects\h\h that physics studies in the world, metaethics\h attempts to understand the basis of moral\h\h phenomena without arriving at any value judgment.\h Instead of saying that X is morally good or bad,\h\h it tries to analyze how people arrive at such a\h decision, and what it means to be “morally good”\h\h or “virtuous” or not. In this tutorial, we will\h take a look at how metaethics was established by\h\h Moore and developed until recent decades, setting\h a new stage for the traditional ethical theories\h\h to expand further into uncharted territory. Moore defended an intuitionist impersonal\h\h consequentialism. This means he defended the idea\h that that which was inherently “good” could only\h\h be assessed by intuition, as it would not be\h something you can simply identify objectively.\h\h Intuition should not be understood as the mystical\h or religious use the term, as an extra faculty\h\h about a hidden reality, but rather as a way to\h obtain direct and simple knowledge – a mistaken\h\h interpretation that John Mackie would later\h attribute to intuitionist theories of morality. So\h\h this interpretation of “good” is quite singular:\h unlike everyday uses of “good” such as with “good\h\h food”, “good night” and so on, the definition\h of a “moral good” cannot be identified as a\h\h single object in the world, but only found within\h specific contexts. Moral goodness then would only\h\h be assessed intuitively in a holistic relationship\h with whatever actually involved it. One would not\h\h say that the “treeness” of a tree lies solely in\h its leaves, branches, trunk, or roots, but only\h\h when all the parts are present. This also happens\h with “good” in his perspective. One can consider\h\h a “good” action, but one can never isolate a part\h of it and say “the precise action of carrying the\h\h bags of that elderly lady to help her cross the\h street is what made the entire action morally\h\h good”. Moral goodness is strictly inherent in what\h we intuitively identify as good, so it’s not a\h\h separate object we can merely associate with other\h things to make or identify them as good – thus,\h\h good itself is undefinable, only identifiable\h through our unmediated intuition within the\h\h context in which it appears. We can make a further\h analogy with colors: you can immediately identify\h\h the color “yellow” when you see it, but it’s hard\h to define or say anything more about it. The same\h\h thing happens with morally “good” action: you know\h it when you see it, but there’s often little else\h\h we can say about it. Moore’s understanding of\h moral good can then be defined as something\h\h having “intrinsic value”. Therefore, he is not\h a subjectivist, as for him goodness is real,\h\h it exists in the world – it simply cannot be\h reduced specifically to something within it.\h He advances this conception as he criticizes\h what was “ethical naturalism” back in the late\h\h 19th century as defined by Spencer. As we saw\h previously, Spencer’s theory had several flaws\h\h in interpreting and adapting Darwin to a proper\h philosophical, ethical, and political theory,\h\h but some of those errors were exaggerated by\h critics, especially by Moore. Not only that,\h\h but Moore also confused himself thoroughly\h in his criticism. To criticize Spencer and\h\h the moral naturalism he represented, he coined\h two concepts which he sometimes used differently\h\h but other times interchangeably, to his and\h his readers’ confusion to this day. Those are\h\h the naturalistic fallacy and the open-question\h argument, either or both of which are frequently\h\h confused with Hume’s “is-ought gap”,\h or Hume’s Law. To save us some time,\h\h an explanation where he recognized his confusion\h and helped clarify what he originally meant\h\h was posthumously published 90 years after the\h original 1903 publication of his major ethical\h\h work where these ideas were first presented,\h Principia Ethica, a title analogous to Russell\h\h and Whitehead’s Principia Mathematica, as we saw\h previously. To commit the naturalistic fallacy,\h\h one has to commit any or all of these errors.\h First, confusing different logical types by taking\h\h a class as a member of itself, such as looking for\h the campus in its individual university buildings,\h\h or claiming that the number 7 is green, known\h today as a “category mistake” in logic. Second,\h\h identifying “good” with anything, for when you do\h so, you can always question “but is that good?”\h\h and it will always make sense to do so, which\h leads back to “good” being undefinable. This\h\h is the famous “open-question argument”, a very\h weak argument, since, for example, “bank” is\h\h not undefinable just because it has multiple\h different meanings – money bank, river bank,\h\h and so forth. The same goes for any word which\h changes its meaning throughout time. Third,\h\h and actually reminiscent of Hume’s Law, would be\h to proceed in an inference involving any of the\h\h previous two, that is, to derive a conclusion in a\h logical argument that presupposes or arrives at a\h\h category mistake or an open-question argument. Therefore, “naturalistic” actually applies to\h\h “natural objects” in the logical sense, as the\h smallest unit of information such as “chair”,\h\h “green”, or “X”, and not necessarily to moral\h naturalism. Though extremely crude and confusing\h\h as he himself recognized, Moore’s analysis of\h what constitutes what we call “good” generated\h\h an impactful wave in ethics, and along with\h the analytic linguistic turn, he helped to\h\h generate with his approach to morality focused\h on language, what would be one of the largest\h\h emerging schools of thought in contemporary\h philosophy, that of metaethics. Afterwards,\h\h he then goes on to defend consequentialism based\h on an impartial evaluation of the greater good,\h\h as defined by him, for the most people. This opened up a wave of new classifications\h\h for ethical theories. Regarding the reality of\h moral judgement, Moore for example was a realist,\h\h because even though you cannot reduce “the good”\h to any other metaphysical properties in the world,\h\h it still exists and can be measured through\h pleasure and pain. Hume, on the other hand,\h\h would be an anti-realist, because moral\h distinctions are generated by our emotions\h\h subjectively, and so the property of being “good”\h isn’t ontologically objective at all. Thus,\h\h he is also a non-cognitivist, meaning that when we\h utter moral sentences, they are a result of this\h\h emotional expression, and are not to be judged as\h true or false – they simply are. A traditionally\h\h cognitivist position would be Kant’s, as when\h one checks their action against his version\h\h of a “golden rule”, also known as the categorical\h imperative, their course of action is objectively\h\h true, while if it doesn’t, then it is false. Metaethics therefore differs from normative\h\h ethics, as it does not attempt to create any\h moral norms as laws or values, only attempting\h\h to understand how we define moral goodness, what\h motivates us, and all the other characteristics\h\h of ethical behavior in a descriptive manner.\h Without noticing, Moore divided the ethicist’s\h\h job into two different tasks: explaining\h moral concepts and prescribing what is\h\h morally desirable. Ironically, most of ethical\h naturalism, the object of Moore’s criticism,\h\h does not commit the naturalistic fallacy, as it\h developed into a strong metaethical position,\h\h not attempting then to set any moral norms. Of course, we can trace metaethics back to\h\h even some early writings in Ancient philosophy\h and throughout the history of philosophy itself,\h\h as many authors attempted to understand moral\h phenomena before trying to theorize what morality\h\h should be, like when Aristotle wrote on human\h nature, when Kant discussed our mental faculties\h\h in order to assess our moral prowess, and so\h forth. But never before Moore had this level\h\h of categorical scrutiny been applied in order\h for this type of philosophical endeavor to rise\h\h so quickly.

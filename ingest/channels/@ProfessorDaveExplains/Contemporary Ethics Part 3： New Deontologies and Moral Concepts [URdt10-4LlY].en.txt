As we saw in the previous tutorial, metaethics\h had a lasting impact on how moral philosophy was\h\h developed, providing new angles to millennia-old\h issues, and also bringing novel concepts and\h\h tools for analyzing moral theories. In\h this tutorial, we’ll take a look at how\h\h deontology developed within this context, and a\h few general noteworthy discussions which became\h\h centerpieces in contemporary ethical debates. William Ross, even though he is known for\h\h translating the complete works of Aristotle,\h attempted to respond to Moore’s intuitional\h\h consequentialism by developing an intuitional,\h pluralist version of Kantian ethics. Instead of\h\h relying on a single norm such as the Categorical\h Imperative, he posits that the moral convictions\h\h of educated people generate moral knowledge.\h Though he is a non-naturalist like Moore,\h\h in that ethics is independent of any biological\h or scientific study, he is also a realist,\h\h as he believes that this knowledge exists as\h unquestionable facts in the world with inherent\h\h value, and which must be considered by any ethical\h endeavor. He even considers these facts the “data”\h\h for moral theories just like sense-perceptions\h are for the natural sciences, thus affirming\h\h that moral knowledge works just like mathematical\h knowledge. And likewise, some of these convictions\h\h may be illusory, given historical beliefs\h or practices of the agent or their context.\h\h This “inherent” value is related to his concept of\h prima facie, or “at first sight” in Latin, in that\h\h our intuition is capable of identifying this value\h without any other instrument or process. Thus,\h\h reminiscent of his admiration for Aristotle,\h he goes on to identify that there are multiple\h\h fundamental goods and manners of action, and\h not just a single main principle which comprises\h\h ethics. These fundamental goods are essentially\h goods in themselves, or have prima facie value,\h\h as do these manners of actions, called “rights”.\h He proposes a few of them but notes that they are\h\h not immutable and others may be added: the four\h main goods may be virtue, pleasure, knowledge,\h\h and justice; and rights may encompass the duty\h of benevolence, of fidelity, and self-improvement\h\h among others. If moral dilemmas were to arise by a\h conflict of two or more of these, intuition would\h\h clearly point to the one which has more weight. Ross’ understanding of ethics influenced the\h\h resurgence of “natural law”, a school of\h deontological theories with religious background\h\h which trace the value of intuition to Aquinas’\h naturalist theory. It is represented in the 20th\h\h century by authors such as John Finnis and Roger\h Scruton. Contrary to what the name suggests,\h\h they also embrace non-naturalism, as the “natural”\h in its name refers to the inherently human and\h\h primordial status of intuitive assertion, with\h which any properly rational person “uncovers”\h\h inherent values which are precursors to any\h laws, norms, or constitutions. Many criticisms\h\h have been leveled against these theological and\h conservative perspectives, as it is unclear what\h\h is the true weight of the authority of intuited\h moral goods, as from Ross to these recent authors,\h\h rational requirements and issues with\h disagreement remain largely obfuscated.\h Contractualism also developed again in deontology,\h given the new debates metaethics brought up. John\h\h Rawls was the first to propose it as a moral\h foundation to his political liberalism centered\h\h on a theory of justice as “fairness”. This\h means that he attempted to dissociate from\h\h strong metaphysical definitions of “justice”\h to the more commonplace definition “fairness”,\h\h such as “fair play” in sports. His conception of\h a person includes two main traits: 1) rationality,\h\h which we use to find what is “good” for\h us and develop a “sense of justice”,\h\h but accepts that in a democratic society there\h may be many conceptions of good and justice,\h\h or what he calls a “reasonable pluralism”, and\h also 2) reasonableness, which is the ability to\h\h defend publicly only our conceptions of “goods”\h which we reckon may be accepted even by those\h\h that mostly disagree with us, or our “considered\h convictions”. By then imagining a hypothetical\h\h “original position”, akin to a state of nature,\h under a “veil of ignorance”, meaning we’d be\h\h blind to our own individual specificities such as\h gender, ethnicity, class, and so forth, we’d be\h\h able to develop principles for public institutions\h with values that appeased everyone. These\h\h principles would then be in constant dialogue with\h people’s considered convictions, both improving\h\h one another in a coherentist process he called\h “reflective equilibrium”. He arrives at three\h\h main principles akin to the ones of the French\h Revolution: liberty, difference, in that true\h\h equality means acquiescing to our differences,\h and equal opportunity, akin to “fraternity”,\h\h in that even people in different positions must\h have just means for everyone to be able to enter,\h\h to achieve access to certain places in society. He\h uses many authors as inspirations for his theory,\h\h but leans noticeably more toward Kant, as these\h values would be universally agreeable instead of\h\h absolutely mandatory. Criticisms to his theory\h include the supposed neutrality of this process,\h\h which would disproportionally burden minorities\h and disabled people, since their rights would only\h\h be considered in a later legislative process. Thomas Nagel was one of his students, having\h\h also previously studied with Austin, and he was a\h strong proponent of rationalism, also in a Kantian\h\h perspective, as opposed to the Humean conception\h that reason is secondary to emotions in moral\h\h agency. This means that, for him, even if all\h individuals have an egoistic psychology and want\h\h only what’s best for themselves before others, in\h order to thrive in society, altruism is rationally\h\h the better course of action. Contrary to Rawls,\h he states that individuals can always think of\h\h reasonable motives for disagreement, or have a\h “reasonable rejection” of any principle. Thus,\h\h rationality leads to recognizing any other person\h as a self-interested individual with a similar\h\h nature as yourself or any other being, providing\h objective moral values from a “view from nowhere”,\h\h an impersonal standpoint. This would allow for\h control of any misgivings of moral luck, or the\h\h accidental context which would impact someone’s\h moral choices, in an interpersonal context instead\h\h of a political one as Rawls formulated.\h He also defended a mix of deontology with\h\h consequentialism, attributing consequentialist\h values to this more neutral, objective viewpoint,\h\h and deontological to our subjective context,\h as in the inviolability of persons – and these\h\h would frequently intertwine given the situation. To close this section on contemporary ethics,\h\h let’s mention a few interesting concepts.\h Hilary Putnam, who we introduced when examining\h\h epistemology, defended a “conceptual relativity”,\h a view that metaphysics was unnecessary for\h\h language and concepts in general. His ethical\h perspective follows suit in that moral judgments\h\h do not require strict laws, rules, or principles,\h but rather an “imperfect” model that may work\h\h generally in several different situations and\h continuously evolve with their communities. It\h\h is not a true relativity, just like his linguistic\h conception, for one interesting concept: the idea\h\h that objective moral value is inherent to the\h very perspective of the world. This means that,\h\h for him, there are no isolated facts which we can\h look upon without some sort of emotion. And so,\h\h wishing to or not, we attribute morality to\h everything we see. A language may look more\h\h barbaric if its symbols are totally different\h from the ones we’re used to. An unknown person\h\h can look dangerous if dressed in dark clothes\h in the night, whereas you might consider them\h\h friendly in another context, so on and so forth.\h This is an understanding akin to Hume, but also to\h\h contemporary neurological data on our perception.\h Thus, the dichotomy of value and fact, as it is\h\h known, does not exist, as they are but one and the\h same, and we can study values as objectively as we\h\h can study facts, which would impede moral\h discourse from falling to fascist ideals.\h Foot’s “trolley problem” also gained much\h recognition from a multitude of formulations,\h\h to neuroscientific studies, and widespread media\h notoriety. As we saw, the original one is an\h\h example of consequentialism where you can choose\h to flip a switch to make a runaway trolley kill\h\h one person instead of five. To represent Foot’s\h argument within the same context, instead of\h\h the jeep example, the “footbridge” scenario was\h developed, where instead of flipping a switch,\h\h you’d push a person big enough for the trolley\h to derail by killing them – and sure enough,\h\h most people wouldn’t do such a thing, even\h though in consequentialist terms there\h\h wouldn’t be a significant difference. Judith\h Thomson, another virtue theorist who focused\h\h greatly on applied ethics and bioethics,\h and the practical functionality of “good”,\h\h was the one who categorized and expanded Foot’s\h thought experiment, and also developed her own\h\h in defense of abortion, where the reader would be\h asked to imagine if all their vital organs would\h\h be connected with those of a famous violinist\h for nine months in order for him to live,\h\h shifting the debate onto pregnant women instead\h of the fetus. This manner of applied ethics and\h\h bioethics are also interesting novel concepts\h in ethics. Attempting to bring ethics to the\h\h direct applicability of other sciences, such\h as the medical relationship with patients,\h\h ethics in human and animal research and overall\h human rights became increasingly necessary as\h\h the second world war ended and public health\h data improved.

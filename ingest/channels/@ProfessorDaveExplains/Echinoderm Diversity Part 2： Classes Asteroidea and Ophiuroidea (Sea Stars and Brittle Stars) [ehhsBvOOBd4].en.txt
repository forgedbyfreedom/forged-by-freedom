As we discussed previously, the five\h extant echinoderm classes are Crinoidea,\h\h Asteroidea, Ophiuroidea, Echinoidea, and\h Holothuroidea. In the previous tutorial,\h\h we covered the crinoids. In this tutorial\h we will discuss Asteroidea and Ophiuroidea.\h Members of class Asteroidea are commonly\h known as “sea stars” or “starfish.” There are\h\h nearly 2,000 described species of sea stars\h that are found in all the world’s oceans,\h\h from the tropical zones to the polar regions, from\h the intertidal zones to the abyssal depths. They\h\h are perhaps the most familiar of the echinoderms,\h and many are opportunistic predators that feed on\h\h benthic invertebrates. Some of them, like\h the sunflower sea star, feed primarily on\h\h other echinoderms. As predators, they play an\h important ecological role, and areas where they\h\h have been removed have generally suffered due\h to overpopulation of prey species. For example,\h\h the purple sea star and the reef sea star have\h become widely known as examples of keystone\h\h species due to their ability to reduce mussel\h populations, and thus maintain a more biodiverse\h\h ecosystem. They move along the substrate using\h their hydraulic tube feet, and feed by extruding\h\h their stomach through their mouths, thus beginning\h the digestion process outside of the body.\h Sea stars are able to reproduce\h asexually through fragmentation,\h\h as long as part of the basal disk is preserved,\h and also sexually, usually indirectly,\h\h where both sexes release sperm and eggs into the\h water. However, some species do brood young that\h\h burst from the mother. Most species that do not\h give live birth release eggs that hatch into free\h\h swimming bipinnaria larvae, and metamorphosize\h into brachiolaria larvae, which in turn\h\h metamorphosize into sexually immature juveniles. The number of asteroid orders is a bit contested\h\h and was recently revised to include the sea\h daises. As of 2023, there are 3 superorders and 8\h\h orders. But let’s briefly\h go over their diversity as it stands currently.\h Order Velatida contains about 200 species of\h starfish that have thick bodies and large oral\h\h disks. They are most common in the deep sea and\h colder regions. They are often extremely flexible\h\h and have weakly developed skeletons. Meanwhile,\h there are only three described species in order\h\h Peripodida, all of which are known as sea\h daisies that lack intestines and anuses.\h\h Less than one centimeter in diameter, they are\h disk-shaped animals with pentaradial symmetry\h\h and no arms. Members of order Brisingida are filter-feeders\h\h that generally have long arms which always number\h more than five, a small “brittle-star-like” disk,\h\h and are most common in the deep sea. Order\h Forcipulatida includes the common starfish,\h\h which are extremely widespread in the Americas.\h They are some of the most familiar forms of\h\h starfish, with many species inhabiting coastal\h seas and intertidal zones. Next up, species in class Ophiuroidea are called\h\h brittle stars or basket stars. Of the more\h than 2,000 extant species of brittle stars,\h\h more than 1,200 of them are found in deep\h ocean waters. They are some of the most\h\h common animals in some types of benthic marine\h habitats, where they may carpet the floor in\h\h what appears to be a living mass. Though they\h superficially resemble the sea stars, they\h\h are remarkably different in form and function. Unlike the starfish, brittle stars have slender\h\h arms that are sharply set off from the central\h disk. They lack pedicellariae and papulae,\h\h and their ambulacral grooves are closed\h and covered in boney ossicles. They also\h\h have tube feet which lack suckers. So instead of\h tube feet for locomotion, they use their arms,\h\h which are made of a column of articulated\h ossicles, sometimes called vertebrae, which are\h\h connected by muscles and covered in plates. Also\h unlike the starfish, the brittle star madreporite\h\h is located on the oral surface by the mouth,\h which has five movable plates that serve as jaws,\h\h so they do not extrude their stomachs to feed.\h Most species feed on small suspended particles,\h\h or dead or decaying organisms, but some species\h are carnivorous. Since their arms are so slender,\h\h nearly all of the brittle star organs are confined\h to the central disk. They have a sac-like stomach,\h\h no intestine or anus, and indigestible food is\h expelled through the mouth. They also have unique\h\h structures known as bursae that open towards\h the oral surface by genital slits at the base\h\h of the arms. These bursae circulate water for gas\h exchange, and aid in the discharge of sex cells.\h Like the sea stars, the brittle stars\h are capable of extreme regeneration,\h\h and some species, like the six-armed members of\h family Ophiactidae, can split their central disk\h\h in two and reproduce through fission. In most\h species, sexes are separate and fertilization\h\h is external. The fertilized eggs may develop\h to become 8-armed ophiopluteus larvae, which\h\h metamorphosize into juveniles and then adults. The extant members of class Ophiuroidea are split\h\h up into six extant orders, those being Euryalida,\h Ophiurida, Amphilepidida, Ophiacanthida,\h\h Ophioleucida, and Ophioscolecida. Members of order\h Euryalida are commonly called “basket stars”,\h\h and are large species with branching arms, or\h long and curling arms, sometimes called “snake\h\h stars”. Meanwhile, members of the remaining orders\h are often simply referred to as brittle stars, use\h\h bursae for respiration and excretion, have their\h madreporite on the oral surface, and move their\h\h arms by using ball-and-socket joints.

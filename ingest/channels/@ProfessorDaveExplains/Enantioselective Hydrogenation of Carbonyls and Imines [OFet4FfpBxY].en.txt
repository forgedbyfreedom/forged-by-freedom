We just looked at enantioselective hydrogenation\h of olefins, and in order to do this we highlighted\h\h breakthroughs by Knowles and Noyori. But if we\h can hydrogenate carbon-carbon pi bonds, are there\h\h methods for enantioselectively hydrogenating pi\h bonds between carbon and some other element? The\h\h first highly effective catalysts to reduce ketones\h enantioselectively also came from Noyori’s lab.\h\h These catalysts display a new architecture with\h respect to the ones used in olefin hydrogenation\h\h that were discussed in the previous tutorial.\h These Noyori catalysts use the same BINAP or\h\h aryl-substituted BINAP motif, but in this case the\h ruthenium two complex contains a second element\h\h of chirality, a chiral diamine or substituted\h diamine, where R is sometimes a sulfonyl group.\h\h These are truly fantastic catalysts, as they\h afford extremely high levels of optical purity\h\h with simple ketones, approaching 100%. In\h addition, they are extremely stable and can\h\h carry out up to millions of turnovers, making\h them eminently practical. An example of their\h\h application is shown here. In this case just\h one part per million catalyst is sufficient\h\h to afford a quantitative yield of product. Now, upon looking at the structure of the\h\h catalyst carefully and critically, you may have\h thought: isn’t the ruthenium center here a bit too\h\h coordinated with chiral elements? How in the world\h can the substrate bind to the metal with all these\h\h chelating ligands stuck around it? The answer is\h simple and perhaps rather surprising: it doesn’t!\h\h This is an example of outer shell mechanism. An\h inner shell mechanism involves binding of the\h\h substrate to the central metal atom in some steps\h of the catalytic cycle. An outer shell mechanism,\h\h on the other hand, does not involve binding of\h the substrate to the metal center itself, but only\h\h to its outer shell, which means the ligands.\h Let us see how this complex reaction occurs.\h Ruthenium two species A, which we had\h called rather loosely a “catalyst”,\h\h is actually a pre-catalyst. A gets activated\h by molecular hydrogen and a strong base such as\h\h tert-butoxide to yield B, the real catalyst.\h The ruthenium-chlorine bonds get reduced to\h\h ruthenium-hydrogen bonds, forming an\h active dihydride, and the strong base\h\h is able to deprotonate one of the two amine\h moieties, yielding a potassium amide ligand,\h\h still bound to ruthenium. The potassium amide\h moiety is actually where the substrate binds,\h\h instead of attacking the metal. Coordination of\h the oxygen from the carbonyl that is to be reduced\h\h with the potassium salt of the amide makes the\h carbonyl more electrophilic, and able to accept a\h\h hydride moiety from the ruthenium complex, and it\h is likely that in this substrate-catalyst complex\h\h there is an incipient bond of the hydride\h with the carbonyl carbon. One of the two\h\h diastereomeric transition states, corresponding\h to two different orientations of the ketone, is\h\h highly favored over the other on steric grounds,\h so this is the step where the stereoselectivity\h\h is introduced. In order to fully appreciate\h this one must look at 3D models. In species D,\h\h the hydride is fully transferred, and this\h results in a positive charge at the ruthenium\h\h center. The vacant site left empty by the hydride\h is now occupied by a molecule of hydrogen forming\h\h an h2 complex. Finally, the departing alkoxide\h needs to be protonated to become an alcohol. The\h\h proton comes from the hydrogen molecule, which\h essentially splits into a proton and a hydride\h\h through h1 complex E. Finally, the alkoxide is\h fully protonated, leaves as an alcohol, and the\h\h catalyst B is regenerated at the end of the cycle. Of course there are some speculative components\h\h in this mechanism, but its key elements are clear.\h The substrate binds to the ligand and is activated\h\h by it in order to receive a hydride from the\h ruthenium center, and the protonation also comes\h\h from molecular hydrogen through activation by\h ruthenium. The outer-shell mechanism may actually\h\h be a deciding factor in making these catalysts\h so stable. Reaction of transition metal complexes\h\h with the substrates via ligand dissociation and\h substrate association makes transition metal\h\h complexes intrinsically unstable and prone to\h decomposition through insufficient stabilization.\h\h The metal here remains fully coordinated at all\h times and is therefore more stable. Clearly,\h\h not all these factors were planned when\h developing the catalyst, and chemists are\h\h still finding more interesting details as they\h continue to study and apply this chemistry to\h\h synthesize fine chemicals and pharmaceuticals. As with the enantioselective hydrogenation\h\h of olefins, hundreds of modifications of this\h initial catalyst structure have been reported,\h\h so many in fact that it would be futile to\h attempt to list them. Suffice it to say that,\h\h as with olefins, one can usually screen dozens\h of commercially available catalysts and find\h\h one which works for any given substrate. For example, one of the early applications\h\h of Noyori’s technology concerns the synthesis of\h the side chain common to most cholesterol-lowering\h\h agents, like rivastatin. The first chiral alcohol\h moiety was prepared in enantiomerically pure form\h\h by an enantioselective ketone reduction. Once\h that center is established, the second one can be\h\h introduced by simple diastereoselective methods,\h using the stereogenic center just introduced to\h\h direct the formation of the second one. Another exciting facet of these catalysts\h\h is their use in dynamic kinetic resolutions.\h Assume that the ketone to be reduced already\h\h has a stereogenic center, and is a racemate.\h Let us take this substrate, a substituted amino\h\h acid. Since the R-directing Noyori catalyst\h is chiral and controls the stereochemistry at\h\h the newly created stereogenic center, we expect\h that the new chiral center at C3 will have the R\h\h configuration. This will then give a mixture\h of 2S,3R and 2R,3R diastereomers. However,\h\h the two transition states yielding these products\h are diastereomeric, and in principle will have\h\h different free energies. This means that the rate\h constant with which the 2S enantiomer reacts,\h\h k1, is different from the rate constant k2 with\h which the 2R enantiomer reacts. If we are lucky,\h\h one of these two rates is so much faster than\h the other that only one enantiomer reacts,\h\h and the other is left unreacted. This is actually\h what happens here, and the 2S, 3R diastereomer\h\h forms exclusively, whereas the 2R substrate is\h left unreacted. Now we can separate them on the\h\h basis of polarity, given the discrepancy between\h the hydroxyl on the product and the ketone on the\h\h substrate, so we have a pure enantiomer,\h the 2S,3R product, and the 2R substrate,\h\h also enantiomerically pure. This is called\h kinetic resolution, because we are selecting\h\h a chiral catalyst which reacts with only one of\h the two enantiomers of the starting material.\h This is quite exciting, but the yield is only 50%,\h and furthermore one must separate two materials\h\h at the end. It would be much more exciting if\h we could also utilize the “wrong” enantiomer\h\h somehow. We note that the substrate here is\h rather acidic: it is a malonate-type substrate,\h\h which means it has a pKa of around 10 or\h 11. A strong base could equilibrate the two\h\h enantiomers. If the rate of such equilibration\h is much faster than the rate of reduction,\h\h the entire manifold can be directed through the\h faster-reacting 2S enantiomer, and if we are lucky\h\h we can get a 100% yield of an enantiomerically\h and diastereomerically pure product. This is\h\h exactly what happens if we use a small amount\h of a strong base, potassium tert-butoxide, to\h\h equilibrate the substrate. The base is not strong\h enough to epimerize the product, and therefore we\h\h obtain essentially a single diastereomer in a\h ratio of 99:1, which has an enantiomeric purity\h\h of 99%. This is what is called dynamic kinetic\h resolution, which hinges on the ability to\h\h equilibrate the two enantiomers of the substrate\h very quickly by any chemical means one can design,\h\h and not necessarily using a base. Examples of\h this powerful strategy in synthesis are numerous.\h Moving on from ketones, enantioselective\h hydrogenation of imines follows similar\h\h criteria as the ones we just discussed. There are,\h however, some complicating factors. Many imines\h\h hydrolyze readily, and therefore in addition\h to amines one gets alcohols as products. Imines\h\h and especially the products, amines, actually do\h bind the catalyst, but not in a productive way,\h\h and tend therefore to be catalyst poisons. Also,\h imines exist in two alternate configurations,\h\h E and Z, and these may or may not interconvert\h at rates commensurate with the reduction,\h\h as well as react at different rates and show\h different enantioselectivities. Therefore,\h\h this is a field of active research and general\h catalysts have not yet emerged which give\h\h the same exquisite enantioselectivities as\h with the hydrogenation of ketones. However,\h\h effective hydrogenations have been developed and\h applied. Perhaps the most famous and certainly the\h\h one working at the highest volumes is the\h one applied to the herbicide metolachlor.\h The catalytic hydrogenation step took many\h years to develop. It uses iridium as the metal\h\h and achieves a stunning turnover number, or TON,\h of over one million. Each molecule of catalyst\h\h can process 200,000 molecules of substrate per\h hour, which is its turnover frequency, or TOF,\h\h and achieves an enantiomeric purity of almost 90%,\h which is far from perfection but can be considered\h\h adequate. It is reported that over 10,000\h metric tons of metolachlor are produced each\h\h year by this method, and given the obligatory low\h cost of agricultural products, this process must\h\h be highly economical. Enantioselective reduction\h of both ketones and imines are fields of current\h\h intensive research and are finding more and\h more new applications in industry all the time.

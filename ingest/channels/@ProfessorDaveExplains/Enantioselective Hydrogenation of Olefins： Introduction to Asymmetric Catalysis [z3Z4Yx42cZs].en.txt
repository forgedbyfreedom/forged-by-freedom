In the organic chemistry series we learned about\h the concept of chirality and the importance of\h\h stereospecificity when it comes to synthesizing\h organic products with chiral centers. Synthesis\h\h of chiral molecules in enantiomerically pure form\h using only a catalytic amount of chiral material\h\h is a very challenging task. Before the end of\h the 20th century, synthesis of chiral molecules\h\h was often carried out targeting the racemate and\h then painstakingly separating the enantiomers,\h\h or by using a stoichiometric amount of chiral\h inducers, either acting as templates or\h\h resolving agents. Only after 1980 were the\h first catalytic enantioselective reactions\h\h developed. The problem is not that people\h had not thought of the idea before that time,\h\h but rather that the results obtained had\h left a lot to be desired. They used large\h\h amounts of chiral catalysts, or they achieved\h disappointing, impractical enantioselectivities.\h Two major breakthroughs in the 1980s were\h recognized with Nobel prizes in 2001. One of these\h\h breakthroughs was in the area of enantioselective\h alkene hydrogenation. The other, an epoxidation\h\h reaction, will be discussed later in the series.\h The awardees for enantioselective hydrogenation\h\h were American chemist William Knowles, who did\h his work at Monsanto in St. Louis, Missouri, and\h\h Japanese chemist Ryoji Noyori, who is currently a\h professor of chemistry at Nagoya University. To be\h\h able to explain their contributions, we first need\h to explain a few concepts in asymmetric synthesis.\h The first terminology that we need to\h become familiar with is that the alkene\h\h to be hydrogenated has to be prochiral. This\h means that the molecule itself is not chiral,\h\h but the corresponding hydrogenated product is\h a chiral molecule. In other words prochiral\h\h means a molecule that is about to become chiral\h once the intended reaction occurs. For example,\h\h let us take Knowles’s favorite substrate,\h the one which led to his major contribution,\h\h dehydroamino acid A. Upon hydrogenation,\h this will give compound B, which is chiral,\h\h due to the chiral center that was generated\h at one of the carbons being hydrogenated.\h\h This means A is prochiral, or capable of leading\h to a chiral product in a single chemical step.\h There is some more new terminology to learn as\h well. There are two faces of the planar alkene\h\h we were just looking at. The “top” face from\h this vantage point is called the “re” face,\h\h because attack from this face leads to the R\h configuration at the newly created stereogenic\h\h center. The other face, the bottom face from this\h vantage point, is called the “si” face because it\h\h leads to the S configuration once hydrogenated.\h Of course we can easily remember this terminology\h\h because re starts with R and si starts with S. With these terms understood, the question becomes:\h\h How can we preferentially form say, the S\h enantiomer over the R? If you look at structures\h\h C and D, with molecular hydrogen coordinated to\h the pi bond that is to be hydrogenated, you will\h\h see that they have an axis of chirality, and if\h you rotate them and try to superimpose them, you\h\h will find that they are enantiomeric structures.\h This means they will have the same free energy\h\h in a non-chiral environment, and therefore they\h will be populated to A-50:50 proportion and will\h\h give rise to a perfect racemate. However, we\h have seen that hydrogenation of olefins does\h\h not occur by simply adding hydrogen gas, but\h rather a metal dihydride, and we have seen\h\h that in Wilkinson’s catalyst the metal is\h rhodium coordinated to triphenylphosphines.\h\h The actual species coordinating with the olefin\h substrate is a rhodium three species bearing two\h\h triphenylphosphine moieties. The two hydrogens,\h once they have coordinated to the complex,\h\h are added to the olefin in succession, the\h first by hydrometalation, and the second by\h\h reductive elimination with retention of\h configuration at the stereogenic carbon.\h Knowles reasoned that the metal gives us a way\h to introduce chirality, because there are ligands\h\h bound to it. If you can replace triphenylphosphine\h with a chiral ligand, you may obtain some\h\h enantioselectivity. In principle, if you take\h our previous scheme about face selectivity and\h\h replace molecular hydrogen with a rhodium three\h dihydride bearing chiral, enantiopure ligands,\h\h you have two diastereomeric intermediates, which\h now have different free energies due to the\h\h differing degrees of steric repulsion that will\h be introduced upon coordination, and may indeed\h\h lead to some enantiomeric excess of S over R. These concepts\h\h can apply not just to rhodium catalysts, but\h also in general to any other metal-ligand complex\h\h capable of carrying out hydrogenation reactions. Trying to apply this idea, Knowles realized that\h\h phosphines with three different substituents are\h chiral, the fourth substituent being a lone pair,\h\h and if they can be resolved, he would be able\h to test a few and see if the idea worked. Before\h\h continuing, we should quickly comment on the\h configurational stability of tertiary phosphines,\h\h because amines invert rapidly through a\h planar form, and phosphines are analogous\h\h structures. However, for phosphines this barrier\h is much higher than that of amines. For example,\h\h ammonia inverts with an activation energy of only\h 24.2 kilojoules per mole, which translates to only\h\h 5 kilocalories per mole, whereas phosphine\h needs 132 kilojoules per mole. While much of\h\h this difference is due to quantum tunneling\h for ammonia, which is absent in phosphine,\h\h the difference extends to tertiary amines and\h phosphines.

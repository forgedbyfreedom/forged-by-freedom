At this point in our study of archeology, it’s\h time to examine the exciting world of excavation,\h\h which is what many people probably imagine first\h when thinking about archeologists. Excavation is\h\h the main way that archeologists collect artifacts\h and record features, such as those we described\h\h in the previous few tutorials. Excavations can\h range in size, from small-scale digs of a certain\h\h feature, to completely unearthing an entire site.\h Excavations are highly systematic and detailed,\h\h so that other archeologists can look at\h excavation reports and perform analysis\h\h on them without having to physically be there.\h Setting up the the systematic method of excavation\h\h at a site is critical before ground is broken. Archeological sites are excavated using grids.\h\h Grid squares are 1 meter by 1 meter, and they are\h called excavation units. This grid is laid atop an\h\h imaginary plane starting at the highest point of\h the site. If the grid was drawn straight on the\h\h surface, distances would be distorted by terrain.\h A total station is used to map this plane onto a\h\h site. Archeologists are then able to geolocate\h each corner and orient the grid north. Often\h\h string is used with stakes at the corner of each\h unit to denote the grid. This assures each grid is\h\h mapped the same in reference to the other grids. Once the units are mapped and marked,\h\h archeologists can begin excavation. Units\h are excavated in levels, and at each level,\h\h the unit itself is level. Most excavations use\h 10 centimeter levels. Level 1 is often simply\h\h getting the surface to the same level. After\h this, the unit is carefully dug in 10 centimeter\h\h increments. The level in which an artifact was\h found will be recorded as well as its x and y\h\h coordinates in centimeters, and remember that\h each unit is 100 by 100 square centimeters.\h\h The dirt from each layer is also sifted to ensure\h that no small artifacts are missed. Artifacts are\h\h then collected and brought back to laboratories\h for analysis. Features and stratigraphic changes\h\h are also recorded. Munsells are used at each\h level to determine which stratigraphic layer\h\h the level exists in. In the past, each level\h was hand-drawn with artifacts and features\h\h being drawn in. Recently, many archeological\h excavations have begun using photos or 3D\h\h imaging to record levels. An excavation unit can\h be stopped for several reasons. A sterile layer,\h\h or a layer with no archeological remains, was\h reached, bedrock was reached, or a feature was\h\h reached, like the floor of a structure. After\h digging is complete on a unit, the profile,\h\h or z-axis, of the unit is recorded. Like before,\h traditionally this was drawn but photo capture\h\h is becoming more popular. This lets archeologists\h record the stratigraphy of the surrounding units\h\h before they are excavated. At one excavation\h many units are being dug at the same time,\h\h so coordination and organization are critical. We now know everything we need to be able to\h\h read excavation reports. These detail\h an excavation, and provide maps of\h\h sites. Being able to interpret excavation\h reports is critical in understanding the\h\h data archeologists use to make hypotheses about\h the ancient world. One of the first things that Dr. Redmond\h\h does is predict the timeframe of the site. Some\h think you can’t make testable predictions in\h\h archeology, but this is not true. Dr. Redmond\h predicts the site to date from 1200-1500,\h\h based on the typologies of lithics and ceramics\h found at the site, as well as the architectural\h\h styles. Many “alternate historians”\h claim things like “this structure might be much\h\h older than we think it is”. However, if we have\h a reliably dated layer that a feature sits on\h\h top of, the feature can’t be older than that.\h Suppose we have a stone monument. We look for\h\h the lowest layer the feature inhabits to date it.\h If the soils under the feature are undisturbed,\h\h the feature cannot be any older than this layer.\h The builders couldn’t have built the monument on\h\h a soil layer that didn’t exist yet. We might\h find habitation on a site goes back farther\h\h than we thought, but that feature can’t get any\h older. It can be younger than this level, having\h\h been pushed into the ground by the builders,\h but it can’t be older. The layer the features\h\h date to likely has artifacts that fit into the\h typologies of the region. All these artifacts\h\h must also date to the period of construction\h for the feature due to the law of superposition,\h\h a date further strengthened by the serialization\h of the artifacts. Furthermore, the feature itself\h\h is usually associated with a style of feature\h present in many sites, which all corroborate a\h\h date range. It’s with all these lines of data that\h archeologists build evidence-based chronologies\h\h of sites. The excavation report goes more\h in-depth with many other unit profiles,\h\h artifact analysis, and more. The report can then\h be used by other archeologists, perhaps noting\h\h how certain artifact types are found in certain\h areas of the site, or comparing the palisade wall\h\h to another at a similar site. Excavation reports\h are in-depth studies, often requiring an entire\h\h team of trained archeologists to complete. Once\h a site is excavated, it and all the context\h\h it holds are gone forever. All that will be left\h are the archeologist's records.

When watching crime-related shows or courtroom\h dramas, some frequent standout moments will\h\h always be the expert testimony. A scientist is\h called upon to testify against or in favor of\h\h the accused and is confronted with a battery of\h questions by both the prosecutor and the defense.\h\h In this tutorial, we will discuss this topic as\h the last stage of the investigation. Actually,\h\h expert testimony isn’t technically the last stage\h of the investigation, but it acts as a full stop\h\h to all the processing the evidence has undergone\h to this point. This is where the evidence will\h\h genuinely influence justice. As we have learned,\h once the evidence gets analyzed and its results\h\h are interpreted, the forensic scientists at the\h laboratory have to communicate their results to\h\h the investigators, judiciary, and attorneys. The\h issue is that forensic scientists understand the\h\h evidence significantly better than anyone else.\h The scientists possess necessary expertise in\h\h this particular domain, and the same can’t\h be said about the investigator and courtroom\h\h officials. It is due to this expertise that we\h consider the scientist an expert. Let us look\h\h at a quote by Sir Bernard Spilsbury, often hailed\h as the “Father of Forensic Science”- “An expert is\h\h someone knowing more and more about less and less,\h eventually knowing everything about nothing”. From\h\h this quote, we can derive the basic function of an\h expert as well as what the criminal justice system\h\h expects from them. The expert’s basic function is\h to communicate information about the evidence in\h\h the domain of their field. But the most important\h requirement while doing this is that the expert\h\h must be able to convey information in the fewest\h words possible. The testimony would be ineffective\h\h if the expert communicates in highly complex and\h scientific terms which are incomprehensible to\h\h most people. And expertise does not always\h translate to effective communication. When\h\h an expert testifies, it is as though they are a\h teacher, and the people in the courtroom are their\h\h students for a moment. The expert must minimize\h the esoteric terminology, the testimony must be\h\h given in simple language understood by everyone.\h If technical terminology is avoided, it helps the\h\h judiciary and other officials to understand facts\h about the evidence in a clear and concise manner.\h So, in this context of expert testimony, who is\h an expert? Can only a scientist be regarded as an\h\h expert? Do all scientists automatically qualify as\h experts? Who decides what makes an expert and what\h\h doesn’t? Let us answer these questions. But before\h doing that, we should rewind a bit and look upon\h\h history. Calling an expert witness to testify is a\h fairly ancient practice. It was documented that in\h\h ancient Babylonia, midwives were adjudged\h as experts to gauge a woman’s virginity,\h\h fertility, and pregnancy. During the Roman\h empire, midwives, handwriting experts, and\h\h land surveyors were recognized as legal experts.\h However, the codification of experts as witnesses,\h\h the admissibility of their testimony, and\h scientific evidence by formulating rules\h\h and regulations is a rather recent phenomenon in\h the western judicial system, for only the last\h\h 250 years. The first time an expert testified in\h the courtroom to offer opinion on the evidence in\h\h a similar way as we see now was in 1782. A civil\h engineer by the name of John Smeaton was called to\h\h give an opinion on litigation regarding silting of\h Wells Harbour in Norfolk. This case is considered\h\h a landmark because it helped in framing the modern\h rules of expert testimony, where the contextual\h\h expertise of the expert helps in judging a case.\h Now, let us look at Rule 702 of the Federal Rules\h\h of Evidence enacted in 1975 that defines expert\h testimony: “(a) A witness who is qualified as an\h\h expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training,\h or education may testify in the form of an opinion\h\h or otherwise if the expert's scientific,\h technical, or other specialized knowledge\h\h will help the trier of fact to understand the\h evidence or to determine a fact in issue. (b)\h\h Expert scientific testimony is admissible only if\h the court is satisfied that the expert testimony\h\h rests upon reliable scientific principles.” From\h this rule, we can understand that the expert must\h\h be qualified in the topic of the testimony.\h To declare a person as an expert witness,\h\h according to the Federal Rules of Evidence, the\h court must be satisfied that the said professional\h\h has specialized education, training, or practical\h experience in the subject matter relating to the\h\h case. There’s no mention in this rule that the\h expert witness should necessarily be a scientist.\h An expert can be anyone who’s not a scientist\h but just a professional: a school bus driver,\h\h a door maker, a blacksmith, or even a scuba diver.\h The only catch here is that these professionals\h\h must be declared as an expert by the court of\h law. Even a forensic scientist is not an expert\h\h by default unless they are declared as such by\h the court. In a similar manner, even a shoemaker\h\h is an expert if they have been declared as an\h expert by the court. Whoever the expert may be,\h\h from a forensic scientist to a shoemaker, the\h testimony should be firmly based on scientific\h\h principles as mentioned in point (b) of Rule\h 702. The expert should only offer an opinion\h\h on how the actus reus, or guilty act of the\h defendant resulted or did not result in the\h\h manifestation of the evidence. They should not\h pass any loaded opinion towards the acquittal\h\h or conviction of the accused due to some vested\h interest. A court often has an independent expert,\h\h but the defense counsel or the prosecutor can\h still refer to a separate expert for a second\h\h opinion on the evidence. Such experts are\h frequently coaxed to testify in favor of the\h\h litigating party, thus making it a very stressful\h job for them. Sometimes, during cross-examination,\h\h lawyers do ask many uncomfortable questions of\h the expert, doubting their integrity, credibility,\h\h and qualification. Therefore, the expert must stay\h focused on the facts related to their expertise,\h\h and handle the stressful questions\h without hampering the testimony. However,\h\h with growing experience over several cases,\h the expert becomes skilled in handling such\h\h situations and becomes an “expert” in the\h experience of delivering expert testimony.\h Lawyers often try to put the expert witness\h who has analyzed the evidence in a spot by\h\h interrogating about its chain of custody. Chain\h of custody is an important factor for ensuring the\h\h admissibility of evidence in the court. We have\h already discussed how evidence is processed from\h\h its recognition to laboratory analysis, and\h between these stages, the evidence is not in\h\h the possession of a single person, like if it\h gets transferred from the investigator to the\h\h laboratory. Within the laboratory, a single piece\h of evidence might have to be analyzed in different\h\h divisions. For example, a bloodstained revolver is\h collected by the investigator. They send it to the\h\h laboratory for analysis. In the laboratory, this\h revolver is sent to the fingerprints division,\h\h then to the biology and serology division, and\h at last to the ballistics division. This change\h\h in custody needs to be documented each time\h the evidence is transferred from one person to\h\h another. This process of documented transfer\h of evidence from one person to another is\h\h called chain of custody. A chain of custody form\h contains information including the description of\h\h the evidence, the time and date of release for\h all prior custodians of the item, and the time\h\h and date of release to the witness. If there\h are any minute discrepancies in documentation,\h\h the evidence is regarded as tainted and\h the chain of custody declared broken,\h\h making the evidence inadmissible. Even if the\h evidence stays idle in the evidence room and this\h\h period is not documented in the chain of custody;\h it can be considered inadmissible. Therefore,\h\h the expert needs to ensure that the chain of\h custody is maintained so that evidence gets\h\h authenticated, proving it is the same thing\h that was collected from the crime scene.\h With the continually developing knowledge in\h different domains of sciences post-industrial\h\h revolution, there needed to be established certain\h standards on what constitutes scientific evidence\h\h and what does not. These standards were necessary\h because when the literature regarding a topic\h\h is not well developed, there exists a very\h thin line between pseudoscience and actual\h\h science. The point is that a theory becomes\h acceptable as science only when the scientific\h\h community accepts it. Due to the scientific and\h technological boom in the last few centuries,\h\h many scientific techniques were developed\h and utilized in crime investigation. However,\h\h many techniques of analysis that are well accepted\h by the scientific community today were rejected by\h\h the scientific community back then, citing a lack\h of enough research. This was when the court felt\h\h the need to put forth a standard for admissibility\h of evidence. In 1923, in Frye vs the United\h\h States, the results of a polygraph lie detector\h were admitted as evidence in the trial. Though\h\h polygraph is well accepted by the scientific\h community as a means to detect deception today,\h\h back then it was heavily resented. Therefore, the\h District of Columbia court rejected the scientific\h\h validity of polygraph. The court wrote that “Just\h when a scientific principle or discovery crosses\h\h the line between the experimental and demonstrable\h stages is difficult to define. Somewhere in\h\h this twilight zone, the evidential force of the\h principle must be recognized, and while the courts\h\h will go a long way in admitting experimental\h testimony deduced from a well-recognized\h\h scientific principle or discovery, the thing from\h which the deduction is made must be sufficiently\h\h established to have gained general acceptance\h in the particular field in which it belongs”.\h\h Therefore, expert testimony must not only be based\h on scientific methods, but these methods should\h\h also be sufficiently established and accepted\h by the scientific community. This standard\h\h for admissibility of evidence came to be known as\h The Frye standard or The General Acceptance Test.\h\h Because of this, the court can consult different\h subject matter experts, or refer to books and\h\h research papers extensively before determining if\h the expert testimony meets the standard or not.\h The Frye standard was commonly followed by all\h jurisdictions for a long period, when in 1975,\h\h the Federal Rules of Evidence were enacted. As\h we saw earlier, Rule 702 of FRE defines expert\h\h testimony. There are two major points\h in this rule: one defines an expert,\h\h and the other states that the testimony should\h be based on reliable scientific techniques. In\h\h this rule, nothing is mentioned about the general\h acceptability of scientific techniques. Therefore,\h\h the courts were confused regarding how\h to determine if the FREs were enacted\h\h to support the Frye standard or to completely\h supersede it, ignoring general acceptability.\h\h To clear this confusion, the court felt the\h need to establish a different standard for\h\h admissibility of evidence. In Daubert vs Merrel\h Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., the Supreme Court\h\h held that the FRE implicitly overturned the Frye\h standard. The court also laid down a different\h\h standard for admissibility of evidence, which\h is now referred to as the Daubert standard. It\h\h provides four factors for the court to determine\h if the expert testimony is admissible or not.\h\h "Whether the expert’s theory or technique\h can be (and has been) tested. Whether the\h\h theory or technique has an acceptable known\h or potential rate of error. The existence\h\h and maintenance of standards controlling the\h technique’s operation. Whether the theory or\h\h technique has attained 'general acceptance”.\h Though the Daubert standard may not seem much\h\h different than the Frye standard, the fact we must\h remember here is that the main issue at hand was\h\h to clarify the confusion arising from Frye and\h FRE and not to form a new standard.

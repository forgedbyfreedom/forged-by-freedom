But first let’s learn one\h\h additional topic that will be of relevance. We\h must understand hard and soft acids and bases,\h\h and how they pertain to transition metals. We certainly will recall acid-base reactions\h\h from our study of general chemistry, and we are\h certainly aware of the Bronsted-Lowry definition\h\h for acidity and basicity that deals with proton\h exchange. But we should also remember the Lewis\h\h model of acidity and basicity, where a Lewis base\h is an electron-pair donor, and a Lewis acid is an\h\h electron-pair acceptor. This definition will be\h relevant for us because transition metal centers\h\h often act as Lewis acids, and ligands act as\h Lewis bases when they coordinate to the metal\h\h centers. So in the context of this tutorial, when\h we talk about acids we are referring to transition\h\h metal ions, and bases will be the ligands that\h coordinate to them. In order to be able to predict\h\h the strength of metal-ligand interactions, we\h need to understand the concept of hard and soft.\h When we say that an acid or base is “soft”, we\h are referring to its polarizability. A highly\h\h polarizable atom or molecule is very soft. This\h means that large metal ions are soft acids. They\h\h have large electron clouds that can exhibit\h large momentary or induced dipoles and are\h\h thus more polarizable. Smaller metal ions are hard\h acids. We can say the same for a base. If a lone\h\h pair or negative charge is highly localized on a\h singular, small atom, this would be a hard base.\h\h If the charge is diffuse, distributed around\h a larger molecular volume or an atom with\h\h a large radius, this would be a soft base. Hard acids have a high charge density and form\h\h bonds that are more ionic in nature. The greater\h the charge on a metal ion, the harder it is,\h\h and the smaller the radius of the metal ion is,\h the harder it is. Early transition metals with\h\h valence electrons in the 3d subshell are hard\h acids. Hard bases are small anions and neutral\h\h molecules with lone pairs. Bases derived from\h electronegative elements in the n=2 shell will\h\h be hard bases, like those of nitrogen, oxygen,\h and fluorine. This means that fluoride anions,\h\h any kind of oxygen anion like an oxide or a\h hydroxide, and even neutral nitrogen atoms in\h\h ammonia or other amines are classified as hard. Soft acids have a lower charge density and form\h\h bonds that are more covalent in nature. Late\h 4d and 5d transition metals tend to be soft\h\h due to their larger size and polarizability.\h These usually have charges of only 1+ or 2+.\h\h Soft bases are larger anions and neutral\h molecules, which are more polarizable than their\h\h hard counterparts. For example, while fluoride\h is hard, iodide is very soft, and the softness\h\h increases going down the halogen group, as radius\h increases. Likewise, while an oxide ion is hard,\h\h a sulfide ion is much softer, again due to\h the increased radius and polarizability.\h There are also plenty of species that qualify\h as having intermediate hardness or softness,\h\h not leaning heavily in either direction.\h These have intermediate size, charge density,\h\h and polarizability. This is not always easy to\h predict, as even different oxidation states of\h\h the same element can differ dramatically,\h so it is best to just learn through example\h\h as we go through the series.

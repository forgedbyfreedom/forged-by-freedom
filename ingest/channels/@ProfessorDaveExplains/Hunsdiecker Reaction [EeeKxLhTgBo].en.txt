Several of the name reactions we\h have investigated in this series\h\h have involved functional group\h interconversion, and in this\h\h tutorial we are going to learn another. This\h particular interconversion is quite challenging,\h\h it is the conversion of a carboxylic acid into\h an alkyl halide, usually a bromide or iodide,\h\h involving the loss of one carbon atom, which is\h eliminated as CO2. It has been appreciated by\h\h chemists for a long time that carboxylic acids\h are plentiful in nature, such as fatty acids,\h\h whereas alkyl halides are not, and therefore\h such a conversion would be quite valuable.\h The method of choice for this transformation is\h the one devised by the German husband-and-wife\h\h team of Heinz and Cläre Hunsdiecker. They patented\h their work in 1939, and published it in 1942 in\h\h the Chemische Berichte. It consists of heating\h a silver carboxylate in the presence of bromine,\h\h or less commonly, iodine. This leads to the alkyl\h halide with the accompanying decarboxylation.\h The mechanism of the reaction is quite\h interesting, and is known to involve free\h\h radicals. First of all, the silver carboxylate\h must be made, which can be prepared by mixing\h\h the carboxylic acid with silver oxide. Removal of\h water leads to the isolation of the silver salt.\h\h These salts must be handled in the dark because\h they are very photosensitive, leading to silver\h\h metal. Indeed, this “photoreduction” chemistry was\h the basis for the first applications of black and\h\h white photography using silver bromide. Silver\h carboxylates react with halogens like bromine\h\h to form acyl hypohalites, in this case the acyl\h hypobromite. The driving force for the reaction\h\h is provided by the formation of silver bromide,\h AgBr, which is highly insoluble and precipitates.\h Now, given that oxygen-bromine bonds are quite\h weak, these acyl hypobromites tend to break.\h\h However, both oxygen and bromine are highly\h electronegative, and neither atom wants to\h\h give up a pair of electrons, which is the case\h in many polar dissociations. What happens instead\h\h is that they split the pair evenly, each ending\h with 7 electrons in their outer shells, and are\h\h therefore both radicals. Carboxylate radicals\h slowly decarboxylate in the following manner,\h\h thereby producing an alkyl radical. So if\h the carboxylate and bromine radicals were to\h\h recombine, they would form the starting material,\h but if CO2 has been lost, we are left with this\h\h other radical pair which can instead recombine to\h form the alkyl halide. The reaction can produce\h\h primary, secondary, or tertiary alkyl halides,\h and also aryl bromides, via this mechanism.\h\h In the latter case, the potential side reaction\h is electrophilic bromination of the benzene ring,\h\h especially if the aromatic\h ring is very electron rich.\h\h The reaction in general works best for bromides,\h less well for iodides, and poorly for chlorides.\h Looking again at the more generalized reaction, a\h common side reaction is the recombination of the\h\h alkyl and carboxyl radicals to form an ester,\h whereby two equivalents of the original alkyl\h\h groups are combined. This may be a useful\h product, and it is sometimes referred to as\h\h the Simonini reaction, but in most\h applications it is a nuisance,\h\h so it is important to use a slight excess of\h bromine so as to minimize formation of the ester.\h From a practical standpoint, the\h Hunsdiecker reaction has not found many\h\h industrial applications and it\h remains primarily a lab tool.\h\h One of the problems when scaling up is the cost\h of silver salts and the stoichiometric amount of\h\h silver bromide waste produced. In addition,\h bromine is a corrosive and toxic liquid,\h\h and is therefore not an attractive\h reagent for large scale operations.\h However, there have been many modifications of\h this decarboxylative bromination. Some methods use\h\h even more toxic salts than silver, like mercury\h or lead carboxylates. These alternatives are even\h\h less practical than the original Hunsdiecker\h protocol. However, some procedures use only\h\h catalytic amounts of silver salts, and use alkyl\h hypohalites as oxidizing or halogenating agents.\h\h As an example, let’s highlight the\h work of Professor Chaozhong Li,\h\h from the Shanghai Institute of Organic\h Chemistry, published a few years ago in\h\h the Journal of the American Chemical Society. His method involves the use of tert-butyl\h\h hypochlorite instead of bromine as\h the halogenating agent, and therefore\h\h forms tert-butanol as a co-product, plus the alkyl\h chloride. The silver salt, a complex of silver\h\h trifluoromethanesulfonate and phenanthroline, does\h not precipitate out as silver chloride, but simply\h\h promotes the reaction without being consumed.\h It is therefore a catalyst, and it can be used\h\h in only 5 mole percent. This procedure is quite\h general for alkyl chlorides, though it is not yet\h\h known whether it can be extended to bromides and\h iodides. The mechanism is also not understood yet,\h\h as the reaction is quite new, but the method sets\h the stage for the use of catalytic silver, and\h\h therefore hopefully more practical applications.\h Thus, the Hunsdiecker reaction is still being\h\h studied and extended, and is more than ever\h an important tool in modern organic synthesis.

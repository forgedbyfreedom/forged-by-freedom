Before we can talk about how the mind works, we\h need to understand how the mind gets information.\h\h Sensation, or how we acquire information\h about the world through our senses,\h\h is one of the building blocks of\h psychology. Perception then steps in,\h\h making sense of the information received through\h sensation and turning it into something useful.\h We talked a lot about how sensation\h works in our biopsychology series,\h\h so head back to some of those tutorials if you\h need a refresher. Otherwise put very briefly,\h\h here’s what you need to know about sensation\h for this psychology course. We get basic\h\h sensory information through our senses, those\h being taste, touch, smell, hearing, and vision.\h\h This information comes in the form of things like\h light waves, sound waves, and molecular entities.\h\h The brain then changes, or transduces, this raw\h information into information that we can perceive.\h\h Perception, then, is just the brain putting\h sensory information together to represent the\h\h external world. It’s these mental representations\h that we use to go through our daily life.\h Now, there is a bunch of information out there\h in the world that our senses are picking up.\h\h In order to not get overwhelmed, we actually don’t\h perceive everything that we could at any given\h\h time. Here’s an example, think about your nose.\h Your nose is always in your field of vision, but\h\h our brains ignore it, so we do not perceive it.\h The visual information of your nose is a sensation\h\h that our brain filters out because it’s not\h information that we need on a day-to-day basis.\h So how does the brain decide what is necessary\h information and what is unnecessary? The mind\h\h uses a number of tricks and shortcuts to process\h all the incoming sensory information efficiently.\h\h Take visual perception, for example. Our minds\h automatically assume that things in shadow\h\h are lighter than they actually\h are. As a demonstration,\h\h look at this checker shadow illusion. Square A is not in any shadow,\h\h while square B is in the shadow of the cylinder.\h It looks like square B is a lighter shade of gray\h\h than square A. But it turns out that they are\h actually the same color. Your mind is perceiving\h\h square B as lighter because we automatically\h assume things in shadows are lighter than they\h\h actually are. But if we connect the two squares\h with a rectangle of the same color, we see that\h\h square A and square B are really the same.\h Our minds are easily fooled by this illusion.\h Another set of rules the mind uses are called\h “Gestalt laws of grouping” or “principles of\h\h grouping.” These rules are a set of heuristics,\h or short-cuts, that the mind uses to group small\h\h objects into larger ones, and they play an\h important role in our visual perception.\h\h The principles are: proximity, similarity,\h closure, good continuation, common fate,\h\h and good form. Let’s take a moment to look at each\h of these more closely. The principle of proximity\h\h states that when objects are close together, the\h mind sees them as a single group or object. This\h\h happens even when the objects differ in shape,\h size, and color, even if they don’t actually\h\h touch each other. As long as they are close to one\h another, the mind makes them one cohesive object.\h\h The principle of similarity states that we\h perceive objects which are physically similar\h\h to be parts of the same object. This lets us\h differentiate between adjacent and overlapping\h\h objects based on their physical characteristics.\h For example, if there was a large field where\h\h multiple different crops grew side-by-side, your\h mind would probably group similar crops together\h\h based on their color and texture. There would be\h a “corn group” next to a “cabbage group” next to a\h\h “wheat group” and so on. The principle of closure\h states that our minds tend to complete figures\h\h even if the picture is incomplete or partially\h hidden by other objects. Look at this panda in\h\h the World Wildlife Fund logo. There are no lines\h connecting its ears to the top of its head.\h\h Even so, your mind looks at that image and\h completes the circle of the panda’s head.\h\h If a shape’s border is missing, we tend to see the\h shape as complete and ignore the gaps entirely.\h\h This tendency allows us to recognize\h patterns and fill in missing information,\h\h a skill that would be important if, say, there was\h a partially concealed predator nearby. We would\h\h be able to recognize it as a predator even if we\h didn’t see the whole thing, and react accordingly.\h\h The next principle is good continuation.\h When objects intersect with each other,\h\h we perceive them as two separate objects rather\h than one single object. This lets us differentiate\h\h between objects as separate entities, even\h if they overlap. When objects intersect,\h\h we tend to continue the contours of each object\h when they have established an implied direction.\h\h The next principle, the principle of common fate,\h states that we perceive visual stimulus as part\h\h of the same overall object when they are moving in\h the same direction. Imagine you see a large flock\h\h of birds. Even though there are many birds, they\h are seen together as a single object, a flock,\h\h because they are all moving in the same direction\h and at the same speed. Even if two flocks crossed\h\h paths, we would see them as separate objects\h because each flock would have a unique direction\h\h and speed. And the final principle we use in\h visual perception is the principle of good\h\h form. This is our tendency to perceive objects\h in the simplest way possible. Think of the\h\h Olympic symbol. We see it as overlapping circles\h instead of a bunch of curved, connected lines.\h The principles of grouping represent just one of\h the many ways our minds take visual information\h\h to create a mental picture of the world. Remember,\h the main goal of sensation and perception\h\h is to give us an internal representation of the\h world around us. But how accurate and objective\h\h is this representation? As it turns out, not\h as perfect and accurate as one might think!\h\h We are susceptible to a variety of visual\h illusions, often due to visual context and\h\h expectations. Let’s take a look at the\h classic illusion, the Shepard’s Table.\h\h In this illusion, there are two tables, a long\h and skinny one and a fatter, almost square one.\h\h The tops of these tables are actually constructed\h with two parallelograms of the exact same size!\h\h Because they are shown to us as tabletops with\h legs, our brains automatically interpret them as\h\h objects in three-dimensional space. The “long and\h narrow” table thus looks long and narrow because\h\h we see it as receding into the distance. If we\h took off the table legs, we would stop perceiving\h\h them as three-dimensional objects and our minds\h would see that they are indeed the same size.\h\h We can even prove that to ourselves by overlaying\h this solid on one shape and then the other.\h Our expectations also impact our visual\h perception. Imagine you are going for a\h\h walk in a forest where there have been many\h moose sightings. You see what appears to be\h\h a moose behind a large tree, and change directions\h so you don’t disturb the moose. As you hike away,\h\h though, you realize that the moose is actually\h just two broken tree stumps. Because of the\h\h Gestalt law of continuation, you perceived these\h two shapes as a continuous object. Then, because\h\h you were walking in a moose-laden land, you\h expected to see a moose, so that’s what you saw.\h In addition to being affected by visual context\h and expectations, our perception of things can\h\h actually be affected by our beliefs, desires, and\h even culture. Language, for example, can impact\h\h how we see colors. Language relativism, also known\h as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, is the idea that\h\h the language we speak constrains our perception\h and cognition. So if your language doesn’t have a\h\h word for the color green, you don’t see the color\h green. Now, this isn’t as crazy as it sounds. We\h\h do have basic universals in perception regardless\h of language. Infants and individuals from cultures\h\h with only terms for light and dark are able to\h tell the difference between basic colors like red,\h\h green, blue, and yellow. However, linguistic\h color categories, or how your language names\h\h and recognizes colors, does shape how readily\h we are able to see and discriminate between\h\h colors. Take Russian, for example. Things aren’t\h simply blue in Russian. They are either dark blue\h\h (siniy) or light blue (goluboy). Russian speakers\h are better at seeing a light blue triangle against\h\h a dark blue background, and vice versa, than they\h are at identifying green triangles against green\h\h backgrounds. German speakers, who do not have\h dedicated words for light blue and dark blue,\h\h are no better at seeing blue triangles than green\h ones. This suggests that simply having distinct\h\h words for light blue and dark blue changes\h Russian speakers’ perception when confronted\h\h with shades of blue. Your language shapes how\h you perceive and discriminate between colors.\h Your daily life experience can also impact your\h perception. A study done in 2010 by Balcetis and\h\h Dunning looked at how desire impacts perception\h by manipulating how thirsty participants were.\h\h Participants were in either the thirsty group,\h where they ate a bowl of pretzels, or in the\h\h quenched group, where they drank up to four\h glasses of water. Each participant was then asked\h\h how far away a glass of water was. The quenched\h group thought the water was significantly farther\h\h away than the thirsty group did. The desire of\h wanting water changed the perception of distance.\h This result has been repeated in multiple studies,\h looking at things like probability of winning,\h\h physical tiredness, and priming with\h ideas, and their effect on perception.\h\h When participants are shown a $100 bill, they see\h it as closer to themselves if they are told they\h\h have a chance to win it, instead of being\h told that it belongs to the experimenter.\h\h Jogging for an hour or wearing a heavy backpack\h both increase the perceived steepness of a hill.\h\h Thinking about fast animals like cheetahs or\h slow animals like turtles changes estimates\h\h of a man’s walking speed. Interpretations\h of an ambiguous figure that can be seen\h\h as a woman’s face or as a man playing a saxophone\h depend on whether perceivers have been primed,\h\h or talked to beforehand, with the\h concepts of ‘flirtation’ or ‘music’.\h So, is perception as perfect as we may\h think? Not at all. Our motivations,\h\h beliefs, and desires can shape the basic\h processes involved in the way we attend to,\h\h remember, and encode the world around us, infusing\h bias at times. Psychological studies can show us\h\h when and how these biases creep in. But never\h fear! At a very basic level, our perceptions\h\h are reliable enough to give us a fairly\h accurate representation of the world around us.

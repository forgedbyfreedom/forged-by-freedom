In the previous tutorial, we learned how to\h identify a fingerprint pattern. Identification\h\h of a fingerprint by categorizing their ridge\h flow into different patterns such as arches,\h\h loops, and whorls are known as Level-1 details of\h a fingerprint. However, to identify an individual\h\h from a given fingerprint impression, the mere\h classification of the pattern is not enough.\h\h The fingerprint needs to have a sufficient number\h of matching ridge characteristics. These ridge\h\h characteristics are known as Level-2 details.\h Individual ridges on our palms and soles are not\h\h continuous lines but are characterized by certain\h breaks and interruptions. Ridges can also diverge\h\h or bifurcate into two branches. These phenomena\h give rise to different types of ridge details.\h\h Such details are minute and can be observed\h clearly only with the help of a magnifying aid.\h\h This is why these details are also called\h minutiae. When we learned about the history\h\h of fingerprint science, we saw that it was Dr.\h Henry Faulds who proposed that fingerprints can\h\h be used for criminal identification. But he did\h not venture deep into studying their individual\h\h characteristics. His comparison was limited to a\h rough classification. Someone without a keen eye\h\h might just look at two whorl patterns and identify\h them as being the same. But the fact remains that\h\h each fingerprint is a marvelously complex pattern.\h It was Francis Galton, who was not only the first\h\h person to specify fingerprints into a limited\h number of patterns, but also to stress that\h\h identification can be more certain if the\h minutiae of a fingerprint are established.\h\h Galton was the first to determine that the\h ridges of a fingerprint are not continuous.\h\h He theorized that the lack of continuity\h in ridges can be utilized to differentiate\h\h between two fingerprints of the same pattern by\h identifying the ridge details. To commemorate\h\h his contribution to fingerprint science,\h these details are known as Galton details.\h Galton details can be majorly classified into\h the following types. One: ridge ending. The\h\h point where a ridge ends abruptly. This\h is the most commonly observed detail in a\h\h fingerprint impression. Two: bifurcation. Here,\h a ridge splits into two arms. Due to its shape,\h\h it is also known as a diverging fork. Bifurcations\h are abundantly observed in a fingerprint. Three:\h\h trifurcation. This is similar to bifurcation,\h the only difference being that in this case,\h\h the ridge splits into three ridges. However, this\h detail is rarely observed in an impression. Four:\h\h interjunction. This is a small ridge that connects\h two parallel ridges, thus acting as a bridge. It\h\h is also known as a crossover. Five: fragment.\h As the name implies, this detail is a small\h\h ridge lying in the space between two ridges.\h It is not connected to any of the ridges. Six:\h\h lake. This detail is formed when a ridge branches\h off and joins the parent ridge again, forming an\h\h enclosure of elliptical shape. Seven: island. This\h is just a dot lying in the fingerprint pattern.\h\h An island and a fragment should not be confused\h because the latter looks more like a short ridge.\h\h Eight: intersection. Also known as changeover,\h this detail is observed where two ridges cross\h\h each other’s paths and travel independently\h without ending. Nine: return. This detail consists\h\h of a ridge abruptly changing its path and taking\h a U-turn. And ten: hook. Also known as a spur,\h\h this detail is observed where a small ridge curves\h into a hook-like shape. The ridge characteristics\h\h on a fingerprint impression can be denoted by\h these names. One thing to note here is that each\h\h of these details can be classified primarily as\h either a ridge ending or a bifurcation. Just have\h\h a look at some of these. What is a lake, a hook,\h an interjunction, or an intersection? All of them\h\h are fundamentally bifurcations. Similarly,\h each of these details has a ridge ending.\h Hundreds of such details can be observed in\h every fingerprint, but their location and\h\h their sequence matter the most in comparison.\h However, identifying all of the details in a\h\h single fingerprint impression is a very tedious\h job. Is it even necessary to locate all these\h\h ridge characteristics for comparison between two\h fingerprints? Let’s say we have to compare the\h\h facial features of two people for identification.\h The first thing we might notice is that one of\h\h them has a deep permanent scar on their face while\h the other does not. Also, their eye colors are\h\h different. Now, should we keep on comparing other\h features like their nose, ear, and lip dimensions\h\h as well? Because the earlier two features were\h strikingly different, we don’t need to establish\h\h a relationship between the other features.\h Similarly, comparing each ridge characteristic\h\h is not essential to confirm identification. From\h the hundreds of details present in an impression,\h\h identification of only a limited number of details\h is sufficient. Nowadays it is exceptionally easy\h\h to upload an impression into an automated system\h that recognizes all of the details present in it.\h\h However, this was not the case in the budding\h years of fingerprint science when everything\h\h was to be done manually. This is the reason why\h there was a need to put a limit on the number of\h\h details needed for identification. Here enters one\h of the most popular figures in forensic science,\h\h the Sherlock Holmes of France, also known\h as the father of modern forensic science,\h\h Dr. Edmond Locard, who we discussed earlier in the\h series when we learned about the law of exchange.\h In 1914, Dr. Edmond Locard put forth the\h tripartite rule concerning fingerprint\h\h identification, aimed at establishing\h a standard of proof. Locard studied and\h\h compared a vast number of fingerprints and laid\h out his assumptions in the following manner: “(1)\h\h The certainty of identity becomes beyond debate,\h if more than 12 concurring points are existing.\h\h (2) If 8 to 12 concurring points are present,\h then the case is marginal and the inevitability\h\h of individuality will depend on: a) The clarity\h of the fingerprints; b) The rarity of its form;\h\h c) The presence of the core and the delta in the\h available part of the print; d) The presence of\h\h pores; e) The papillary ridges and valleys,\h the direction of the lines, and the angular\h\h value of the junctions. (3) If lesser number of\h characteristics are found, then the fingerprints\h\h will not provide identification but only an\h assumption to the number of points available and\h\h their clarity.” From this rule, we can understand\h that 12 matching details is the threshold value\h\h for identification. So if more than 12 details are\h matching in two fingerprint impressions, they must\h\h be one hundred percent identical. If 8-12 matching\h details are present, then the identification will\h\h depend on several different factors, including\h clarity of the impression, and the extensive\h\h study of the ridges and the pores present in the\h impression.

To\h understand how antihistamines alleviate allergies,\h\h we must understand more about histamine,\h which is this nitrogenous compound here.\h Within the immune system, histamine is\h released by mast cells in our tissues,\h\h and basophils in our blood, which are cells we\h described in detail in the immunology series.\h\h This process initiates an inflammatory cascade\h and a series of effects mediated primarily through\h\h the H1 histamine receptor. It is worth noting\h at this point; H2, H3 and H4 receptor isotypes\h\h have also been documented, with H2’s physiological\h function much better understood than the other\h\h two. Although H2 mediates some immunological\h functions, H2’s primary physiological function\h\h is to aid gastrointestinal motility and\h promote gastric secretion. To remain in\h\h the context of allergies, we will focus on\h H1-related antihistamines in this tutorial.\h Through the H1 receptor, histamine dilates blood\h vessels and constricts the cells lining these\h\h vessel walls, causing increased blood flow to an\h affected area and allowing immune cells from the\h\h blood to escape the vessels and enter inflamed\h tissues. This can lead to the swelling and\h\h sensation of warmth in tissues, the presence of\h hives in the skin, and can also cause fluid to be\h\h expelled in the sinuses, leading to a runny nose,\h teary eyes, and sneezing. Histamine can also act\h\h locally on nerve terminals, causing an itching or\h painful sensation. Airways constrict in response\h\h to histamine, and the contractile force of the\h heart can be increased as well. Most importantly,\h\h histamine release initiates an immune response\h where other immune cells are recruited, additional\h\h pro-inflammatory molecules are released, and\h inflammation is exacerbated. Antihistamines help\h\h prevent this cascade from occurring, so let’s\h discuss the mechanism by which this occurs.\h Antihistamines do not prevent the release of\h histamine, but rather inhibit its pro-inflammatory\h\h effects by acting as inverse agonists of the H1\h receptor. This works as follows. The H1 receptor\h\h has a high level of basal activity, which means\h it can undergo spontaneous conformational changes\h\h towards active receptor conformations even in the\h absence of histamine. Antihistamines can not only\h\h block the effects of histamine through receptor\h antagonism, preventing histamine from binding,\h\h but also through inverse agonism, suppressing or\h decreasing the basal activity of the receptor by\h\h stabilizing the receptor’s inactive conformation,\h thereby reducing the degree of spontaneous\h\h activation. Antihistamines are thus efficacious\h in relieving allergy related symptoms, but because\h\h inflammation begins with histamine release, and\h is followed by additional inflammatory signals,\h\h treatment is most efficacious when used\h prophylactically, meaning in a preventive manner.\h\h In cases of significant histamine release leading\h to anaphylaxis, which is a severe and potentially\h\h life-threatening allergic reaction, antihistamine\h treatment is not sufficient. In such a case,\h\h exogenous adrenaline must be used to rapidly\h counteract the airway constriction and hypotension\h\h induced by histamine. Adrenaline and the receptors\h adrenaline interact with are of great importance\h\h and will be covered in a future tutorial.

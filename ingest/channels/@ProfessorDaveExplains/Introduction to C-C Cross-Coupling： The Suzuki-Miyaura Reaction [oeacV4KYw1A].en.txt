Now it’s time to move on to an extremely important\h topic in organometallic chemistry. Cross-coupling\h\h reactions forming C-C bonds with transition-metal\h catalysts have been known since the 1940s through\h\h the work of Morris Kharasch, a Ukrainian-born\h chemist working at the University of Chicago.\h\h Among his many contributions to chemistry, he\h noticed that aryl Grignard reagents give fair\h\h yields of biaryls when coupled with aryl bromides\h in the presence of trace amounts of transition\h\h metals. It was not until the 70s, however, that\h the modern cross-coupling reaction was born,\h\h using well defined palladium homogeneous catalysts\h like palladium tetrakis triphenylphosphine. Now\h\h these reactions are one of the most important\h tools in the synthesis of fine chemicals and\h\h pharmaceuticals, and have been used on ton scale. Usually, a pre-catalyst is used,\h\h largely because Pd(0) complexes are\h air sensitive and difficult to use,\h\h whereas Pd(II) complexes are generally stable and\h not air sensitive. Moreover, Pd(II) complexes are\h\h easily reduced in situ to Pd(0) complexes, which\h then enter the catalytic cycle. The cycle is\h\h predicated on the ability of palladium complexes\h to easily alternate between two oxidation states,\h\h 0 and +2. Thus, Pd(0) complexes usually undergo\h oxidative addition with alkenyl or aryl halides,\h\h especially iodides and bromides, to yield an\h intermediate R-Pd(II)-X complex. The halide\h\h is usually sp2 hybridized, although rare examples\h of sp3 halides exist and will be mentioned later.\h\h The halide can also be chloride, but this\h requires special ligands, and even fluoride,\h\h although this requires alternative metals. The oxidative addition step is common to all\h\h cross-couplings, but the transmetalation\h step can occur with a variety of metals,\h\h as we will see later. This is a nucleophilic\h attack of an organometallic reagent onto Pd(II)\h\h and replaces the palladium-halide bond with\h a palladium-carbon bond. The R-Pd-R’ species\h\h then undergoes reductive elimination to generate\h the cross-coupling product as well as the initial\h\h catalyst Pd(0), which then re-enters the cycle. There are several classes of cross-coupling\h\h reactions, and they bear the name of their\h discoverers. The most popular version employs\h\h boron nucleophiles, usually boronic acids or\h esters, and is called the Suzuki coupling,\h\h or sometimes the Suzuki-Miyaura coupling, from the\h Japanese chemists Akira Suzuki and Norio Miyaura,\h\h who worked at Hokudai University and described\h the reaction in 1979. The Stille reaction uses tin\h\h nucleophiles, usually trialkyl stannanes, and is\h named after John Stille, an American chemist who\h\h worked at Colorado State University. The Negishi\h reaction uses organozinc halides as nucleophiles,\h\h and is named after Ei-ichi Negishi, a professor\h of chemistry who worked at Purdue University. The\h\h Hiyama reaction uses a number of silicon-based\h nucleophiles. It is named after Japanese\h\h chemist Tamejiro Hiyama who worked at Kyoto\h University. The Kumada reaction uses Grignard\h\h reagents as nucleophiles, and was invented\h in 1972 by Japanese chemist Makoto Kumada,\h\h working at Osaka City University. Kumada was\h a real pioneer in cross-coupling chemistry,\h\h having disclosed the first example of modern\h cross-coupling at the same time as French\h\h chemist Robert Corriu. The first embodiment of\h the reaction used nickel catalysts, which are\h\h still used today, but it can also be carried\h out with the more common palladium catalysts.\h In cross-coupling chemistry, examples\h have been described where the nucleophile\h\h features essentially any kind of metal, including\h lithium, sodium, aluminum, zirconium, germanium,\h\h and a few others, but these are niche reactions\h not normally used in practical applications. In\h\h addition to halides, other leaving groups can\h also be used, first and foremost, phosphate and\h\h sulfonate esters. Suzuki and Negishi received a\h Nobel Prize for their fundamental discoveries in\h\h 2010. Of all the cross-coupling procedures, the\h most widely used today is the Suzuki coupling,\h\h which we will briefly describe in this tutorial. If one attempts the reaction of bromobenzene\h\h with arylboronic acid in the presence of\h a palladium catalyst, there is no product.\h\h Suzuki then discovered that the coupling occurs\h in the presence of hydroxide ion. One can run\h\h the reaction in two phases, with a strong aqueous\h base and the reactants in an organic solvent, or\h\h under homogeneous conditions, in a dipolar solvent\h like dimethylformamide or dimethyl acetamide and a\h\h soluble base like tetrabutylammonium hydroxide.\h Under these highly basic conditions, with pH\h\h typically 11-13, the reaction occurs smoothly.\h Given that arylboronic acids are Lewis acids,\h\h hydroxide ions reacts with them to form, at\h equilibrium, a borate species. Suzuki proposed\h\h that this compound is the nucleophile that\h performs the transmetalation. On the other hand,\h\h hydroxide ion also replaces the halide in the\h palladium coordination sphere, yielding, at\h\h equilibrium, a Pd-OH species. Might this species\h play a role in the transmetalation as well?\h The first confirmation that this\h is the case came from John Hartwig,\h\h currently a chemistry professor at UC Berkeley.\h He showed that the reaction between 6 and 2,\h\h run in THF and water, is about 10,000 times faster\h than that of 5 with 4. Therefore, it appears that,\h\h at least under these conditions, the borate\h species 4 is not the active transmetalating\h\h agent, and if the neutral boronic acid is\h the active species, too much hydroxide,\h\h which converts the active 2 to the inactive\h 4, may inhibit the reaction. This was indeed\h\h demonstrated by the group of Christian Amatore\h and Anny Jutand at the Ecole Normale Superieure\h\h in Paris. The kinetic law shows a maximum rate\h at intermediate pH. Too high a pH inhibits the\h\h reaction by forming borate 4, whereas too\h low a pH also slows down the reaction by not\h\h forming enough 6. The optimum pH depends on the\h acidity of the boronic acid and on the position\h\h of the equilibrium between species like 5 and\h 6. Much more synthetic and mechanistic work\h\h has been carried out on this important reaction\h than we are able to describe in this tutorial,\h\h and it is possible that borate species 4 may play\h a positive role in some versions of this reaction.\h One of the most significant developments of\h the last two decades has been the replacement\h\h of the old-fashioned triphenyl phosphine ligand\h with a variety of bulky, electron-rich ligands,\h\h which afford much more robust and efficient\h catalysis. Some examples are tri-tert-butyl\h\h phosphine, tri-cyclohexyl phosphine, and\h biaryl ligands, often called Buchwald ligands,\h\h like SPhos. The latter were invented\h by Professor Stephen Buchwald at MIT.\h From a synthetic standpoint, the Suzuki\h coupling is very general and literally\h\h thousands of compounds have been prepared by\h this methodology. However, when compared with\h\h other cross-coupling reactions, it is important\h to highlight some limitations. The most obvious\h\h drawback is that it employs highly basic, protic\h conditions. All functions that can be hydrolyzed,\h\h such as esters, are potentially labile under\h the conditions used. Any acidic C-H bond can\h\h in principle be deprotonated, potentially\h leading to epimerizations or racemizations.\h\h Boronic acids can be protodeboronated\h under basic conditions, and this means\h\h an excess of boronic acid must often be used. Another one of the limitations of the original\h\h Suzuki coupling is its inability to couple sp3\h hybridized halides. This has been overcome by\h\h way of nickel catalysis. Alkyl halides can be\h generally employed with a number of boronic\h\h acids using a Ni(0) precatalyst, and a number of\h bidentate nitrogen-based ligands. In this field,\h\h the key figure has been Professor Gregory Fu\h at the California Institute of Technology,\h\h who has even reported examples of enantioselective\h couplings under nickel catalysis. The main limitation towards the general\h\h application of nickel chemistry is the poor\h mechanistic understanding of the reaction.\h\h There are clear indications that, alongside\h the classical mechanism shown for palladium,\h\h a Ni(I)/Ni(III) mechanism also operates. In\h addition, the amount of catalyst needed to\h\h perform these reactions is often in the range of\h 5-10 mol%, against 1 mol% or less with palladium.\h\h As a consequence, the nickel-catalyzed coupling is\h an area of intense investigation. It is possible\h\h that in the future, scarce and expensive palladium\h will be routinely replaced by cheaper metals,\h\h like nickel or cobalt, for an environmentally\h more benign cross-coupling, but this area still\h\h needs a lot of development. Even with the\h current limitations, the palladium-catalyzed\h\h Suzuki coupling remains the most popular C-C\h coupling reaction in all realms of synthesis,\h\h including real life applications of\h pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals.

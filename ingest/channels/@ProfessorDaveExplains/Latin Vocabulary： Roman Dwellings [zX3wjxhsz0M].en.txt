We’ve learned about how the Romans ate and\h dressed, but how did the Romans live? What\h\h were their homes like? If you were a farmer\h in ancient Rome, you would live in a villa.\h\h Unlike what your intuition might tell you,\h a villa-ae was not a wealthy dwelling, but\h\h rather just a farmhouse. A large family house for\h the master and his family, called villa dominica,\h\h was normally sided by pars rustica or farm\h buildings for the animals and the people\h\h taking care of the animals. Sometimes even a\h pars fructuāria would be present. That would\h\h be an orchard of fruit trees and buildings\h for making wine and olive oil. All in all,\h\h it appeared like something very similar to\h what is called a hacienda in Latin America.\h Even in the city, common people didn’t have a\h glamorous life. They would all live packed in\h\h apartment buildings called insulae-ārum. This is\h the same word that in Latin normally means island.\h\h These buildings were small, cold, and damp in the\h winter, and steamy hot in the summer. Sometimes\h\h they were large enough to house an entire city\h block, but that didn’t mean more comfortable\h\h living. It just meant a greater concentration of\h people, sometimes with an entire family in one\h\h room. The insulae were poorly built and because\h of that they often collapsed. Other times they\h\h simply burned down or were flooded by the River\h Tiber. The ground floor was often dedicated to\h\h shops called tabernae-ārum, while the apartments\h would be found on the upper floors, up to six or\h\h seven stories. Counterintuitively, the uppermost\h floors were the less desirable ones because of\h\h safety issues. They were usually the cheapest.\h Often they didn’t even have running water,\h\h and that meant no bathrooms were present either.\h So Romans had to leave their apartments in order\h\h to use lātrīnae-ārum, which were public bathrooms\h in the street, and it wasn’t unusual for them\h\h to throw refuse out of their windows, with all\h the associated sanitary issues you can imagine.\h Not far from these buildings were the homes of the\h very wealthy. They were called domus-ūs, a single\h\h family dwelling. These houses might include a\h taberna facing the street in order to conduct\h\h business, or to store items not suitable for the\h house. The door or entrance - ōstium was always\h\h from the street. Inside the house you would first\h see a foyer or vestibule, vestibulum-ī, where the\h\h owner would greet visitors. This would lead to\h the internal part of the house - ātrium-iī through\h\h a small hallway - faucēs-ium. The ātrium had\h enclosed rooms on all sides and a compluvium-iī,\h\h an inward-sloping open roof to provide both light\h and shade. This open roof also had the purpose of\h\h collecting precious rain water into a little pool\h - impluvium-iī. Sometimes a small altar called\h\h larārium-iī was present, with the house protector\h gods, Lāres. A tablīnum-ī would serve both as the\h\h study room for the pater familias, the head of\h the house, or as a reception room. The house would\h\h have a lovely internal garden - peristȳlium-iī\h often decorated with a colonnade - porticus-ūs\h\h to help air circulation on those hot summer days.\h Here is where the owner would grow flowers such as\h\h roses - rosa-ae and white lilies - līlium-iī. A large room was dedicated to dining and\h\h entertaining - trīclīnium-iī. A kitchen -\h culīna-ae would be present for cooking, and\h\h several bedrooms - cubiculum-ī would accommodate\h family and guests. These were usually small,\h\h just the necessary space to accommodate a bed. A\h larger house would also have room for a vegetable\h\h garden - hortus-ī to grow items essential for\h cooking. The interior walls were plastered and\h\h decorated with beautiful paintings of mythology or\h countryside, while floors were often covered with\h\h mosaics - opus vermīculātum made with small tiles\h - tessera-ae. Surprisingly there was not much\h\h furniture, with this being limited to couches, to\h relax or have dinner, as well as tables and chests\h\h to store things. When the weather was particularly\h cold or damp, these homes were made comfortable\h\h by the use of braziers -foculus-ī designed to\h hold hot coals which could be moved conveniently\h\h from room to room if necessary. In order to light\h the house candles, lamps, and torches were used.\h The most beautiful examples of domus, extremely\h well preserved, can be admired today in the town\h\h of Pompeii. Villa of the Mysteries, the House\h of Menander, and the House of the Faun are great\h\h examples. And with that, we have a better sense\h of how the Ancient Romans lived in their homes.

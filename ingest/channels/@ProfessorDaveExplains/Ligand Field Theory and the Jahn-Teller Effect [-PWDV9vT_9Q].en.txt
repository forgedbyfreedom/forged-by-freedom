So\h we introduced the concept of t2g orbitals, which\h\h are the lower-energy dxy, dxz, and dyz orbitals,\h and the higher-energy eg orbitals, which are the\h\h dx2-y2 and dz2 orbitals, and we called the energy\h between them the crystal field splitting energy.\h\h Now we have to build on this understanding\h of bonding in transition metal complexes\h\h by supplementing with another theory called ligand\h field theory, which can be thought of as somewhat\h\h of an extension of crystal field theory. These are the orbitals that will\h\h participate in bonding interactions with ligands.\h Let’s say we are looking at 4s, 4p, and 3d\h\h orbitals, such as with the cobalt center in this\h complex with six ammonia ligands and a 3+ charge.\h\h According to this model, we have six degenerate\h ligand orbitals that house the electrons involved\h\h in the metal-ligand bonds, and when the 9 orbitals\h from the metal overlap with these 6 orbitals from\h\h the ligands, 15 molecular orbitals will be\h produced. Ligand field\h theory also describes the manner in which these d\h\h orbitals are affected differently by different\h sets of ligands, and can have their energies\h\h raised or lowered depending on the strength\h of their interaction with the ligands.\h There is much more that we could discuss regarding\h ligand field theory, but let’s return to crystal\h\h field theory and discuss a particular ramification\h that will be of interest to us in learning about\h\h transition metal complexes. The Jahn-Teller\h effect, also known as Jahn-Teller distortion,\h\h is a phenomenon which describes the way\h that nonlinear molecules, and in particular\h\h transition metal complexes, may distort their\h geometry in order to remove the degeneracy\h\h of unequally occupied orbitals in order to lower\h the overall energy of the complex. In other words,\h\h this is something that will happen when the d\h orbitals are not filled in a symmetrical manner.\h\h Taking a typical octahedral complex, we recall\h that the d orbitals are split up into the\h\h lower-energy t2g orbitals and the higher energy\h eg orbitals. If electrons are arranged in these\h\h orbitals in a symmetrical manner, with one or\h both of these sets of orbitals either half full or\h\h completely full, the Jahn-Teller effect will not\h be observed. In any other case, it will. So high\h\h spin d4, low spin d7 or d9, these will be common\h situations that elicit the Jahn-Teller effect.\h Let us now understand precisely what kind of\h distortion will occur. Take for example an\h\h octahedral copper complex with a 2+ charge. This\h will have 9 electrons distributed around these d\h\h orbitals, with the t2g orbitals completely\h full, while the eg orbitals will be one full\h\h and the other half full. These eg orbitals\h are degenerate as shown, but the Jahn-Teller\h\h effect predicts that the complex will distort\h in such a way that the degeneracy is eliminated,\h\h which we call tetragonal distortion. When this\h happens, between the dx2-y2 and dz2 orbitals,\h\h one will become higher energy than the other, and\h they will end up the same distance away from the\h\h original orbital in energy, in either direction.\h The same goes for the other three orbitals,\h\h where the dxy will no longer be the same energy as\h the dxz and dyz. One set will be higher-energy and\h\h the other set will be lower-energy, compared with\h the original energies, and the distance to the dxy\h\h will be twice the distance to the others,\h because of the 1 to 2 ratio. As a result of\h\h this activity the complex will either stretch\h or compress along the z axis. If the asymmetry\h\h is in the t2g orbitals, the effect is weak.\h If in the eg orbitals, the effect is strong.\h Here are some examples so we can get a better\h understanding. First, a d4 high spin complex.\h\h For these orbitals down here, there is no\h net energy change. But for the ones up here,\h\h this lone electron will become lower in\h energy as it moves to the dx2-y2 orbital.\h\h This stabilization produces a tetragonal\h compression, so the metal-ligand bonds along the\h\h z axis will compress. If instead the dz2 orbital\h is the lower-energy orbital for a d4 high spin\h\h complex, we will get the same stabilization but\h we will observe tetragonal elongation, where these\h\h bonds will stretch instead of compress, due to the\h shape and orientation of the dz2 orbital. Compare\h\h these to a d5 high spin complex, where now all\h the orbitals are half full, so when distributed\h\h amongst the new orbitals there is actually no\h net energy change and therefore no distortion.\h The Jahn-Teller effect will be something to\h keep in mind when considering the geometry and\h\h properties of transition metal complexes.

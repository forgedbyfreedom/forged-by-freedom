We’ve spent the past few tutorials looking\h at first-order differential equations,\h\h but now it’s time to move on to second-order\h differential equations. As the name suggests,\h\h these are DEs that contain a second derivative\h term, like d squared y dx squared. What could\h\h such a thing be used for? As it turns out, this\h kind of differential equation crops up everywhere\h\h in physics. That’s because Newton’s second\h law, while commonly written as F equals m a,\h\h is actually a second order differential equation\h in disguise, since acceleration can be defined as\h\h the rate of change of velocity, which in turn\h is the rate of change of displacement. We’ll\h\h see more examples of this kind of thing later,\h but first, let’s get to grips with the basics.\h Firstly, let’s define what is meant by a linear\h homogeneous second-order differential equation.\h\h This will be any DE of the general form d squared\h y dx squared plus P of x times dy/dx plus Q of\h\h x times y equals zero. But we’re actually going to\h restrict this form a little more, and we’re going\h\h to look at the constant coefficients case, where\h all the coefficients that could be functions of x,\h\h are actually just constants. We are therefore\h really learning about solving differential\h\h equations of the form a times d squared y\h dx squared plus b times dy/dx plus c times y\h\h equals zero. To solve this, we are going to write\h down something called a characteristic equation,\h\h or sometimes known as an auxiliary equation. We\h convert the derivatives into powers of an unknown\h\h variable lambda, giving us a times lambda squared\h plus b times lambda plus c, which is a quadratic\h\h equation in lambda. We can solve this using\h the quadratic equation to get two solutions,\h\h which we’ll call lambda 1 and lambda 2. It turns\h out that if we take y equals e to the lambda x as\h\h a guess solution, or ansatz solution, then this\h will always satisfy our differential equation\h\h in the cases where lambda is either lambda 1\h or lambda 2 from our characteristic equation.\h We now need to learn an important fact:\h for any linear differential equation,\h\h no matter what order, if we can find any number\h of different linearly independent solutions,\h\h we can say for sure that their sum, when\h multiplied by any set of constant coefficients,\h\h will also be a solution, which is a fact\h known as the superposition principle.\h Let’s apply this to our second-order DE, where\h we know that e to the lambda 1 x and e to the\h\h lambda 2 x are both solutions. There are three\h possible cases. First, if lambda 1 and lambda 2\h\h are distinct real numbers, then we already have\h two linearly independent solutions, so we can\h\h directly apply the superposition principle to get\h the general solution to our DE as y equals A times\h\h e to the lambda 1 x plus B times e to the lambda\h 2 x, where A and B are independent arbitrary\h\h constants. The second case is if our roots lambda\h 1 and lambda 2 are not real but instead complex\h\h numbers. In this case, since the coefficients of\h our DE are real, the complex roots lambda 1 and\h\h lambda 2 must be complex conjugates of each other,\h so we can write them as lambda 1 equals alpha plus\h\h beta i and lambda 2 equals alpha minus beta i,\h where alpha and beta are the real and imaginary\h\h parts of lambda respectively. Using some complex\h number arithmetic which we’ll skip, we can show\h\h that the appropriate ansatz solutions are y 1\h equals e to the alpha x times cosine of beta x,\h\h and y 2 equals e to the alpha x times sine of beta\h x, so by superposition, our general solution for\h\h the case of complex roots is y equals e to the\h alpha x times the quantity A times cosine of\h\h beta x plus B times sine of beta x. The third and\h final case is if the characteristic equation has a\h\h single repeated real root. In this case, we will\h only actually have one ‘ansatz’, which is just e\h\h to the lambda x, so we can’t use the principle of\h superposition. The good news is that it’s an easy\h\h fix. Whenever we have a repeated root, we can\h just multiply our ansatz by x to get a linearly\h\h independent function that is still a solution\h to our DE. So for this repeated roots case, the\h\h general solution will be A times e to the lambda x\h plus B times x times e to the lambda x, and we can\h\h factor out the exponential to get this neat form\h shown here: e to the lambda x times the quantity\h\h A plus B x. These three cases: real distinct\h roots, complex roots, and repeated root, are worth\h\h committing to memory, because when solving second\h order DEs, the algebra can get pretty lengthy,\h\h so it will really help if we can just cut straight\h to these solutions as and when we need them.\h Now let’s look at how to use boundary conditions\h with this type of DE. Since we have two unknown\h\h constants A and B in our general solution, we\h will require two different boundary conditions\h\h to completely identify a particular solution.\h Sometimes, these will be given to us as the values\h\h of the function y, as well as its derivative\h dy/dx, or y prime, at some point x. This is\h\h called an initial value problem, or IVP. Other\h times, we might be given two different values\h\h of y at two different values of x, which is\h a boundary value problem, or BVP. Either way,\h\h we need to substitute these conditions into our\h general solution, and if it’s an IVP, we’ll need\h\h to first differentiate our general solution before\h subbing in the given point x. We will then end up\h\h with a system of 2 algebraic equations in\h 2 unknowns, which can easily be solved by\h\h elimination, to yield our particular solution. Let’s wrap up this section with an application\h\h that you will become very familiar with if\h you go on to use differential equations in\h\h a physics or engineering context. Suppose\h that we have a trolley of mass m kilograms,\h\h sliding on frictionless horizontal rails. Attached\h to the trolley is a spring, with a stiffness k\h\h newtons per meter, as well as a dashpot, with\h a damping rate of c newton seconds per meter.\h We know that springs exert elastic forces given\h by F equals negative k x according to Hooke’s law,\h\h where x is the extension or displacement, but what\h is a dashpot? This means\h\h a dashpot will exert a large resistive force\h on the trolley when the trolley moves quickly,\h\h damping its motion. Since speed is the rate of\h change of displacement x, we can say that the\h\h dashpot force in our case is given by negative c\h times dx/dt. Newton’s second law states that the\h\h sum of the forces acting on the trolley must\h equal m times a. We know that acceleration a\h\h is the rate of change of velocity, which in turn\h is the rate of change of displacement, so we can\h\h write a as the second derivative of x, and we can\h put in our spring and dashpot forces as shown. If\h\h we move everything to one side, we end up with the\h equation: m times the second derivative of x plus\h\h c times the first derivative of x plus k times\h x equals zero, which exactly matches a linear\h\h homogeneous second order differential equation\h with constant coefficients. We can therefore jump\h\h right to our solutions that we found earlier, to\h completely describe the motion of the trolley for\h\h all times t, and in fact, the three cases that we\h learned have some notable physical significance.\h In the first case, the roots of the characteristic\h equation were real, and we say that the motion is\h\h overdamped, and the trolley will be slowly brought\h to rest.

After Aristotle’s first “official” establishment\h of Logic, it was not surprising that the first\h\h developments came from the Peripatetics\h themselves, his students and followers.\h\h Theophrastus of Eresus and Eudemus of Rhodes were\h the main contributors, publishing more on the\h\h subject than Aristotle himself. However, much like\h several others in this period, very few fragments\h\h remain, with most records coming from posterior\h commentators. These two figures simplified and\h\h improved several minor aspects of Aristotle’s\h logic, especially his modal theory, by clarifying\h\h the roles of different degrees of contingency.\h For example, that possibility is weaker than\h\h actuality, which is weaker than necessity, and\h that a conclusion can only be as strong as the\h\h weaker of them in the premises. Besides developing\h a few short-lived new forms of argument,\h\h they were the first to propose what would become\h the modus ponens and modus tollens in logic.\h\h Here are examples of each: “If Dave is human,\h then he is mortal./Dave is human./Thus,\h\h Dave is mortal.” and “If YouTube is human,\h then it is mortal./YouTube is not mortal./Thus,\h\h Youtube is not human.” These are simple,\h conditional syllogisms where the subject and\h\h predicate become ‘antecedent’ and ‘consequent’,\h and the only new information in the second premise\h\h is either affirming the antecedent or in denial\h of the consequent. So the structure of a modus\h\h ponens would be “If A, then B./A./Then B.”, and a\h modus tollens “If A, then B./Not B./Then not A.”\h Pyrrhonism also veered somewhat into logic,\h as they developed a criticism of deduction and\h\h induction while arguing that affirmations of\h knowledge can be neither true nor false. They\h\h criticized judgements of knowledge by reducing\h all possible justification, or basis for truth\h\h or falsity, to fallible methods in any form\h of argument. Agrippa, a famous pyrrhonist,\h\h proposed five modes of knowledge justification,\h developed afterwards in a trilemma, or an argument\h\h presenting three alternatives in the premises.\h This would be the basis for most epistemological\h\h analyses, as theories establish the basis for\h knowledge either: 1) as unquestionable dogmas,\h\h or axioms, serving as a base to build all\h knowledge, known today as foundationalism,\h\h 2) as a circular relationship between truths\h supporting each other, known today as coherentism,\h\h or 3) as a series of regressions where\h one truth is the basis for another,\h\h and so on indefinitely, known as infinite regress. Here is an example to lend some context. Consider\h\h the phrase: “The word of God is the Bible”.\h First, according to foundationalism, you can’t\h\h ask “why?” for this proposition, because it is,\h from a religious perspective, true in itself.\h\h If you are a Pyrrhonian skeptic you will ask\h “why?”. The lack of answer from dogmatists will\h\h make this proposition unjustified, and therefore\h neither true nor false for our inquirer. Second,\h\h for coherentism, this proposition is circular in a\h vicious manner because it incorporates the concept\h\h that it is trying to prove. Because the Bible says\h that God exists, God can make the Bible the word\h\h of God. This doesn’t act as firm justification.\h Lastly, considering the infinite regress, we can\h\h point to the fact that if anyone wants to justify\h the existence of God, this will need another\h\h justification, which will need another one, and\h another, and so forth. Given that we can’t explore\h\h all possible justifications for any statement,\h we can’t take its truth or falsity at face\h\h value. Agrippa’s trilemma can help us identify\h fallacious forms of argumentation in rhetorical\h\h or theological babblings, and it also ties in\h with what we saw in the previous tutorial about\h\h Pyrrhonism, given that using this method is a path\h to suspend one’s judgments. Thus, for them, this\h\h recognition would compromise any attempt to assert\h the truth or falsity of any proposition, although\h\h several philosophers still defend some version\h of these formulations to this day. Interestingly,\h\h Sextus recognized the issue of using arguments\h to criticize arguments, but compared his to a\h\h “purgative medicine” which eliminates itself\h as well as the bodily fluids it intends to.\h\h This was similar to Ludwig Wittgenstein’s first\h perspective of philosophy some 1750 years later.\h The rather unknown Dialectician school, risen from\h the platonic and Eleatic-mixed Megarian school,\h\h also developed a few notions of logic that were\h quite advanced for their time. They treated\h\h propositions as a whole, instead of Aristotle’s\h term logic, as being crucial in the development\h\h of propositional logic. Thus, instead of\h operators, it was the proposition itself\h\h that defined its mode, if necessary or possible,\h or the negative of both, depending on how it was\h\h phrased. Another relevant development was a theory\h of meaning, where everything said had only one\h\h correct interpretation according to the intention\h of the speaker, and ambiguity only existed with\h\h intentionally obscuring speech – a prototypical\h formulation of what would only recently become the\h\h Speech Acts theory by Austin. However, this also\h rendered their analysis of validation problematic,\h\h as what is said is the “actual”, and thus\h counterintuitively only the “actual” is possible.\h To prove this, they developed the first\h and weakest version of the Master Argument,\h\h an important logical topic in antiquity,\h which summarily posited that: 1) past truth\h\h is always necessary, 2) the impossible does not\h follow from the possible, and 3) something is\h\h possible which neither is nor will be true. Zeno of Citium, founder of the Stoics,\h\h studied with the dialectician Diodorus, and\h eagerly wrote on logical topics as well as the\h\h Master Argument with his successor Cleanthes, but\h produced nothing too original. Chrysippus of Soli,\h\h Cleanthes’s successor, however, is said to be the\h second greatest logician in the history of logic\h\h itself, with a lasting impact on several branches\h of logic. Much like the dialecticians, their\h\h logical inquiry also encompassed epistemology and\h philosophy of language. Reason played a great role\h\h in asserting the truth of an argument, and true\h knowledge could only arise from the conviction of\h\h a sage altogether with the verification of their\h peers and general consensus. Logic then was the\h\h study of the functioning of reason itself.\h Propositional logic was further developed,\h\h and similarly to the Peripatetic systematization\h of conditional propositions, premises were called\h\h “antecedent”, and the conclusion “consequent”.\h Their subject matter in logic was the sayable,\h\h a meaning beyond what is said or written, much\h like the dialectician speech acts. A single\h\h premise then would be a complete sayable, as its\h meaning would be contained in itself, while an\h\h incomplete one required further questioning\h for its completion, or what we would call an\h\h isolated predicate. They were also the first to\h propose complex propositions. While Aristotle\h\h focused on terms and the form of a syllogism, the\h Stoics focused on how propositions could interact\h\h with one another via different connectives,\h which allowed them to surpass his teachings.\h\h By treating a proposition as a variable, much\h like predicates and subjects in term logic,\h\h one could further develop systems to verify them.\h To be valid, again inspired by the Peripatetics\h\h and the Dialecticians, any combination of\h arguments should be reducible to the five\h\h “indemonstrables”, or most basic argument forms. The first two are the modus ponens and modus\h\h tollens as we have previously seen,\h where p and q are complete sayables,\h\h or propositions. They are followed then by “It is\h not the case that both p and q/p/Therefore not-q”,\h\h and then “Either p or q/p/Therefore not-q” and\h finally “Either p or q/Not p/Therefore q”. Thus,\h\h all propositions would be true or false, even\h those that were possible but did not happen.\h\h This would then also be a response to the Master\h Argument, where Cleanthes would defend that past\h\h truth was not necessary, and Chrysippus would\h deny that what is impossible does not follow from\h\h what is possible, as there is no motion without a\h cause – and thus, not everything is true or false.\h As their five indemonstrables attempt\h to prove, that is not the case,\h\h and thus everything that exists would have a\h necessary causal relationship with other things,\h\h what would be called a “causal determinism”. This\h is consonant with the overall Stoic position that\h\h through reason one can know everything, for\h if something was neither true nor false, it\h\h would be unknowable. In contrast with contemporary\h interpretations of speech acts and propositional\h\h logic, as well as other logical theories of the\h time, similar utterances in different moments\h\h refer to the same sayables for the Stoics, and\h truthfulness may change according to its context.\h The width of the subjects in which the Stoics and\h especially Chrysippus delved into, improving logic\h\h to an extent never even imagined by Aristotle,\h is far beyond the scope of this series. But\h\h hopefully, the role of the Stoics and their\h contemporaries in the development of logic is now\h\h a bit more apparent.

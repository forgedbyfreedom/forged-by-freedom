We’ll take a look at\h just a few formal fallacies since they’re mostly\h\h technical errors, and then focus on the informal\h ones as they’re much more common and varied.\h Among formal fallacies there are simple ones\h such as four terms, where instead of our usual\h\h three terms in a syllogism there is an extra\h one which lacks logical connection with the\h\h others. Another example is the epistemic fallacy,\h\h or the “masked man” fallacy given its first\h formulation by the Greek Eubulides. An example\h\h of this would be “I know who Dave is./I do not\h know who this masked man is./Thus, this masked\h\h man is not Dave.” This of course is invalid since\h the lack of knowledge of who lies behind the mask\h\h is not correlated in any way to whether it is\h or is not me; thus its “epistemic” reference, as\h\h the knowledge or belief of an entity is different\h from that entity itself. Similar but lesser known,\h\h there is also an ontological fallacy or fallacy\h of misplaced concreteness which mistakenly\h\h assumes that something does exist without the\h guarantee that it exists in reality. We can also talk about the proof by example\h fallacy in quantificational logic that happens\h\h when a single or merely a few instantiations\h of something are taken to be representative of\h\h that thing instead of an adequate proof of\h it. Conversely,\h\h the accidental fallacy attempts to invalidate\h given statements as exceptions to a given rule,\h\h much like its more extreme version, the no true\h Scotsman fallacy, or an “appeal to purity”,\h\h which attempts to invalidate a statement by\h contrasting it with a hypothetical perfect rule\h\h or standard. This is also related to\h\h the moving the goalpost fallacy, very common in\h internet discourse, where as the name suggests,\h\h the objective of the discussion is always altered\h to benefit one’s argument in detriment of another,\h\h as if the goalposts were always changing around\h in a football match in order to invalidate the\h\h field goals of the opposing team, or making them\h more difficult to achieve. There’s also\h the appeal to nature, which attempts to settle\h\h or invalidate an argument based on some natural\h quality involved, widely used in food fads,\h\h such as: “This chemical is found in this kind of\h food, and also in yoga mats.

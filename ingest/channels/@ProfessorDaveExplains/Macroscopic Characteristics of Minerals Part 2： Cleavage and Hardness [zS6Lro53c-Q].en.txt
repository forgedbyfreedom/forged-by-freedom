We just learned about how to identify minerals on\h the basis of luster and color. There are two more\h\h important characteristics we will also need to\h discuss. One of the best ways to identify minerals\h\h is by examining their cleavage, or lack thereof.\h A mineral is said to have cleavage if it breaks\h\h along a planar surface in one or more directions.\h The way that a mineral breaks is fundamentally\h\h controlled by its crystalline structure, and more\h specifically its relative strength along different\h\h crystallographic directions. For example, graphite\h is composed of stacks of two-dimensional sheets of\h\h carbon atoms bonded together through van der Waals\h forces. The carbon atoms themselves are strongly\h\h bonded within a sheet, but the bonds between the\h sheets are extremely weak. Because of this, when\h\h graphite is broken, it breaks into planar sheets\h along the direction of the weak van der Waals\h\h bonds, but breaks irregularly along the direction\h of the stronger, more isotropic covalent bonds.\h\h A mineral that has one direction of cleavage,\h like graphite, is said to have basal cleavage.\h\h A class of minerals called phyllosilicates,\h or sheet silicates, are easily identified\h\h based on their perfect basal cleavage, causing\h them to break apart like the crust of baklava.\h Some minerals totally lack cleavage and break\h along random, uneven fractures. The mineral\h\h quartz, or silicon dioxide, has a lattice composed\h of equal-strength silicon-oxygen bonds in all\h\h directions, and therefore lacks cleavage. However,\h quartz does have a characteristic fracture pattern\h\h called conchoidal fracture. A conchoidal\h fracture appears as a concave “scoop” with\h\h a series of parallel arc-like steps along its\h surface. Think of a broken piece of thick glass,\h\h like the bottom of a glass soda bottle. When\h a mineral has more than one plane of cleavage,\h\h it is useful to estimate the angle between them,\h which can also be diagnostic. The minerals quartz,\h\h calcite, and halite often appear very similar in\h color and luster. The easiest way to tell them\h\h apart, is to look for cleavage. Quartz has no\h cleavage, whereas calcite has 3 directions of\h\h cleavage. In addition, while both halite and\h calcite have three directions of cleavage,\h\h the cleavage planes of calcite are 60- and\h 120-degrees apart vs. 90 degrees for halite. If\h\h you were to smash halite with a hammer, it would\h break into tiny cubes. Do the same to calcite\h\h and it would break into tiny rhombohedrons. Two\h of the most common minerals in Earth’s crust,\h\h quartz and plagioclase, can also look similar in\h color and luster, especially to the untrained eye.\h\h But plagioclase has two directions of cleavage at\h 90 degrees in contrast with quartz’s lack thereof.\h Even scratching a rock with your fingernail can\h be a quick way to classify and identify minerals,\h\h telling you something about the strength of its\h chemical bonds. A typical human fingernail has a\h\h hardness, or resistance to scratching, similar\h to the mineral calcite. Austrian mineralogist,\h\h Friedrich Mohs, determined this literal rule of\h thumb while developing the Mohs Hardness Scale.\h\h The Mohs scale ranks minerals by their ability\h to scratch each other, and is used to measure\h\h the relative hardness of a mineral sample.\h The scale goes from 1, the softest mineral,\h\h talc, to 10, the hardest mineral, diamond.\h Geologists also use common objects like coins or a\h\h metal fingernail file to approximate the hardness\h of minerals. Pennies have a hardness of about 3.5\h\h on the Mohs scale, similar to fluorite, and metal\h files have a hardness of 6.5 on the Mohs scale,\h\h similar to quartz. For example, say you have an\h unknown mineral that can be scratched by a steel\h\h file, but not by a penny. This would indicate that\h your mineral has a hardness between 3.5 and 6.5.\h So that wraps up a discussion surrounding some\h of the physical characteristics of minerals.\h\h Now that we’ve related the atomic structure\h of minerals to their macroscopic properties,\h\h we can move on to the\h different classes of minerals\h\h and discover how crystal chemistry and\h crystal structure relate to one another.

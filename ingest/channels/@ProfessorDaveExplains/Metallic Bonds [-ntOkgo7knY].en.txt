The same can be said for a\h\h metalloid and a nonmetal, again covalent bonding\h will be most likely. But what occurs between two\h\h metals? What kind of bonding is present in\h a substance that is made entirely of metal,\h\h whether just one metallic element or several? This\h is called metallic bonding. We have previously\h\h discussed metallic bonding in passing, but\h now it’s time to get a closer look at this\h\h phenomenon so that we can be better prepared for\h more advanced topics like inorganic chemistry\h\h and materials science, as these will be important\h applications of our general chemistry knowledge.\h Metallic bonds are quite different from ionic\h or covalent bonds. One major difference stems\h\h from their extreme strength, which is what gives\h most metals such high melting and boiling points.\h\h Each atom within a metal is undergoing atomic\h orbital overlap with many neighboring atoms,\h\h and this generates an enormous number of molecular\h orbitals that extend across the entire substance,\h\h holding the lattice together. Although we\h technically consider all of the atoms to\h\h be neutral, we can also view a metal as a grid of\h metal cations, with all of their valence electrons\h\h completely delocalized over the entire lattice.\h Electrons can move freely within the molecular\h\h orbitals throughout the lattice, forming a sea of\h electrons, and the metal is held together by the\h\h attraction between the nuclei and these electrons,\h thereby giving a metal both ionic and covalent\h\h characteristics. Electrons dissociate from\h their respective atoms, like with ionic bonds,\h\h but the electrons are shared among\h the atoms like with covalent bonds.\h\h So this is the other major difference between\h metallic bonds and the other bonds we are used to.\h\h The extreme delocalization of electrons is the\h key feature that gives metals their signature\h\h properties, which we will discuss in a moment. Metallic bonds can exhibit varying strengths,\h\h and this will depend on the degree of\h attraction between the nuclei and the electrons.\h\h This is where electron configuration becomes\h relevant. For example, sodium has one valence\h\h electron and therefore tends to form a 1+ cation,\h while magnesium has two valence electrons and\h\h therefore tends to form a 2+ cation. Coulomb’s\h law dictates that a charge of a greater magnitude\h\h results in a stronger attraction, so magnesium\h will make stronger metallic bonds than sodium.\h\h In addition, more delocalized valence electrons\h will also correlate with stronger metallic bonds.\h\h We must also understand that the strength\h of these metallic bonds does not depend\h\h on the directionality of the bond, as their\h strength persists even in the liquid phase.\h\h Gallium, for example, has such a low melting\h point that it will melt in your hands\h\h simply due to the warmth emanating from your\h body. And yet, gallium has a high boiling point\h\h typical of other metals, like copper. Metallic\h bonding remains strong in the liquid phase.\h Continuing, we can look at common substances like\h aluminum and copper in order to illustrate the\h\h key properties that result from the delocalized\h electrons. These are malleability, ductility,\h\h and luster. Malleability describes the ability\h of a metal to be shaped by physical force,\h\h via hammering, pressing, rolling, and other such\h methods. So we are essentially describing the\h\h metal’s ability to withstand pressure without\h breaking.

Now that we have a better understanding of\h minerals, we are ready to discuss rocks.\h\h Minerals and rocks form in response to\h environmental forcing, with important\h\h variables being pressure, temperature,\h and chemistry of the starting material.\h\h Here, we will discuss the crystallization of magma\h and the importance of its chemistry in determining\h\h a rock’s mineralogy. There are three types of\h rocks: igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic,\h\h and these differ in their mechanism of formation.\h We will begin our discussion with igneous rocks,\h\h which were the first type of rocks to appear on\h Earth after its formation and subsequent cooling.\h Igneous rocks are formed when a mass of melted\h rock crystallizes. There are two terms geologists\h\h use for molten rock. When it is underground, it\h is called magma, up here it is called lava. So,\h\h where does this magma come from? Most igneous\h rocks were once mantle rock that, at some point,\h\h melted and moved toward the surface, where it\h cooled and solidified. You may now be wondering\h\h why lava isn’t erupting everywhere on Earth. It\h turns out that under normal conditions, the mantle\h\h is perfectly happy as a solid and cannot melt;\h and volcanoes are an exception to the rule. So,\h\h what sort of process is going on here that allows\h the mantle to melt? In fact, there are two ways\h\h to melt the mantle in large quantities:\h decompression melting and flux melting.\h\h Let’s start with decompression melting. This is\h exactly what it sounds like, melting the mantle\h\h by lowering its pressure. Decompression melting is\h responsible for the volcanism above both mid-ocean\h\h ridges and hotspots. Beneath these environments,\h hot mantle rock is moving upward due to mantle\h\h convection. Since hot material is less dense\h than cold material, the weight of a column of hot\h\h material will be less than that of cold material.\h Therefore, at a given depth, the pressure is lower\h\h beneath a mid-ocean ridge or hotspot than\h it is beneath a regular piece of crust.\h Let’s follow an ascending piece of mantle rock as\h it rises beneath a mid-ocean ridge. At a depth of\h\h about 30 kilometers, our piece of mantle has\h a temperature of 1400°C. Its pressure can be\h\h calculated using the following equation: P = ρgz,\h where ρ is its density, g is acceleration due to\h\h gravity, and z is the depth below Earth's surface.\h Since ρ and g are more or less constant for this\h\h example, the main variable affecting the pressure\h is the depth, which should be fairly intuitive:\h\h the more mass above a given area, the higher\h the pressure will be. The two black arrows\h\h are representative of the downward pressure.\h The upper right section shows a phase diagram\h\h corresponding to the state of our piece of mantle\h in pressure-depth space. The green dashed curve is\h\h the average geotherm, which shows how temperature\h changes with depth. The red solid line is the\h\h melting point of mantle rock. Notice how the two\h curves are nearly parallel, this illustrates the\h\h requirement for abnormal conditions to get the\h mantle to melt. As our mantle rock ascends,\h\h it experiences lower pressure, since there is\h now less mass above it. But notice that the\h\h temperature did not change, it is still 1400°C.\h This is an example of an adiabatic process,\h\h meaning heat has not been added or removed from\h the system. Essentially, the rising motion of\h\h the mantle occurs at sufficient speed that we can\h assume that heat is not lost to its surroundings\h\h during ascent. Therefore, the key to decompression\h melting is the advection of hot material upward,\h\h to a lower-pressure environment. Finally, at a\h pressure of 2 kilobars, the mantle is melting,\h\h with the purple dashed line signaling the onset of\h melting. So long as there is upward-moving mantle,\h\h our magma factory will persist - this is\h decompression melting. The vast majority of\h\h volcanism on Earth, both past and present,\h is due to decompression partial melting.\h Flux melting involves the addition of water\h to mantle rock, which lowers its melting\h\h point. This is responsible for the volcanism at\h subduction zones. Here, the subducting oceanic\h\h lithosphere releases water into the mantle\h above it, which induces melting. It is not\h\h actual liquid water that is transported to the\h mantle, but hydroxide anions that are released\h\h from the high-pressure breakdown of hydrous\h minerals, like serpentine, or Mg3Si2O5(OH)4.\h\h This chemically bound water is released\h around the same depth everywhere on Earth,\h\h about 100 kilometers, which explains why volcanoes\h occur in a line, or arc at subduction zones.\h Since most igneous rocks are derived from\h the mantle, which is relatively chemically\h\h homogeneous, we might expect igneous rocks to also\h be this way, but they aren’t. To understand this,\h\h we must first discuss the crystallization process,\h and more importantly, crystallization sequence. In\h\h a cooling magma, crystallization order is\h dependent upon the chemistry of the magma\h\h and the relative melting points of the stable\h minerals. Since most magmas are derived from\h\h melting the same stuff, the mantle, we can ignore\h the influence of chemistry for these purposes.\h\h In 1928, pioneering geologist N. L.\h Bowen created an experimental model\h\h for the crystallization order of a standard\h magma as it cools and chemically evolves. In\h\h Bowen’s Reaction Series, the first mineral to\h crystallize is olivine, followed by pyroxene,\h\h then amphibole, then biotite, and finally\h potassium feldspar, muscovite, and quartz.\h\h Plagioclase can exist at all stages of Bowen’s\h Reaction Series, as it is the most abundant\h\h mineral in the crust. We may recall that the first\h mineral to crystallize, olivine, an orthosilicate,\h\h has the smallest amount of silica of all the\h silicate minerals, while the last group contains\h\h potassium feldspar and quartz, which are framework\h silicates, the minerals with the most silica. This\h\h has important implications for the composition of\h a cooling melt. Let’s consider an average basalt,\h\h having 50% SiO2, 15% Al2O3, 20% MgO, 10% CaO, and\h 5% alkalis. Now, let’s start to crystallize out\h\h forsterite, the magnesium endmember of olivine,\h which is composed of 57% MgO and 43% SiO2. What\h\h is going to happen? Well, the olivine crystals,\h which are more dense, will sink to the bottom\h\h of the magma chamber, becoming isolated from\h the upward-moving magma. Since olivine has a\h\h smaller fraction of silica in comparison to the\h melt, the melt will become enriched in silica\h\h as olivine crystallizes. In fact, the melt\h will become enriched in everything but MgO,\h\h which gets depleted making olivine, since olivine\h has a higher fraction of MgO than the melt. The\h\h result is a melt that becomes enriched in silica,\h calcium, alkalis, and aluminum as it cools and\h\h moves upward. This process is then repeated for\h pyroxene, amphibole, and biotite, which depletes\h\h the melt in everything but silica, aluminum, and\h the alkalis, which become concentrated in the\h\h cooling melt. This results in the last minerals to\h form being those with silicon, aluminum, sodium,\h\h and potassium in their chemistry, such as\h quartz, muscovite, and alkali feldspar.\h Solid solution further complicates things. For a\h general rule of thumb, magnesium-rich endmembers\h\h crystallize before iron-rich endmembers,\h and calcium-rich endmembers form before\h\h sodium-rich endmembers. This explains the\h right side of Bowen’s Reaction Series;\h\h since anorthite has a higher melting point than\h albite, the first plagioclase that crystallizes\h\h will be calcium-rich and the last will be more\h sodium-rich. The same thing happens with olivine,\h\h pyroxene, and amphibole; the first minerals\h to form will be magnesium-rich, with crystals\h\h becoming progressively iron-enriched during\h cooling. These processes are together referred\h\h to as differentiation, which describes how a\h magma changes, or becomes different, as it cools.\h The amount of differentiation a magma\h undergoes is generally related to the\h\h amount of crust that it had to move through to\h make it near the surface. Therefore, the coolest\h\h and most differentiated magmas commonly erupt\h in areas where the crust is relatively thick,\h\h such as on continents. This is easy to see when\h comparing Hawaii and Yellowstone, as they are both\h\h hotspots. The volcanoes of Hawaii erupt basaltic,\h or mafic lava at around 1300 degrees Celsius,\h\h whereas Yellowstone erupts cool, felsic magma\h that is so thick and viscous, that it clogs up the\h\h volcano, causing extremely explosive eruptions.\h What is the difference? The Yellowstone hotspot\h\h is beneath thick continental crust, while the\h Hawaii hotspot is beneath thin, oceanic crust.

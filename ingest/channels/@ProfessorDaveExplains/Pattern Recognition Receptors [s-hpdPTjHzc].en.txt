These are protein sensors\h\h in and on cells that detect evidence of infection\h or tissue damage, which then launch a signaling\h\h cascade designed to deal with the threat. We’ve\h introduced the general concept of these pathogen\h\h and danger-sensing molecules before, so in this\h tutorial, we are going to learn precisely what\h\h these molecules sense, and how they translate\h detection into immunological action. As we\h\h already know, the signals that pattern recognition\h receptors sense are called pathogen-associated\h\h molecular patterns or PAMPs, and damage-associated\h molecular patterns, or DAMPs. PAMPs are specific\h\h molecular sequences or patterns that are\h only found in pathogens; for example,\h\h components of bacterial cell walls or unique forms\h of nucleic acids that are found in viral genomes,\h\h like single-stranded DNA or double-stranded\h RNA. PAMPs also tend to be highly conserved,\h\h meaning that many different pathogens have\h them, and as we have already discussed,\h\h they tend to be molecules that a pathogen needs\h in order to survive. This prevents a pathogen\h\h from evolving away from innate immune recognition.\h DAMPs, on the other hand, are host molecules that\h\h appear in the wrong place at the wrong time. Think\h about ATP. ATP is all over the place inside cells,\h\h but it is rarely found outside of cells. If a\h cell senses extracellular ATP, it suggests that\h\h there is a damaged cell nearby, and in this case,\h the extracellular ATP would be acting as a DAMP.\h Not only is the location of PAMPs and\h DAMPs important for immune signaling,\h\h but the location of pattern recognition\h receptors is also extremely important.\h\h Some are on the surface of cells, and can\h sense extracellular PAMPs and DAMPs. Others are\h\h cytosolic for intracellular sensing, and\h still others can be found in endosomes,\h\h an organelle formed during endocytosis.\h Endosomal pattern recognition receptors\h\h sense PAMPs or DAMPs that have been endocytosed\h from the extracellular space. The distribution of\h\h membrane-bound pattern recognition receptors\h can be extremely polarized. For example,\h\h intestinal epithelial cells are constantly exposed\h to the microbes in the intestinal microbiome. If\h\h they were constantly sensing the extracellular\h bacteria in the gut, the intestine would always be\h\h inflamed. Instead, many of these bacterial-sensing\h pattern recognition receptors are only located on\h\h the basolateral side of the cell, which is\h the side not facing the inside of the gut.\h\h In this way, these cells will only sense bacteria\h that have crossed the epithelial barrier and\h\h that need to be contained by the immune system. One of the most well-studied families of pattern\h\h recognition receptors are the Toll-like receptors\h or TLRs. These receptors are homologs of a fruit\h\h fly protein called Toll, which is involved in\h defense against bacterial and fungal pathogens.\h\h TLR homologs can be found in many mammals,\h other invertebrates, and even plants,\h\h which tells us that this family of sensors\h is very ancient in an evolutionary context.\h\h Let’s take a look at the structure of a TLR.\h TLRs are single-pass transmembrane proteins,\h\h meaning that part of the protein passes through\h a lipid membrane one time. The extracellular\h\h domain of a TLR has leucine-rich repeats that\h form a horseshoe or C-shape, and this is the\h\h part of the protein that binds to ligands. The\h intracellular side has a distinct region called\h\h a TIR domain. This region is special, because it\h can interact with TIR domains on other proteins\h\h that are essential for signal transduction. When\h a TLR binds its ligand, it either homo-dimerizes,\h\h or forms a heterodimer with other TLRs.\h Dimerization brings together cytoplasmic\h\h tails of the receptors, which allows for docking\h of TIR domain-containing adaptor proteins MyD88,\h\h TRIF, Mal, and TRAM, with each adaptor having\h different endpoints to their signaling cascades.\h\h Let’s discuss the differences\h between the signaling cascades\h\h of the two major adaptor proteins, MyD88 and TRIF. The MyD88 pathway begins when the cytoplasmic\h\h tails of dimerized TLRs are brought\h together, allowing MyD88 to bind. Here,\h\h MyD88 recruits the kinases IRAK4, IRAK1, and\h IRAK2. The IRAKs phosphorylate and activate TRAF6.\h\h TRAF6 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase, which is a type\h of enzyme that tags other proteins with ubiquitin\h\h groups. TRAF6 polyubiquinates itself and the\h protein NEMO, which recruits and activates TAK1.\h\h TAK1 then phosphorylates and activates the\h IKK complex. However, when IκB gets\h degraded, NF-κB translocates to the nucleus,\h\h where it acts as a transcription factor for\h inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-a and IL-6.\h TRIF is the other main adaptor protein\h for TLRs. TRIF recruits TRAF6 and TRAF3.\h\h TRAF6 recruits RIP-1, which\h activates TAK1. From here,\h\h the pathway looks similar to the MyD88\h pathway, and results in NF-kB activation.\h\h TRAF3, on the other hand, recruits TBK1 and IKKi.\h Together, these phosphorylate and activate IRF3.\h\h Activated IRF3 forms a dimer and then moves into\h the nucleus, where it drives expression of type I\h\h interferons, which are cytokines that\h are critical for antiviral responses.\h\h Different TLRs use different adaptors or\h combinations of adaptors, which allows the cell to\h\h tailor its response to the type of threat at hand.

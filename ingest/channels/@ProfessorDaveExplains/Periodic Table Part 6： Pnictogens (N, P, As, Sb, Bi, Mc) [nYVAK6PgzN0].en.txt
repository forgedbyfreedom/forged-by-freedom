Beyond\h nitrogen, this group includes phosphorus, arsenic,\h\h antimony, bismuth, and moscovium. These elements\h have electron configurations ending in ns2np3, and\h\h metallic character increases going down the group,\h as does atomic radius, while ionization energy\h\h decreases. Phosphorus\h can be found in ores containing phosphate,\h\h like apatite, more than half of which is located\h in Arab nations, but also China, Russia, Morocco,\h\h and the United States. Structurally speaking, nitrogen is the simplest of\h\h these elements. It will form a diatomic molecule\h which is a gas at room temperature, and there are\h\h no other allotropes. Phosphorus, on the other\h hand, has three allotropes. It can form P2 gas,\h\h just like nitrogen, and this is quite flammable.\h It can also exist as P4, where each phosphorus\h\h is bound to all of the others to form a pyramid.\h This is a white solid that ignites spontaneously\h\h when exposed to air, and is a key component of\h certain incendiary weapons used in war. And it\h\h can exist as red, purple, or black phosphorus,\h which are different arrangements of P4 subunits.\h\h Arsenic also has three allotropes, yellow, gray,\h and black. The yellow is structurally analogous\h\h to white phosphorus, with the same pyramid\h shape. Gray is metallic and the most stable,\h\h and therefore the most common. Black is\h amorphous, similar to red phosphorus.\h\h Meanwhile, antimony and bismuth\h are both simply low-melting metals.\h So what do these elements do?

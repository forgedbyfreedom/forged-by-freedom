Moving along in our journey through Spiralia,\h we arrive at the phylum Chaetognatha,\h\h or bristle-jaws. The animals that make up this\h phylum are commonly known as “arrow worms.” They\h\h are famous, or perhaps infamous, for a number of\h reasons. First, they are morphologically distinct,\h\h voracious predators with unique\h methods of hunting and feeding.\h\h Second, they play an important ecological role\h that is often overlooked or outright completely\h\h ignored in most biology courses, though they are\h commonly addressed as important members of the\h\h food web in more advanced planktology courses. And\h three, they are some of the most phylogenetically\h\h confounding creatures on our planet. Let’s start\h with their physical features and well-known\h\h appetites, and then work towards their taxonomy. Arrow worms are bilaterally symmetrical coelomates\h\h with transparent, or translucent bodies. They\h are dart or arrow-shaped worm-like animals\h\h partially protected by a collagenous cuticle,\h capable of short bursts of speed, hence the name,\h\h “arrow worms”. Their bodies can be\h divided into three distinct regions:\h\h the head, trunk, and tail. These body regions\h are separated both externally and internally.\h Their head region terminates with the neck,\h under which the animal bears a mouth that is\h\h surrounded by sets of grasping spines which are\h used in capturing prey. This mouth is located on\h\h the ventral side of the head, which along with the\h entire head, can be enclosed by a protective hood.\h\h Like the flatworms, they lack any type of discrete\h gas exchange or urinary systems, and instead rely\h\h on diffusion achieved by the epidermis and gut\h system. However, like most bilaterians, they have\h\h a complete gut and a ventral anus. They have a set\h of cup-shaped eyes and a peculiar central nervous\h\h system consisting of a dorsal brain and ganglionic\h rings encircling different parts of the gut\h\h system. The first ring is confined to the head and\h contains paired vestibular and esophageal ganglia,\h\h as well as an unpaired suboesophageal ganglion\h surrounding the foregut. The second ring encloses\h\h the midgut and connects the brain with the ventral\h ganglion, which regulates the twitchy swimming\h\h motion which is typical for chaetognaths. All\h known species of arrow-worms are hermaphroditic.\h\h They have their testes in their tail region, and\h their ovaries are located at the posterior end\h\h of their trunk. Some species are bioluminescent,\h some live deep in the ocean on benthic sediments,\h\h and some inhabit shallow water sediments such as\h sand. Some have demonstrated unique mating dances,\h\h and others like Spadella cephaloptera, spend\h most of the day attached to seagrass leaves.\h Chaetognaths carry a variety of sense organs in\h their thick, multilayered epidermis. They have\h\h eyes, but the biological role of vision for them\h is not yet known. What is well known, however,\h\h is that they rely mainly on mechanical vibrations\h for information about their environment and,\h\h thus, can hunt in total darkness. They do\h this by sensing vibrations through their\h\h fans of mechanoreceptive hairs, and at\h least one species, Spadella cephaloptera,\h\h will attack a metal or glass probe vibrating\h at an appropriate frequency and amplitude,\h\h with the same ferocity as it will attack\h a copepod, its primary food source.\h Speaking of food sources, arrow worms have\h been documented to swallow their prey whole\h\h and consume anything they can fit\h in their mouths. After sensing prey,\h\h an arrow worm will suddenly shoot forward and\h use its chitinous spines and teeth to capture it.\h\h In addition to their imposing jaws, quick bursts\h of speed, and sensory fans, some species of arrow\h\h worms have been found to harbor the extremely\h potent neurotoxin known as tetrodotoxin. However,\h\h it is not yet clear if the arrow worms are capable\h of injecting small doses of the toxin from their\h\h chaetae, or if the bacteria are simply symbionts\h that live in and around the arrow worm spines.\h\h The neurotoxin seems to be produced by Vibrio\h alginolyticus, the very same pathogen infamously\h\h present in the bodies of pufferfish that makes\h them a deadly meal if not properly prepared.\h In warm waters, arrow worms are often the\h most abundant planktonic predators, sometimes\h\h accounting for more than 10% of zooplankton\h biomass. With such a high abundance, it is\h\h perhaps unsurprising that they play an important\h ecological role. Like many planktonic organisms,\h\h chaetognaths migrate up and down in the water\h column. They come to the surface to feed at night,\h\h then retire to deeper waters as the sun rises.\h Unlike other planktonic organisms, though,\h\h some chaetognaths migrate annually. They\h live in the surface waters during the winter\h\h but sink to deeper waters in summer. As such, they\h play an important role in the vertical oceanic\h\h carbon flux. Essentially, their migratory life\h cycle transports carbon up and down in the ocean,\h\h while their fecal pellets transport organic\h matter downward in the form of “marine snow.”\h\h Thus, they, like most planktonic organisms, are\h an important part of the oceanic carbon cycle and\h\h even play a role in global carbon sequestration. In fact, it is possible that they have been an\h\h important ecological component of our planet for\h over 500 million years. The oldest fossil records\h\h of arrow worms known so far date back to the\h Cambrian period, 541 to 485 million years ago,\h\h making them some of the oldest members of\h Bilateria, or Metazoa. Interestingly enough,\h\h these specimens that appear in the so-called\h Burgess shale are extremely morphologically\h\h similar to modern arrow worms, meaning they have\h changed very little over their half-a-billion-year\h\h tenure on our planet. Currently, over\h 132 extant species have been discovered,\h\h though some taxonomists put that number closer\h to 190. This number is very likely to increase\h\h over the coming years, not least because\h the species diversity of benthic spadellids\h\h is far away from being entirely resolved. Lastly, let’s discuss arrow worm classification.\h\h Looking at our diagram of animal phyla we can see\h that the arrow worms sit within Spiralia. This,\h\h like the diagram itself, is based on\h the book The Invertebrate Tree of Life,\h\h by Gonzalo Giribet and Gregory D. Edgecombe. These\h researchers have placed the arrow worms there\h\h due in part to phylogenomic analyses, namely an\h analysis of Hox genes across the protostomes,\h\h and the morphological similarities of the\h grasping spines of the arrow worms to the jaw-like\h\h structures of the gnathiferans, which we will\h discuss momentarily. So we have them placed here,\h\h as a sister group to the gnathiferans.\h However, they weren’t always placed like this.\h Part of the notoriety of these animals\h comes from the fact that for quite some time\h\h scientists were unsure which of their traits were\h simply derived or apomorphic characters, those\h\h that arose since their common ancestry, and which\h were truly ancestral traits that could be used to\h\h classify them. Charles Darwin himself highlighted\h two interesting aspects of the arrow worms.\h\h First, their ecological abundance, which we\h have already covered, and second, their odd\h\h anatomy which makes them difficult to place. Due in part to their apparent three-part body\h\h plan and their post-anal tail, when they\h were first discovered, they were placed\h\h close to phylum Chordata, and subsequent studies\h placed them as a phylum within Deuterostomia.\h\h However, their unique method of development cast\h doubt on that placement. Unlike all deuterostomes,\h\h the blastopore of the arrow worms closes up\h entirely during embryogenesis, and the anus\h\h forms later in development from the posterior end.\h This caused researchers to then consider the arrow\h\h worms as members of Bilateria, but as neither\h protostomes nor deuterostomes. This implied that\h\h chaetognaths were most likely an early offshoot of\h bilaterians rooting themselves in the tree of life\h\h close by protostomes and deuterostomes. But this thinking continued to be revised.\h\h In 1996, they were considered a sister group to\h the nematodes. In 1998, to members of Ecdysozoa,\h\h or as a sister group to all protostomes. However,\h more recent studies have found these placements\h\h to be inaccurate. The most recent studies support\h the placement of the arrow worms within spiralia,\h\h as a sister group to Gnathifera, though\h admittedly there are researchers that\h\h still disagree with this placement. Only time\h will tell if the evidence of subsequent studies\h\h will unify the field around this designation. Such\h studies are still ongoing, and it is likely that\h\h we will learn much more about these enigmatic\h creatures in the years to come. But, for now,\h\h let’s leave the arrow worms behind.

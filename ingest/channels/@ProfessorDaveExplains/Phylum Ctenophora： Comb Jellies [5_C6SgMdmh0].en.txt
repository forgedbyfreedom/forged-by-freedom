In this series we’ve been working our way through\h the various animal phyla, and by now, you’ve\h\h probably realized the term “jellies” gets thrown\h around a lot. Only members of phylum Cnidaria,\h\h class Scyphozoa are true jellyfish, which\h we’ve already covered. This means that the\h\h “comb jellies” of phylum Ctenophora may resemble\h jellyfish but are completely different organisms.\h The name “Ctenophora” means “comb-bearer,” which\h is how this group of animals gets its common name.\h\h All living comb jellies have\h comb-like plates of cilia\h\h used for locomotion. These combs are arranged\h in eight equally-spaced bands called comb rows,\h\h which extend from a few centimeters before the\h mouth to a few centimeters before the anus.\h\h Ctenophores, unlike the Cnidarians, Placozoans,\h and Poriferians that we’ve examined so far, have\h\h a distinct mouth and two anal pores. The mouth of\h a comb jelly leads into a pharynx which empties\h\h into a branching digestive tract that ends in anal\h pores. Ctenophores normally move their combs so\h\h that the propulsion stroke is away from the mouth,\h meaning that ctenophores, unlike cnidarians,\h\h usually swim in the direction in which the\h mouth is eating, while the anus trails behind.\h\h All ctenophores live in saltwater environments,\h and they can exist from the surface waters\h\h all the way down into the deep ocean. Many species\h demonstrate vertical migration through the water\h\h column based on day-night cycles, where they\h come to the surface to feed during the day\h\h and return to deeper waters at night, where many\h emit flashes of luminescence. During the day,\h\h most are colorless and almost transparent, but\h their comb rows produce a rainbow-like effect\h\h that is due to the prismatic scattering\h of light as the combs move. In addition,\h\h a few species can also produce secretions of\h luminescent ink in order to confuse attackers.\h Ctenophores have a range of body types.\h However, some of the most commonly studied\h\h are the Cydippids, Lobates, and Beroids.\h Cydippids, like the sea gooseberry,\h\h are spherical with two long tentacles that drag in\h the water and capture prey in epidermal glue cells\h\h known as colloblasts. These colloblasts\h contain an adhesive glue that binds to prey.\h\h In addition, some cydippids that feed on jellyfish\h are capable of incorporating their prey’s stinging\h\h nematocysts into their own tentacles. Lobates,\h like the sea walnut, have muscular cuplike\h\h extensions of the body that extend beyond the\h mouth. Their tentacles originate from the corners\h\h of the mouth, and run in convoluted grooves that\h spread out over the inner surface of the lobes.\h\h Beroids, like the cigar comb jelly, have\h massive pharynxes lined with large, hardened,\h\h fused cilia known as macrocilia that can be\h used to tear into prey too large to swallow.\h\h Their large mouths can be sealed shut\h by forming intercellular connections\h\h when not feeding or when digesting prey. Some other highly modified ctenophore body types\h\h include the compressed and ribbon-shaped “band\h animals,” like the Venus girdle, the medusa-like\h\h Thalassocalyce, and the flattened,\h creeping Platyctenida that lack comb rows.\h The germ layers of the diploblastic\h ctenophores are similar to the cnidarians\h\h except that instead of a mesoglea that separates\h the epidermis and gastrodermis, ctenophores have a\h\h collenchyme that contains the muscle cells and\h amoeboid cells. In addition, ctenophores are\h\h considered to be biradial since their arrangement\h of internal canals and tentacles, in many species,\h\h change their symmetry from radial into\h a combination of radial and bilateral.\h Comb jellies have a nerve network which forms\h a ring around the mouth, and is concentrated\h\h under each comb plate. Though they have no\h brains, or eyes, they do have abundant sensory\h\h cells that line their epidermis, and a large\h aboral organ opposite the mouth that functions\h\h as a statocyst, or balance sensory receptor. Most ctenophores, except for 3 known species,\h\h are monoecious, which again means that any\h individual can create both types of gametes\h\h within its own body. Gonads are stored on\h the lining of the gastrovascular canals\h\h under the comb plates, and gametes, or sperm and\h eggs, are released during synchronous spawning.\h\h Eggs are generally fertilized outside of the body,\h but some species, like the sea gooseberry, use\h\h internal fertilization and keep the eggs in brood\h chambers until they hatch. Self-fertilization has\h\h occasionally been seen in the sea walnuts,\h and it is possible that most monoecious comb\h\h jellies are capable of self-fertilization. In any case, the larval forms of most species\h\h are very similar in appearance and behavior to\h the adults, just smaller. Adult ctenophores will\h\h produce gametes while they have a ready supply of\h food, but if food grows scarce, they will first\h\h halt production of gametes, then shrink their\h bodies. If food becomes readily available again,\h\h they will reverse the process by expanding their\h bodies and then resuming production of gametes.\h\h And with the ctenophores covered, we’ve\h now discussed all of the non-triploblastic\h\h animal phyla.

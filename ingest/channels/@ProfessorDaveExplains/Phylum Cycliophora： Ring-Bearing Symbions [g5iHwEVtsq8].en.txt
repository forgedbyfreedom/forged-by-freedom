So far in our investigation of Spiralia\h we have covered the phyla Platyhelminthes\h\h and Gastrotricha. Let’s now move on to their\h close relatives in phylum Cycliophora. The\h\h fascinating creatures which inhabit this clade\h were first discovered only very recently in 1995,\h\h by Dr. Peter Funch, who has graciously\h assisted in the writing of this tutorial,\h\h as well as Dr. Reinhardt Møbjerg Kristensen,\h whose assistance we will enjoy when we cover the\h\h phylum Micrognathozoa a bit later in the series. Now, we’ve covered some strange animals so far,\h\h but the microscopic sac-like cycliophorans,\h also known as Symbions or “ring-bearers”\h\h just might be the strangest. They, like the\h flatworms and Gastrotrichs, are bilaterally\h\h symmetrical, meaning they can be split into left\h and right halves which are rough mirror images.\h\h They are triploblastic, meaning they have three\h unique germ cell layers. They are unsegmented,\h\h meaning they do not demonstrate metamerism.\h They are generally noncolonial, meaning they\h\h do not form interconnected colonies, though\h their unique feeding stage with a developing\h\h buccal funnel inside can be regarded as\h two individuals. And they are acoelomates,\h\h meaning they do not have a body cavity, and\h lack respiratory organs. The fluid inside\h\h their brood chambers is circulated by cilia. They comprise a phylum of incredibly tiny animals\h\h that measure less than a half a millimeter wide\h at their largest, and live their lives attached\h\h to the mouthparts of cold-water lobsters in the\h North Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea.\h\h They are considered to have a commensalist\h relationship with their lobster hosts\h\h since they feed by driving uneaten food\h particles from the lobster into their\h\h digestive systems though ciliary action. They do\h not appear to harm their host lobster in any way.\h Cycliophorans are deeply connected to their\h host. Their unique life cycle is due, in part,\h\h to the fact that they live primarily\h upon the cuticle attached to the setae,\h\h or the bristle-like extensions, of an animal\h that continually molts. They have six basic\h\h life stages: the asexual feeding stage, the\h sexual male stage, the sexual female stage,\h\h the pandora stage, the Prometheus\h larva, and the chordoid larva.\h Let’s follow the life journey of a single\h cycliophoran starting with a settled larva.\h\h This larva will develop into a feeding individual\h with an inner bud. At this point a few different\h\h things can happen. First, let’s follow the asexual\h stage. A small organism, known as a pandora larva,\h\h can escape from the feeding individual\h and colonize the same lobster host.\h\h These larvae are produced in the intermolt period\h of the host. In this way, the pandora larva can\h\h colonize the same lobster. This larva will then\h settle and develop back into a feeding stage.\h A feeding stage individual can potentially\h develop to hold a male larva released as a\h\h Prometheus larva that settles onto another\h feeding individual. The settled juvenile\h\h male will then further develop one to three\h sexually mature males inside its own body,\h\h each of which is only about one third of its\h size, meaning the sexually mature males are\h\h smaller than the juvenile males and are one of the\h smallest known animals in the world. Meanwhile,\h\h other feeding stages develop to hold developing\h females. Each female contains a single oocyte.\h\h Once fertilized, the pregnant female escapes and\h settles into a sheltered area of the same host.\h\h It then encysts and produces a single motile\h chordoid larva that hatches from the cyst and\h\h settles onto a new host, where it develops into\h a feeding stage and initiates its asexual cycle.\h Due to their unique life cycle and cryptic\h nature, the exact phylogeny of the cycliophorans\h\h has been debated for quite some time. Proposed\h homologies have placed the cycliophorans as\h\h cousins to Rotifera, kin to Bryozoans, and closely\h related to Entoprocta.

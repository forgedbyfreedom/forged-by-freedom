In the previous tutorial, we introduced\h homogeneous catalysis, a process in which\h\h both catalyst and reactants are in the same\h phase, usually the liquid phase. The reaction\h\h media typically consist of reactants, catalyst,\h and solvent. Application of homogeneous catalysis\h\h is mainly in the area of complex molecules,\h such as pharmaceuticals or fine chemicals,\h\h where high selectivity and mild conditions\h are imperative. In the chemical industry,\h\h much simpler products are typically prepared, and\h these can accept much more robust conditions. The\h\h type of catalysis usually exploited in the\h chemical industry is called heterogeneous\h\h catalysis, because the catalyst is in a\h different phase from the reactants. Usually,\h\h the catalyst is part of a solid phase,\h and reactants are in solution or in the\h\h gas phase. The main advantage of this type of\h catalysis is the stability of the catalysts,\h\h which can usually be recycled, given that they\h do not have to be recovered from solution.\h However, the mechanism of this type of catalysis\h is more complex and less straightforward to depict\h\h than for the catalytic cycles we just saw.\h Catalytic cycles in this context also include\h\h several steps. Of course, the molecules first\h have to diffuse towards the catalytic surface.\h\h In most cases, they will then become adsorbed on\h the surface. Remember, adsorption is different\h\h from absorption, in that absorption involves\h the transfer of particles into another material,\h\h like water into a sponge, while adsorption\h involves the adhesion of particles onto the\h\h surface of another material. Adsorption consists\h of a variety of attractive forces, ranging from\h\h very weak van der Waals forces to very strong\h forces, in some cases actual covalent attachment\h\h of the adsorbed molecule with cleavage of a former\h bond, as is the case with hydrogen gas during\h\h hydrogenation. In many cases, one reactant can be\h more strongly adsorbed than the other, and in rare\h\h cases adsorption activates only one of the two\h reactants, and this is sufficient for catalysis.\h The key step in the cycle is the reaction of\h the species involved, which must be adsorbed\h\h at vicinal sites, which is made possible given\h their capacity for surface migration, or the\h\h ability to move along the catalytic surface once\h adsorbed. The details of this type of reaction\h\h are challenging to pinpoint. After the reaction,\h the product must quickly desorb and re-enter the\h\h bulk phase. Clearly, the secret to effective\h catalysis is a strong adsorption process,\h\h but this interaction cannot be too strong,\h otherwise the reactants will be too stable\h\h to react. In addition, the product should not be\h too strongly adsorbed to the catalyst, otherwise\h\h product molecules will coat the surface and block\h reactant molecules from adsorbing. Balancing these\h\h tendencies is usually done by trial and error. An important goal of heterogeneous catalysis\h\h is to maximize the contact area between\h the catalytic metal and the reactants,\h\h because only the external layer of bulk\h metal participates in catalysis. Therefore,\h\h active metals are often deposited on an inert\h surface in a layer as thin as possible, so as\h\h to maximize the utilization of the precious metal.\h Active surfaces and inert supports can be combined\h\h in a large number of ways to produce a huge\h variety of potential catalysts, which are often\h\h tested empirically for a reaction of interest. One important difference between homogeneous and\h\h heterogeneous catalysis is the fact that\h in the former, all catalyst molecules,\h\h disregarding decomposition, are identical and\h all behave the same way, which makes reactions\h\h easier to characterize and reproduce. Surfaces,\h however, are not easy to construct reproducibly.\h\h Defects are common, and they actually make a\h very important contribution to catalysis. The\h\h formation of defects leads to variability in the\h reactivity of each single catalyst molecule or\h\h atom. A solid defect is defined as the distortion\h of the crystal structure vs. the ideal lattice\h\h structure. Defect structures always form in solids\h and can be subdivided into a variety of types.\h\h The analysis of lattice defects in catalysis is\h actually a fertile research area in itself. Here\h\h we can see a few of the typical defects found in\h catalytic surfaces: point defects, line defects,\h\h and bulk defects, which describe different\h ways that the crystal structure can be altered.\h These and other types of defects result in\h variability for the catalytic performance\h\h of solid catalysts. Modern nanofabrication\h techniques are resulting in the formation\h\h of much improved surfaces, with more precise\h control at the single atom level. Single-atom\h\h catalysis is touted as a brand new exciting\h field which in the near future may provide\h\h us with much improved heterogeneous catalysts. Whereas solid catalysts are in general much more\h\h stable than homogeneous catalysts, they also have\h a finite life expectancy, which of course depends\h\h on the chemistry they are performing. Fouling is\h caused by the deposition of decomposed reactant\h\h materials on the surface of the catalyst, and\h coating the surface reduces catalytic performance.\h\h The surface can modify itself on heating, since\h active particles can be displaced by particles of\h\h solid support rising to the surface, a process\h called solid-phase transition. In addition,\h\h metal particles can be lost from the surface\h into the bulk phase. This is called leaching,\h\h and sometimes the leached particles have activity\h of their own. Redeposition never occurs at the\h\h exact site where the particle was leached, and\h this results in a surface displaying variable\h\h reactivity. If the leached particles are lost\h permanently, the result is an erosion of catalytic\h\h performance. Given the importance of heterogeneous\h catalysis, the construction of improved catalytic\h\h surfaces is a very important area of research. A popular area of heterogeneous catalysis is a\h\h hybrid with homogeneous catalysis. We have seen\h that ligands can tune the reactivity of transition\h\h metals, leading to highly selective processes\h under mild conditions, and sometimes even highly\h\h enantioselective processes, if the ligands are\h chiral. A way to combine the two areas is to\h\h covalently link these ligands to a solid support,\h so that the catalyst can easily be recycled at\h\h the end. This area is currently enjoying a lot\h of popularity. However, the fragility of many\h\h metal/ligand combinations we see in homogeneous\h catalysts extends to the immobilized versions, and\h\h often the overall turnover number is not improved\h by attaching the ligands to the solid supports.\h The fact that catalysts are in a different\h phase with respect to the reactants leads\h\h to the frequent application of heterogeneous\h catalysis in flow instead of batch. Homogeneous\h\h catalysis is usually carried out in batch, meaning\h that all reactants and the catalyst are added to\h\h the reactor, the conditions are adjusted, and at\h the end the product is isolated in the work-up,\h\h whereas the catalyst is lost. In flow\h chemistry, the reactants are allowed to flow,\h\h either by the action of a gas or a solvent,\h through a fixed bed hosting the catalyst,\h\h and this is done at such a rate that molecules\h exiting the bed are fully reacted. This permits\h\h a continuous production of material and is\h advantageous in terms of speed and cost.\h To conclude, whereas homogeneous catalysis is\h much more powerful in terms of the variety of\h\h operations it can perform under mild conditions,\h up to 90% of the products of the chemical industry\h\h involve heterogeneous catalysis. So we can safely\h say that homogeneous catalysis has a much broader\h\h scope, whereas heterogeneous catalysis accounts\h for much larger product volumes.

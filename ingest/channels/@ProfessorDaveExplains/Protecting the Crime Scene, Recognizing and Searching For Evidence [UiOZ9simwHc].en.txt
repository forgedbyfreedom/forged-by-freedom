In the previous tutorial, we listed the different\h principles of forensic science and their relevance\h\h in investigating a crime scene. Proper processing\h of the scene is important, as it serves as the\h\h foundation for subsequent analysis of evidence\h in the laboratory. This analysis would be\h\h misleading if the proper protocol for CSI is\h not followed. CSI is a simple and methodical\h\h process, but it doesn’t have to be the same for\h each crime scene. It is essential to remember\h\h that the only thing that is consistent about\h crime scenes is their inconsistencies. All\h\h crime scenes are unique. Hence, CSI differs from\h scene to scene. Remember the law of individuality.\h\h The only requirement is that it should take into\h account all the principles we mentioned earlier. A\h\h crime scene, or CS, can be classified into primary\h CS and secondary CS. This classification does not\h\h reflect priority or importance, but rather the\h sequence of the events. Let us consider this\h\h example: a child is kidnapped from a school\h and then murdered by the riverside. Here,\h\h the school is the primary CS and the riverside\h is the secondary CS. Other classifications will\h\h be based on location: indoor and outdoor; type\h of crime committed; and the size of the CS. Even\h\h with these classifications, it is not possible\h to adopt a single method to investigate a CS.\h\h The investigators constantly adapt depending on\h two essential factors: logic and feasibility.\h\h Logic refers to determining how the event would\h have taken place at the CS, and feasibility\h\h refers to determining the extent to which the\h particular adopted method could produce results.\h Crime investigation is broadly\h divided into two important stages:\h\h CSI and laboratory investigation. CSI\h involves steps such as protection of the CS,\h\h followed by recognition, searching, collection,\h packaging, and transportation of the evidence\h\h to the laboratory. After applying suitable\h scientific techniques for analyzing the evidence,\h\h their results are interpreted and presented in the\h courtroom in the form of expert testimony. All of\h\h these stages are important in investigation\h and the efficacy of each stage will depend\h\h on the steps taken in the previous ones.\h Let us look at each one of them in detail.\h The first stage in CSI is the protection of\h the CS. The main goal is to uphold Locard’s Law\h\h of Exchange, meaning to secure physical evidence\h that could establish the forensic linkage triangle\h\h between crime scene, victims, and suspects. It is\h not necessary for the first responding officers at\h\h the CS to be police officers. They can be medical\h emergency personnel and fire department personnel\h\h as well. FROs are the only personnel who view the\h CS in its original condition. But before thinking\h\h further about the CS, their primary job is to\h attend to the safety of the people involved at\h\h the scene. Once the safety of everyone involved\h is guaranteed, the FROs notify the nearest police\h\h station about the crime. The FROs try to keep as\h much of an open and objective mind as possible and\h\h try to understand the CS according to their\h own experience. The first step is to assist\h\h the victim. If the victim is dead, the body\h should not be displaced before all evidence\h\h documentation is completed. All the witnesses\h involved are detained. To maintain objectivity,\h\h it is advised to keep them separate from\h each other. Many suspects have the innate\h\h urge to return to the CS once the crime has\h been committed. From the witnesses accounts,\h\h if the suspect is still present at the CS, the\h FROs attempt to search for and apprehend the\h\h suspect. This is followed by securing the CS.\h However, securing can be initiated as soon as\h\h possible if there is no scarcity of staff. This\h can be done with the help of physical barriers\h\h that would prevent other unauthorized people from\h entering the scene. These could be barricading\h\h tape, traffic cones, containers, or even other\h vehicles. After establishing such barriers,\h\h an officer is designated as the Scene\h Security Officer. They maintain a log of\h\h everyone entering and exiting the scene. Simply\h curious onlookers, detectives, police officers,\h\h media, and others should not be allowed at the\h CS at all. Care should be taken that none of the\h\h personnel eats, drinks, or smokes at the CS. As\h these officers are not formally trained in CSI,\h\h their actions depend more on their experience at\h such scenes. Thus, according to their experience,\h\h they should note everything that distinguishes\h the CS from others. There is a class of evidence\h\h known as transient evidence which degrades in\h a short time and cannot be documented in the\h\h later stages. Examples of this evidence include\h peculiar odors, temperature, and impressions on\h\h perishable items. An odor of gasoline may be\h gone when the investigators arrive. It is the\h\h job of the FRO to note and communicate it to the\h investigator. If an FRO notes that the smell of\h\h gasoline is prominent at the CS, it gets easier\h for the investigators to search for the linked\h\h evidence accordingly. Seemingly insignificant\h details, such as the initial position of doors,\h\h windows, light switches, furniture, etc. must also\h be noted. Along with this, the FRO notes all the\h\h movements and alterations made at the CS. It is\h not uncommon to find people at the CS attempting\h\h to clean it up. They must be interrogated\h thoroughly to gather information regarding the\h\h conditions prior to the cleanup. If anyone from\h the staff unwillingly leaves behind a fingerprint\h\h or footwear print, the personnel must not try\h to remove it. The FRO should just document the\h\h contamination and share it with the investigating\h team. Thus, a CS is termed as secure when only\h\h authorized personnel are allowed to enter the\h scene, at which point proper entry and exit,\h\h as well as contamination logs, are maintained. The FRO’s duties are over when the investigating\h\h team arrives. However, they must stay at the\h CS until the investigation gets completed. The\h\h FRO assists the investigators in the initial\h walkthrough, to evaluate the protected areas\h\h and secure other areas, if necessary. The\h lead investigator benefits immensely from\h\h the walk-through, as it helps them in getting\h a second-hand account of the CS, as observed by\h\h the FRO. This description along the walk-through\h helps them in formulating a reconstruction theory\h\h regarding how the event must have transpired.\h All possible points of entry and exits as well\h\h as the paths of travel missed by the FRO must be\h secured. The investigator has to be painstakingly\h\h careful and vigilant at this stage and protect\h the scene accordingly. There have been many cases\h\h where evidence that seems unimportant at first,\h gains importance in the later stages of the\h\h investigation. If such evidence is destroyed or\h contaminated because the scene was unsecured, then\h\h the investigation gets jeopardized. Therefore,\h the investigator must keep an open mind. After\h\h the assessment of the CS, the investigator\h roughly calculates the manpower and logistics\h\h required in later stages of investigation.\h They then communicate to the senior officials\h\h this estimate for the number of personnel,\h equipment required, and precautions needed.\h The second stage of CSI is recognition\h of the evidence. This precedes actual\h\h searching and collection of evidence. Recognizing\h evidence is one of the hardest steps to perform,\h\h as the investigator has to take great care not\h to miss any important evidence. At the same time,\h\h they should also discard materials irrelevant\h to the CS. As we’ve already seen, some evidence\h\h might gain importance in the later stages of CSI.\h Therefore, the grounds on which to distinguish a\h\h piece of evidence as important or unimportant\h are very ambiguous. Like the other stages,\h\h there are no hard and fast rules in this stage.\h As a result, recognition primarily depends on the\h\h investigator’s own experience. There is no need\h to rush unless some rapidly perishable evidence\h\h is involved. A mental reconstruction of the\h events that may have occurred helps immensely\h\h for recognizing evidence linked to the crime.\h For example, if this is a scene concerning\h\h murder by a firearm, then cartridges, bullets,\h gunshot residue, bloodstains, fingerprints,\h\h footwear prints at entry and exit, can all be\h considered crucial evidence. Once this evidence\h\h is recognized, the investigators move on to\h searching for and later collecting them. However,\h\h evidence that is important for one crime scene\h might be insignificant in others. Therefore,\h\h extreme foresight is needed on the part of\h the investigator. This foresight extends into\h\h laboratory investigation as well. If the right\h questions are posed in the laboratory, the most\h\h useful answers will arrive. If a scientist is made\h aware of the facts of the case, they will analyze\h\h the evidence more appropriately than just another\h routine request for analysis. To achieve this,\h\h proper documentation of the evidence and their\h communication to the laboratory are required.\h The third stage of CSI is searching for\h the evidence that is recognized in the\h\h previous stage. Searching for and collection\h of evidence are often known as the backbone\h\h of CSI. The primary objective of searching is to\h establish linkage between the perpetrator, victim,\h\h and the CS, and answer significant questions such\h as: Who committed the crime? How was the crime\h\h committed? Why was the crime committed? Which\h circumstances led to the commission of the crime?\h\h During recognition of evidence, there is a chance\h that some evidence might go unrecognized. However,\h\h while undertaking a CS search, reconstructing the\h scene mentally as evidence is being discovered may\h\h indicate that some items are missing. These items\h are sought after. Statements from the witnesses,\h\h notes taken by the FRO and the lead investigator,\h preliminary medical reports, these all contribute\h\h to the ongoing search. Here, it is important\h to search only for what is sought. If any other\h\h insignificant substances are searched for, it may\h turn out to be a waste of time. Actual important\h\h evidence might get lost, contaminated, or\h destroyed in the process. This is where we\h\h realize how important it is to recognize evidence\h in the first place. Therefore, it is paramount for\h\h the lead investigator to brief each searcher on\h exactly what to do, and most importantly, what not\h\h to do. When a piece of evidence is obtained during\h the search, it may be tempting for the searcher to\h\h touch, handle, or move it. However, such actions\h must be strictly prohibited. Searchers must only\h\h mark and designate evidence for further stages,\h without touching it. CS searches are usually\h\h carried out by a small number of knowledgeable and\h skilled personnel. With the number of searchers\h\h being less significant, the investigator must\h decide on the search pattern to be used at the\h\h particular CS. No single method is specific for\h a CS, and an experienced investigator will be\h\h able to adopt a search pattern most suitable\h for the CS. Commonly used search methods are:\h\h line or strip, grid, zone, wheel or ray,\h spiral, and link. Each one has its own set\h\h of pros and cons. The actual search can also\h be a combination of these different patterns.\h\h Let’s learn about each one of them in detail. The first method of search is the line or strip\h\h method. The CS is divided into parallel rectangles\h and the searchers look for clues by walking in a\h\h straight line. As soon as a piece of evidence is\h located, they inform the search coordinator. After\h\h one rectangle is completed, they move on to the\h next, and so on until the whole scene is covered.\h\h This method works best outdoors. The second method\h of search is the grid method. This is similar to\h\h the line method, the only difference being that\h the horizontal and vertical line searches overlap\h\h each other, resembling a grid. This type of\h search is very effective as evidence missed\h\h in the vertical line search might get discovered\h in the horizontal. Different searchers will have\h\h a different perspective to look at the CS,\h thus leading to the detection of previously\h\h unnoticed evidence. However, the grid method\h is very time-consuming, which can lead to decay\h\h and contamination of perishable clues. The third\h method of search is the quadrant or zone method.\h\h Here, the CS is divided into different quadrants\h or zones, and a different team of searchers is\h\h assigned to each section. The individual zones\h can also be further divided into lines or a grid\h\h to make the search easier. This method is usually\h employed for an indoors CS. A house or a factory\h\h can be effectively divided into zones according\h to the rooms and compartments. Therefore,\h\h this is the preferred method in the case of search\h warrants. The fourth method of search is the wheel\h\h or ray method. Here, the searchers start their\h search from the center of the crime scene and\h\h continue along the radii. They can repeat this\h search as many times as required. This method\h\h has limited applications and is typically used\h for small and circular crime scenes. The fifth\h\h method of search is the spiral method. Often used\h for a large outdoor CS, it can be classified as\h\h either an inward spiral or an outward spiral. In\h the case of a CS with no physical barriers, an\h\h outward spiral method is used. Here, the searchers\h start from the center of the CS and move along a\h\h spiral outwards until no more significant clues\h are found. When the CS is large but within certain\h\h limits, the inward spiral is used. In this case,\h the search is started from a point just outside\h\h the CS, which is continued in an inward spiral\h until the searchers reach the center of the CS.\h\h This method ensures maximum coverage of the CS. It\h can also be used for searching a special CS such\h\h as a search involving a drowning case in a lake.\h The sixth and last method of search is the link\h\h method. Like the others, this does not refer to\h any geometrical shape. It is based on the linkage\h\h theory, where finding one piece of evidence leads\h to the search for another. As a result, the logic,\h\h reasoning, and experience of the investigator play\h an important role here. This method works for all\h\h types of CS and can be used in combination\h with any of the previous search patterns.\h\h If the investigator does not find something\h which they feel should be logically present,\h\h they should look for it more thoroughly. At a CS, nothing is insignificant. The tiniest\h\h piece of evidence must catch the attention of\h the searchers. The investigator must guarantee\h\h that the searchers are wearing enough protective\h gear, from head to toe. This serves two purposes:\h\h to secure the CS from contamination, as well\h as to ensure the staff don’t get affected\h\h by toxic and biological evidence present at\h the CS. As evidence is discovered along with\h\h the search, they are marked with different tags\h according to a letter or a number series.

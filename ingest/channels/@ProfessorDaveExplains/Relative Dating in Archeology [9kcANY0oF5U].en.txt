Radiometric\h dating of entire sites just isn’t viable. Because\h\h of this, absolute dating is usually combined\h with relative dating to make chronologies\h\h of sites. Let's look at how this is done. The primary manner of collecting artifacts\h\h is through excavation. We will cover excavation\h techniques more fully a bit later in the series.\h\h But for now, let's go over the basics.\h When archeologists excavate, they start\h\h from the top, taking off layers of soil to reach\h some desired depth, often either a feature or a\h\h layer of soil with no human occupation. Collecting\h artifacts in this way is important because it lets\h\h archeologists utilize a very important geological\h concept known as the Law of Superposition. The Law\h\h of Superposition states that sediment is deposited\h in horizontal layers, and newer layers are always\h\h deposited on top. This means that the deeper the\h layer, the older it will be. These layers are\h\h called stratigraphic layers, and the investigation\h and interpretation of them is called stratigraphy.\h This is important for a few reasons.\h First, if we find a stone tool in a layer\h\h below a piece of pottery, we know by the Law of\h Superposition that the stone tool is older than\h\h the pottery. Think back to that chronology of our\h hypothetical site. Once again, it went like this:\h\h stone tools are made, a temple is constructed, a\h village is built, the village is burnt, the site\h\h is abandoned. What might that have looked like in\h the ground? In the lowest layers, we find stone\h\h tools. On top of those stone tools we find remains\h of a temple. On top of the remains of that temple,\h\h we find the remains of houses, and so on. If the\h law of superposition tells us that artifacts in\h\h different layers have different ages, that means\h artifacts in the same layer are the same age.\h\h If we find deer bones in the same stratigraphic\h layer as the stone tools, we know that they date\h\h to the same time period as the stone tools. This\h is where absolute dating methods can be combined\h\h with relative dating methods to make accurate and\h useful site chronologies. Since the deer bones are\h\h organic artifacts, radiocarbon dating can be used\h to give an actual date to the bones. As we just\h\h discussed, we know the entire stratigraphic\h layer containing the deer bones is also that\h\h age. Let's say we were given a carbon dated range\h of 2900-3100 BP. We now know several new things\h\h about our site. For starters, we know that our\h site was first inhabited around 3000 years ago,\h\h and the people here were making stone tools. By\h the law of superposition, we also know that the\h\h entire rest of the site is younger than 3000 BP. If you were an archeologist looking at this site,\h\h what might your next move be? You may remember\h that there is a burn layer towards the top of\h\h the stratigraphic layers, and higher than that we\h don’t see any more human artifacts. As we learned,\h\h burned objects preserve the C14 we use to perform\h radiocarbon dating. If we date this layer,\h\h we then would know the entire length of occupation\h of the site. For our example, let's say we got a\h\h radiocarbon date of 900-1100 BP for the burn\h layer. Let's jot down some interpretations of\h\h the site based on our data: We know this site was\h occupied from around 3000 BP until around 1000 BP,\h\h meaning the site was occupied for a total\h of 2000 years. Before a permanent population\h\h was established at the site, people were using\h stone tools and eating deer here. After this,\h\h a temple was built at the site, and a village grew\h around it. This highlights the\h importance of an artifact being in its original\h\h context when excavated. An artifact's context\h refers to its surroundings. An artifact still\h\h in its original stratigraphic layer is called\h “in situ”. An artifact removed from its layer\h\h without being properly recorded loses its\h usefulness in dating that artifact and the\h\h layer it came from. Since\h\h we placed our pottery in the same typology, we\h can estimate our pottery's age as being from\h\h 1000-1800 BP, making the full chronology of\h the site look something like this.

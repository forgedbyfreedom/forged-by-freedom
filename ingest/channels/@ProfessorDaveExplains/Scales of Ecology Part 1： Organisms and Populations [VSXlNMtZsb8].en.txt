In the introduction to this series we looked at\h all the tiers of complexity that an ecologist\h\h may study, starting with an individual organism\h and zooming out to consider the entire biosphere.\h\h Let’s now take a closer look at all of these\h tiers one at a time so that we can get a better\h\h understanding of what ecologists study, starting\h at the most fundamental level, an organism. What\h\h can an ecologist study when it comes to a single\h organism? First, they can study an organism’s\h\h life history, or cycle. This pertains to how the\h organism grows, develops with time, reproduces\h\h and, ultimately, survives. Life histories vary\h tremendously from one species to the next. For\h\h example, some organisms die immediately after\h reproducing, such as salmon, many insects, and\h\h all grain crops, while others live on to reproduce\h repeatedly, like most plants and vertebrates. Why\h\h is there so much variation? Researchers who study\h ecology at the organism level ask this question\h\h and many others, like: How have different\h life cycles developed? How do they serve\h\h the organism? They also attempt to understand\h adaptations they observe in an organism. These\h\h are beneficial features or behaviors arising by\h natural selection which allow organisms to thrive\h\h in specific habitats. They range from varying\h body coverings, like fur, feathers, and scales;\h\h to patterns of color and camouflage; to defenses\h like a skunk’s spray and a snake’s venom;\h\h and finally, to body parts like beaks, claws,\h and antlers. Adaptations can take multiple forms,\h\h including alterations to an organism’s morphology,\h which refers to a structural change which gives\h\h an organism a greater chance of survival in its\h particular habitat. A great example is the Fennec\h\h fox, which lives in the desert. It has large ears.\h You might think it’s so the fox can hear better,\h\h but it’s actually to allow heat to radiate\h away from the body, helping to cool the\h\h fox down in the intensely hot environment. Adaptations can be physiological in nature as\h\h well: these are internal processes that regulate\h and maintain homeostasis, so that an organism is\h\h able to survive in the environment in which it\h exists. Examples of this include temperature\h\h regulation, release of toxins or poisons for\h protection, and even releasing natural antifreeze\h\h in the form of proteins, to avoid freezing in cold\h environments. When it comes to humans, we can find\h\h some familiar examples. Calluses on our hands\h can be considered physiological adaptations to\h\h repeated contact or pressure, as can tanning\h or darkening of the skin after repeated\h\h exposure to the sun. There are also behavioral\h adaptations, which are changes in behavior that\h\h certain organisms or species use to survive in\h their environment. There are two types: learned\h\h behaviors and instinctive behaviors. Learned\h behaviors include habituation, or getting used\h\h to a particular stimulus after being exposed to it\h multiple times; and sensitization, or responding\h\h to certain stimuli in an enhanced or more\h reactive manner. Think about becoming allergic\h\h to something – like a certain type of food or\h pollen. Your body, by becoming more sensitive,\h\h protects you from exposing yourself to these\h irritants in the future by producing a noticeable\h\h and worsening response. Then there’s imprinting –\h an adaptive function that allows a young animal to\h\h distinguish its own mother from other females of\h the same or different species – and to remain near\h\h her for safety and protection. Learned behaviors\h can also come about from play and observation.\h\h Crows, for example, are notorious for their knack\h for observation-based learned behaviors, including\h\h solving puzzles and using tools. Instinctive\h behaviors are those behaviors that animals are\h\h born knowing how to do. Changes usually come about\h over many generations until they finally become\h\h a part of instinctive behavior. Some examples\h include the migration of birds like geese during\h\h the cold seasons of the year to areas further\h south, in search of food; the hibernation of some\h\h mammals during the winter, like bears; courtship,\h or mating patterns; and foraging behaviors,\h\h which include knowing ways of finding food,\h depending on the habitat or environment.\h Moving on from organisms, population ecologists\h are scientists who study the size, or total number\h\h of individuals, as well as density and structure\h of populations, and how they change over time.\h\h Of interest are a population’s geographic range,\h which has limits that a species can tolerate, like\h\h fluctuations in temperature or moisture. The study\h of population ecology also includes understanding,\h\h explaining, and predicting species distributions.\h Why do species inhabit particular areas, and how\h\h are they prevented from establishing beyond their\h range limits? Such range questions have become\h\h popular in the last decade or so in response\h to concerns about climate change. Additionally,\h\h what are the birth and death rates of a particular\h species? What urges populations to immigrate,\h\h or explore new areas beyond their previous\h range? What causes them to emigrate,\h\h when they leave familiar territory altogether?\h What are the long-term probabilities of species\h\h persisting and surviving in certain habitats?\h What factors play a role in deciding this?\h Now let’s discuss population growth. Populations\h can grow in two different modes: at geometric\h\h rates or at exponential rates. While exploring\h these, keep in mind that we will be assuming\h\h the presence of unlimited resources which urge\h population growth. Exponential growth happens\h\h continuously, with reproduction happening at\h any time, like in human populations. Geometric\h\h populations grow through pulsed reproduction\h instead. An example of this is deer which have\h\h a constrained mating and reproduction season,\h keeping them from constantly reproducing. When\h\h exponential growth occurs, it is in favorable\h environments and at low population densities.\h\h Because of this, exponential growth may apply\h to populations establishing new environments;\h\h during transient, favorable conditions; and by\h populations with low initial population density.\h\h Of course, neither geometric nor exponential\h growth can continue indefinitely. In nature,\h\h population growth must eventually slow, and\h population size eventually ceases to increase.\h\h As resources are depleted, the population growth\h rate slows and eventually stops. This is known as\h\h logistic growth. The population size at which\h growth stops is generally called the carrying\h\h capacity, which is the number of individuals\h of a particular population that the environment\h\h can “carry”, or support. Carrying capacity is\h characterized by equal birth and death rates,\h\h creating a population growth of zero. In some\h populations, organisms in lower trophic levels,\h\h meaning the positions that organisms occupy\h in a food web, are controlled by organisms at\h\h the top. This is known as top-down control. For\h example, carnivores at the top keep the herbivore\h\h population in check. If herbivores weren’t\h controlled, all the plants would be eaten,\h\h leading to a potential collapse of the\h entire ecosystem. In bottom-up control,\h\h it’s the producers at the lower trophic\h levels who drive the changes instead. If\h\h plant populations change, then the population\h of all species will be impacted.

We just examined the two lowest tiers on the\h scale of ecology, organisms and populations.\h\h When populations of different species can be\h found in the same location, this constitutes a\h\h community. So how do ecologists study communities? Community ecologists focus on interactions between\h\h populations of different species and how these\h interactions shape the communities where they\h\h live. This includes not just the study of the\h interactions of species, such as mutualism,\h\h predation, and competition, but also the\h dynamics and structure of the community.\h\h How is it organized? How does it function?\h In a nutshell, community ecology examines how\h\h coexisting organisms interact and compete in\h a particular niche or geographical location,\h\h such as woodland, prairie, or lake. Let’s start\h by defining the different kinds of interactions\h\h between populations of species. Mutualism is\h a symbiotic relationship that is beneficial\h\h to both organisms involved. This occurs when two\h organisms of different species “work together”,\h\h and each reaps benefits that increase\h survivability. The oxpecker, a kind of bird,\h\h and the zebra are one such example: the bird gets\h food in the form of ticks and other parasites on\h\h the zebra’s skin, while the zebra gets a kind\h of “pest control.” Going a little deeper,\h\h mutualism can take two forms. When the species are\h entirely dependent on each other, this is called\h\h obligate mutualism. An easy way to remember\h this is that both organisms are obligated,\h\h or forced to, rely on one another. An example\h of this is mycorrhizal fungi with hyphae that\h\h exchange crucial nutrients with plant roots. In\h other situations, where the species can survive\h\h on their own, like the bird/zebra relationship we\h mentioned, this is called facultative mutualism.\h\h Another example of obligate mutualism is the yucca\h plant and the moth: the flower of the yucca plant,\h\h in the habitat of the arid climate\h of the southwestern United States,\h\h relies on the moth for the pollination process.\h In turn, the moth benefits by laying its own eggs\h\h on the flower and feeding the larvae with the\h seeds. Honeybees and plants are another example\h\h of facultative mutualism, given that honey bees\h visit many different plant species for nectar,\h\h and the plants receive a number of\h different insect visitors for pollination.\h Now let’s look at the opposite of mutualism,\h predation and competition. The former is when\h\h one population is the resource of the other, while\h the latter is when individuals seek to obtain the\h\h same environmental resource. Think of competition\h as occurring horizontally on the same resource\h\h level while predation takes place vertically\h between different resource levels. These are\h\h still ecological relationships, but they’re\h not symbiotic ones. The best-known examples\h\h of predation involve carnivorous interactions,\h in which one animal consumes another – like\h\h when wolves hunt moose, or owls hunt mice. If\h there’s an increase in predation in a community,\h\h or any other source of mortality, this will\h generally reduce the absolute change in density\h\h of consumers, simply because the direct impact of\h predators is to reduce the numbers of their prey.\h\h While the term “reducing” seems negative,\h predators are often a positive influence\h\h on communities, and can increase diversity.\h They do this by hunting dominant species,\h\h which reduces the pressure on or overconsumption\h of foundation species, those species that have\h\h a strong role in structuring a community\h by providing habitat structure and food.\h\h Some examples of foundation species are corals,\h kelp beds, seagrass meadows, or hardwood trees.\h Predation can have a positive effect when the\h predators feed more on the dominant competitors,\h\h that would otherwise use up all of the community’s\h resources, which organisms are competing for.\h\h Animals fight over territory, food, water, and\h shelter. Plants compete for light exposure,\h\h pollinators, and soil nutrients. Microbes\h compete for nutrients and chemical substrates.\h\h Competition is important in an ecosystem as the\h best competitors are the ones who survive and\h\h pass along their genes. Their offspring have\h an increased chance of survival because their\h\h parents out-competed other species and passed\h down the genetic tools that helped them succeed.\h What can affect population size? It\h comes down to the availability of food,\h\h which can act as a “bottom-up” control. In years\h when preferred food is abundant, populations are\h\h able to grow. As predator populations increase,\h in response to the growth of consumer populations,\h\h they put greater strain on the prey\h populations and act as a top-down control,\h\h pushing the prey towards a state of\h decline to keep the community in balance.\h\h While the presence of a predator can decrease\h the growth rate of a population, that doesn’t\h\h affect the overall carrying capacity, which is\h reliant on the availability of resources. So,\h\h how does predation drive population\h dynamics? When predators are scarce,\h\h prey increases in abundance. As their food source\h increases, predators increase in abundance. When\h\h the predators reach sufficiently high numbers,\h the prey population is driven down to low numbers.\h\h With a scarcity of food, the predator population\h declines and the cycle repeats. We should also\h\h discuss how predation drives selection. Predators\h are effective agents of natural selection because\h\h they possess key traits that allow them to capture\h and consume their prey. They adopt different\h\h strategies to hunt which can drive prey evolution,\h to increase their likelihood of survival.\h\h With communities covered, let’s now pull back\h even farther to take a look at ecosystems.

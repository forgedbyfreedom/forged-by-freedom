As we learned in the previous\h\h tutorial, this idea is called scarcity, and it\h drives everything in economics. We make personal\h\h choices, business choices, and collective choices\h differently, but we all do so based on scarcity.\h\h We can’t do everything or have everything, so we\h must weigh our options and make the best choice\h\h for ourselves that we can, based on our situation. All choices involve trade-offs. A trade-off is the\h\h act of giving up one benefit in order to\h gain another potentially greater benefit.\h\h For example, you are benefiting from\h watching this tutorial right now,\h\h but you chose to watch it because the perceived\h benefit of watching this tutorial outweighs\h\h other things that also could benefit you. But watching this\h tutorial benefits you more at this moment.\h\h When a restaurant makes a choice to pay its\h servers higher wages, it may have to be at the\h\h cost of spending money on advertising, or buying\h new appliances, or even paying its custodians\h\h higher wages. When a government makes the choice\h to spend more money on combat drones and bombs,\h\h it may have to be at the cost of spending more\h money on education, healthcare, or fixing roads.\h Whenever we make a choice, there is always an\h opportunity cost. Opportunity cost is the next\h\h best thing we give up by making that choice. It’s\h essentially the most desirable alternate choice,\h\h something which is useful to identify when making\h decisions because it reminds us to compare the\h\h value associated with various options. Going\h back to our earlier example of you choosing\h\h to watch this tutorial, say you were to rank\h your alternate choices of playing video games,\h\h fishing, rollerblading, watching Netflix,\h and building birdhouses in this order.\h\h Remember, you chose to watch this video, so these\h are your next best choices. But your opportunity\h\h cost is watching Netflix, since that is your\h very next best choice, and you are giving up\h\h the opportunity to watch Netflix at this moment\h by choosing to watch this tutorial instead.\h Let’s look at another example. Every night, we\h make a choice regarding when to go to sleep.\h\h Say you normally get eight hours of sleep,\h but you only get five hours of sleep one\h\h night since you stayed up late studying three\h hours for an economics test. Your alternatives\h\h were eight hours of sleep and no studying,\h seven hours of sleep and one hour of studying,\h\h or six hours of sleep and two hours of\h studying. What is your opportunity cost? Well,\h\h it depends on who you are, but if you value\h studying over sleep you might rank the options\h\h in this way, with the options becoming less\h desirable as the increment of study decreases.\h\h According to this ranking, the opportunity cost\h associated with an extra three hours of studying\h\h rather than two is the additional\h hour of sleep you gave up.\h This ranking of choices leads to the idea\h of something called thinking at the margin.\h\h Thinking at the margin has to do with making\h decisions based on small changes in the allocation\h\h of resources. The average adult makes around 35,000 choices\h\h per day. Recognizing and weighing the trade-offs,\h\h thinking at the margin, and considering\h the opportunity cost of every choice is\h\h a mental exercise that can help make the\h big decisions in life a little easier.\h\h And now we understand the concepts of scarcity,\h trade-offs, and cost/benefit analysis.

As we have mentioned several times throughout\h this series, functional group interconversion\h\h is essential in organic synthesis. The ability to\h take a cheap or conveniently available substrate\h\h and turn one of its functional groups into another\h one is a fundamental strategy in the synthesis of\h\h complex organic molecules. In a previous tutorial,\h we introduced a reaction called the Curtius\h\h rearrangement. This reaction was first described\h by German chemist Theodor Curtius in 1885. Curtius\h\h converted an acid chloride into an acyl azide by\h reacting it with azide salts like sodium azide.\h\h Forming the acyl azide is the first step of\h the Curtius reaction. The rearrangement itself\h\h consists of heating the substance to a temperature\h of about 100oC, where it then decomposes to form\h\h an isocyanate. This process is now believed\h to be concerted, with the R group migrating to\h\h the nitrogen atom at the same time as diatomic\h nitrogen is released, leaving this isocyanate.\h With this context we can introduce the\h Schmidt reaction. One simple version of\h\h the Schmidt reaction involves the direct reaction\h of carboxylic acids with HN3, or hydrazoic acid.\h\h This leads directly to the acyl azide, without\h having to preform the acid chloride, which then\h\h reacts via Curtius rearrangement to give the\h isocyanate as we just discussed. But a much\h\h more interesting extension of this chemistry was\h first described by German chemist Karl Friedrich\h\h Schmidt in 1924, and deals with the reaction of\h hydrazoic acid with ketones, yielding amides in\h\h a process referred to as the Schmidt reaction. There are generally two mechanisms proposed for\h\h this reaction, and it is believed that they may\h operate simultaneously in many cases. In the first\h\h pathway, the nitrogen that bears the proton on\h hydrazoic acid attacks the protonated carbonyl\h\h to give this intermediate, and this is followed\h by the simultaneous migration of an R group and\h\h loss of the very stable N2 molecule, to yield the\h amide. This is called the Baeyer-Villiger pathway,\h\h because it is analogous to the mechanism for this\h reaction, which we covered in a previous tutorial.\h The second pathway starts the same way, but then\h further proton transfer takes place so as to kick\h\h off a water molecule and produce this diazoiminium\h ion intermediate. At this point, we again see the\h\h migration of an alkyl group to kick off nitrogen\h gas, while the nitrogen that remains generates a\h\h third C-N bond to maintain neutrality of the\h adjacent carbon. That gives us this nitrilium\h\h cation. This is readily hydrated by the water\h generated during the first step, and that again\h\h brings us to the amide. This second pathway is\h called the Beckmann pathway, because it resembles\h\h the mechanism for the Beckmann rearrangement,\h which we also discussed in another tutorial.\h Now, an immediately obvious problem that needs\h resolution is the selectivity of alkyl migration\h\h in non-symmetrical ketones. If R1 is the same as\h R2, then there is no ambiguity. But the situation\h\h is more complex when R1 is not the same as R2. It is believed that in the Beckmann pathway\h\h the diazoiminium intermediate forms two possible\h isomers, where the R1 group can be either cis or\h\h trans with respect to the diazonium group, which\h will be of differing stabilities depending on\h\h sterics. Then the migration proceeds in anti\h fashion with respect to the departing group,\h\h as is depicted here. This becomes trickier when we\h realize that the two isomers of the diazoiminium\h\h ion interconvert under acid catalysis, when\h this nitrogen becomes protonated, giving this\h\h C-N bond considerable single bond character and\h thus the ability to rotate. But in any case,\h\h as a general rule, the more substituted alkyl\h group migrates preferentially, whether proceeding\h\h via the Baeyer-Villiger or Beckmann pathways. So\h bulkier alkyl groups migrate preferentially over\h\h smaller alkyl groups, and aryl groups\h migrate preferentially over alkyl groups.\h An interesting observation which is quite\h recent, is that alkyl azides undergo the\h\h same reaction as hydrazoic acid under acidic\h conditions, whether using Brønsted-Lowry acids\h\h like trifluoroacetic acid, or Lewis acids\h like tin tetrachloride. In some cases,\h\h the substrate may contain both reacting\h functionalities, a ketone and an alkyl azide,\h\h and some acid treatment may be enough to activate\h the substrate toward a regiospecific Schmidt\h\h rearrangement. This strategy has been applied\h broadly in the synthesis of natural products.\h We can further illustrate this brilliant\h innovation by reporting work from Professor Jeff\h\h Aubé, now at the University of North Carolina. In\h this example, the target is the alkaloid stenine.\h\h Professor Aubé found that he could combine\h a Lewis-acid catalyzed Diels-Alder reaction\h\h with a Schmidt reaction, using the same Lewis\h acid for both reactions. When he reacted the\h\h following diene and dienophile, the cycloaddition\h gave this intermediate, an azido ketone. We will\h\h notice that the regiochemistry of the Diels-Alder\h reaction is governed by the rules we discussed in\h\h a previous tutorial on this reaction, namely that\h the electron-withdrawing group of the dienophile,\h\h in this case the ketone, ends up opposite the\h electron-donating substituent in the diene,\h\h in this case the trimethylsilyl enol\h ether. In addition, the endo transition\h\h state prevails over the exo, and therefore\h the four stereocenters that are generated\h\h must have this specific configuration out of\h the eight that are geometrically possible.\h\h Under these same reaction conditions, the\h Lewis acid induces the Schmidt rearrangement,\h\h with regioselectivity and stereospecificity.\h Finally, hydrolysis of the TMS enol ether\h\h yields the following ketoamide. This was then\h converted to stenine via five additional steps\h\h that we will not discuss. Note how this brilliant\h approach, with the Diels-Alder cycloaddition\h\h and Schmidt reaction happening all in one\h pot, allows for the establishment of the\h\h correct stereochemistry and the construction\h of the skeleton of the natural alkaloid.\h A complex strategy such as this one illustrates\h first of all how organic chemists like to combine\h\h reactions in “domino” sequences to assemble\h complex molecular structures very quickly\h\h and efficiently. It also illustrates how the\h old Schmidt reaction can be extended to quite\h\h complex situations, where its regioselectivity\h and stereospecificity can help create complicated\h\h molecular structures. Thus, this venerable old\h reaction is still very much of current interest\h\h to synthetic organic chemists, and every student\h of organic synthesis should be familiar with it.

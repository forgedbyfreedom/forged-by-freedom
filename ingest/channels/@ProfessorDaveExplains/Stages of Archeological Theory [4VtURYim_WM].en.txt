Before we can learn about archeological\h practices, we need to look at the field\h\h of archeology itself. As archeologists\h are humans, the very thing they study,\h\h they must be careful not to insert their\h own biases. Archeological theory refers to\h\h the conceptual framework archeologists use to\h interpret past peoples. A good understanding\h\h of this theory helps archeologists better\h align their concepts of the past with a\h\h less biased approach. However, archeology\h has changed a lot since its inception,\h\h and therefore so has the theory. Understanding\h past archeological theory is important if we\h\h want to use sources of information gathered\h by earlier studies. Knowing what conceptual\h\h framework an archeologist was using is critical in\h interpreting the information in a modern context.\h The first stage of archeological theory is\h called Cultural History. Starting in the 1860s,\h\h the theory had a great deal of influence from\h Darwinian evolution. Cultural evolution was\h\h established as a concept from this influence. This\h includes the idea that human cultures “evolve”\h\h similarly to organisms. Selection played a part\h in this concept. More “fit” societies survived,\h\h and less fit societies went extinct. Fitness\h was usually a society's technological progress,\h\h “will” as a people, or something similar. To\h a cultural history theorist, human history was\h\h basically a game of Sid Meier's Civilization. This\h explained how Europeans were able to establish\h\h themselves all over the world. To employ this\h theory, archeologists identified and defined\h\h thousands of cultures. An archeological culture\h is a group of people in a region with very similar\h\h material remains, such as similar building\h practices, pottery, and burials. To a cultural\h\h history theorist, these cultures interacted and\h competed in a similar way as organisms, either\h\h adapting or going extinct. From this idea came the\h concept of the evolution of societies from a band,\h\h to a tribe, to a chiefdom, and lastly a state,\h each society growing in complexity and fitness.\h\h By the 1950s it was clear that this view of human\h history was too narrow. Human societies don’t\h\h compete like animals or plants. They are ideas,\h something intangible. They do change and evolve,\h\h but not in predictable ways, and especially not\h in ways determined by fitness. While we still use\h\h cultures as a way to define groups with similar\h material remains, we don’t see them interacting\h\h in the way conceived by cultural history. In response to the narrow and problematic\h\h worldview of cultural history, archeologists\h created Processual Archeology. Seeing how\h\h biased many theories of cultural history\h were, processual archeologists emphasized\h\h being impartial. Ideas from hard sciences, such\h as the strict use of the scientific method in\h\h interpreting material remains, were put into\h place. Hypotheses were made, tested, revised,\h\h accepted or rejected, and theories were created\h from them. Processual archeology also attempted\h\h to refocus archeology to studying humans,\h rather than the study of cultures defined by\h\h archeologists themselves. This included decoupling\h the idea of a culture and ethnic group. It became\h\h clear that just because archeologists thought\h two groups had similar material remains, they\h\h didn’t act as the same ethnic group or political\h organization, as had been previously envisioned.\h However, many archeologists also found processual\h archeology to be problematic. One of the main\h\h problems they saw was in how the scientific\h method was implemented. While they recognized the\h\h importance of trying to be unbiased, they argued\h that it was impossible to be completely impartial\h\h in archeology, and archeological work shouldn’t\h be presented in a way such that certain data leads\h\h to unchallengeable outcomes. This conceptual\h framework is called Postprocessual Archeology,\h\h which gained popularity in the 1980s.\h There typically are no experiments that\h\h can be repeated in archeology which lead to a\h predictable set of results like in chemistry\h\h or physics. Postprocesuallists argued that in the\h study of past humans, we will always impart some\h\h of our biases, and it’s important to acknowledge\h that. This has led some archeologists to accept\h\h “multiple pasts”. There is no exact way things\h happened in history. Think of our modern world,\h\h and ask two people in different political parties\h what they think American culture is, and the\h\h important events that led to its formation. You\h will likely hear two very different answers that\h\h lead to very different interpretations of American\h culture. Is one of these interpretations more\h\h correct? And this is the case when looking at\h a living culture. Trying to understand ancient\h\h cultures from our worldview will only exacerbate\h this problem. Therefore, multiple past proponents\h\h conclude that there is no single, correct\h interpretation of the past. This doesn’t mean\h\h that any interpretation of the past is valid. The\h interpretation must be based on observations, be\h\h able to explain all current data, and survive peer\h review, and there should still be a prevailing\h\h view on most concepts, time periods, and peoples. Modern archeological theory builds on all previous\h\h theories. Cultures are still defined in the\h manner of cultural history but without the lens\h\h of cultural competition. Processual archaeology\h is still used in making hypotheses and theories\h\h in interpreting remains with the scientific\h method, but these interpretations aren’t seen\h\h as unfalsifiable outcomes. Modern archeology\h also places importance on fixing centuries of\h\h unethical practice toward descendants of people\h archeologists were studying. Past archeological\h\h theory has often treated artifacts and sites\h as datapoints rather than objects of cultural\h\h heritage to a group.

Amino acids are the monomers that comprise\h proteins, and thus they are fundamental\h\h components of every known living organism, just\h as we have discussed in the biochemistry series.\h\h But while nature has considerable expertise in\h their production, chemists have also developed\h\h a wide variety of techniques for amino acid\h synthesis. The first lab synthesis of these\h\h compounds was published in 1850 by German chemist\h Adolph Strecker, and is now known as the Strecker\h\h amino acid synthesis. Although 170 years old,\h this synthesis still plays an important role in\h\h the repertoire of the synthetic organic chemist. Strecker discovered that heating a mixture of an\h\h aldehyde, such as formaldehyde, with hydrogen\h cyanide and ammonia, led to an amino nitrile,\h\h which could then be hydrolyzed at the nitrile to\h produce an amino acid, in this case glycine. It is\h\h widely believed that this reaction may have led\h to the formation of amino acids on this planet,\h\h since aldehydes, hydrogen cyanide, and ammonia\h were likely abundant on prebiotic earth.\h\h However in the lab, the reaction is carried out\h by heating an aldehyde with potassium cyanide\h\h and an ammonium salt, in order to avoid the\h dangers associated with ammonia and especially\h\h hydrogen cyanide gas, which is extremely toxic. When the synthesis is applied to higher aldehydes\h\h such as acetaldehyde, a chiral amino acid is\h formed, in this case alanine. Of course with\h\h this reaction the product is racemic, meaning\h an equal mixture of the R and the S enantiomers,\h\h because the reaction is not supported or\h catalyzed by a chiral template or catalyst.\h\h The mechanism of the reaction\h is believed to be as follows,\h\h proceeding at first just like imine formation,\h which we learned about in a previous tutorial.\h\h But then the cyanide ion attacks\h here, to give the amino nitrile.\h\h Then hydrolysis of the nitrile occurs over several\h steps under acid catalysis, again via chemistry we\h\h have already learned, and there is our amino acid.\h The reaction applies to ketones as well, producing\h\h alpha-alkyl amino acids, and can also work with\h primary amines, to give N-alkyl amino acids.\h While amino acids are produced industrially\h by enzymatic methods because it’s so cheap,\h\h the Strecker synthesis can be extremely useful\h for producing unnatural amino acids. However, in\h\h order for it to be really useful, a procedure must\h be developed to produce only a single enantiomer\h\h of the desired target, because separation by\h resolution is often tedious and inefficient.\h Over the last 60 years or so, chemists have been\h able to turn the Strecker amino acid synthesis\h\h into a tool to prepare enantiomerically\h pure amino acids.

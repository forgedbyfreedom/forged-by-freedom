Now we will discuss another\h\h important application of hydrogen gas: its\h reaction with nitrogen gas to produce ammonia.\h As we know, living organisms require nitrogen\h to biosynthesize key biopolymers like proteins\h\h and nucleic acids. The single most abundant\h source of nitrogen on earth is nitrogen gas,\h\h which makes up slightly over three fourths of\h the air we breathe. Unfortunately, most living\h\h organisms are unable to utilize this source of\h nitrogen. The molecule consists of two atoms of\h\h nitrogen bound by a triple bond, and has a bond\h dissociation energy of 945 kilojoules per mole,\h\h which makes it one of the strongest bonds we\h know. There are microorganisms which are able\h\h to fix nitrogen to ammonia using enzymes called\h nitrogenases and using ATP as energy currency.\h\h These are called diazotrophs. Unfortunately,\h however, higher animals have not evolved this\h\h ability. We are most definitely not diazotrophs. Ammonia is actually a very toxic molecule to most\h\h living organisms. It is a toxic gas under ambient\h conditions with a boiling point of -33oC, and\h\h it is not found in appreciable amounts in life.\h Diazotrophs capture ammonia as glutamate, and this\h\h “storage” reaction forms the basis of amino acid\h and nucleoside synthesis. Once introduced into\h\h glutamate, the amino group can be exchanged with\h a number of substrates to effectively transfer\h\h ammonia using enzymatic reactions. Ammonia is\h excreted as urea or uric acid in higher animals,\h\h again because ammonia is too toxic to be\h tolerated in appreciable concentrations in vivo.\h Due to its toxicity, there are no bulk sources of\h ammonia in the animal world. Sources of nitrogen\h\h include nitrate minerals and guano, the excrement\h of seabirds and bats. Yet ammonia, which is used\h\h to make fertilizers, is needed in huge amounts to\h sustain modern agricultural production. Therefore,\h\h an industrial process to convert nitrogen gas\h into ammonia has always been a target. The first\h\h practical version of this process was invented by\h German chemists Fritz Haber and Carl Bosch at the\h\h beginning of the twentieth century. Actually,\h the driving force for this development had to\h\h do with the weapon industry, which relied\h on nitrates. Nitrates were not available to\h\h Germany and therefore Haber and Bosch were tasked\h with finding a solution to the unavailability\h\h of large enough sources of “fixed” nitrogen. The basic equation for the process is as follows,\h\h with one molecule of nitrogen reacting\h with three molecules of hydrogen to yield\h\h two ammonia molecules. The reaction is\h exothermic under ambient conditions,\h\h with a delta H at room temperature of -46\h kilojoules per mole, and therefore the equilibrium\h\h favors formation of ammonia. Unfortunately, the\h reaction does not work under ambient conditions\h\h even in the presence of the best metal catalysts.\h Using the traditional iron-based catalysts,\h\h a temperature over 400oC is needed to achieve\h reasonable conversion rates, and under these\h\h conditions the equilibrium becomes unfavorable,\h since additional heat drives the system back\h\h towards reactants via Le Chatelier’s principle. To better understand why this is the case,\h\h we need to recall that what truly drives chemical\h reactions is Gibbs free energy, which means not\h\h just enthalpy, but also entropy, as illustrated by\h the equation delta G equals delta H minus T delta\h\h S, and the entropic contribution becomes\h more substantial at higher temperatures,\h\h since the change in entropy is multiplied by\h temperature. For a spontaneous reaction, delta\h\h G has to be negative. Delta H is indeed negative\h for the reaction, but delta S is also negative.\h\h The entropy decreases in the reaction, because\h we start with four molecules and end with two,\h\h and a decrease in entropy makes the reaction less\h favorable, and at high temperatures this entropy\h\h term overwhelms the change in enthalpy to make the\h process nonspontaneous. To get more quantitative,\h\h under typical pressures of 150-200 bar, the\h two terms cancel each other out around 200oC,\h\h and therefore the equilibrium is\h unfavorable under typical conditions,\h\h or the temperature of 400oC that would typically\h be used. However, a way to force the reaction\h\h forward was discovered. One trick that can\h be used is to increase the pressure. Higher\h\h total pressure favors the forward reaction,\h because we are turning four molecules into two,\h\h so Le Chatelier’s principle dictates that the\h products will be favored in order to alleviate\h\h some of the pressure increase. High pressure\h requires a large energy expenditure and has\h\h safety issues, as in practice it is difficult\h to run the process beyond the 200 bar threshold.\h\h Under such conditions, ammonia constitutes\h only 15% of the gas mixture at equilibrium.\h The most important trick we can use is to recycle.\h We have to keep in mind that the relative boiling\h\h points of the three species involved are as\h follows: nitrogen is -196oC, hydrogen is -253oC,\h\h and ammonia is -33oC. This means that if we\h flow the gaseous mixture through a catalyst\h\h bed and cool the effluent to a temperature that is\h under -33oC but not as low as -196oC, much of the\h\h ammonia gas will condense to a liquid and can be\h collected in a receiving flask, while the hydrogen\h\h and nitrogen gases will be recycled through\h the reactor bed. This also drives the reaction\h\h forward via Le Chatelier’s principle as we are\h removing the product as it forms. If the process\h\h is repeated several times, very good conversions,\h usually in excess of 90%, are obtained.\h This is the basis for the current Haber-Bosch\h process, which is still used to make ammonia\h\h industrially. Each year, about 230 million tons of\h ammonia are prepared by this process. Obviously,\h\h a lot of attention is paid to improvements. One\h obvious way to improve the process is to optimize\h\h the catalyst. Here we are doing heterogeneous\h catalysis, and even without changing the\h\h catalytic metal, it is possible to improve\h the process by better catalyst fabrication,\h\h or improving the quality of the surface. Lots\h of attention is paid to reducing fouling,\h\h which slowly degrades the performance of the\h catalyst and requires catalyst reactivation or\h\h replacement. Iron is the key metal used simply\h because it is so cheap, and the final product\h\h has to be extremely cheap, but other metals,\h like ruthenium, work even better. In addition,\h\h non-transition metal salts are added, and this\h affects the catalytic power of the surface.\h There are drawbacks to this key process. One\h is energy consumption. CO2 emissions related to the\h ammonia production process represent 3% of global\h\h human emissions. But the area that is attracting\h the most attention is the use of alternative,\h\h greener sources of hydrogen gas. Hydrogen is currently produced through\h\h gasification of hydrocarbons, mainly natural gas\h like methane, and 3-5% of the methane produced in\h\h the world goes toward making ammonia. This is a\h huge amount. Independently of ammonia production,\h\h cheaper approaches to hydrogen gas production are\h therefore being sought. Thus, the century-old Haber-Bosch\h\h process will not be easily supplanted. Finally, you may be curious to know what\h\h the mechanism of this reaction looks like. Because\h we are discussing a heterogeneous reaction under\h\h conditions of extreme heat and pressure, you can\h imagine that experimental work with this system is\h\h not trivial. However, given our detailed knowledge\h of catalytic surfaces and the very simple nature\h\h of the intermediates involved, theoretical\h calculations have probed the intimate details\h\h of the reaction.

Studying fingerprints and\h their recovery for a person’s identification is\h\h a branch of science known as dermatoglyphics. This\h is derived from two words: derma which means skin,\h\h and glyph which means carving. So dermatoglyphics\h is the study of skin carving. Our main\h focus will be on fingerprints, not only because\h\h they are the most common, but also because the\h techniques of recovery for all other patterns\h\h are similar to those of fingerprints. As a piece\h of evidence found at the CS, fingerprints are\h\h exceptionally important, for two reasons. One:\h every fingerprint is unique to an individual.\h\h And two: fingerprints remain the same throughout\h a person’s life unless the dermal layer of skin\h\h is permanently damaged. Identification of a\h person from their fingerprints generally involves\h\h identification of the overall pattern followed\h by individualization of the pattern. We will\h\h talk about this in the next few tutorials in great\h detail, but first, let's look at the fascinating\h\h history of fingerprints and their study. The use of fingerprints or palm prints\h\h for a person’s identification actually dates back\h thousands of years. Fingerprint-like patterns have\h\h been observed on many archeological artifacts such\h as pots, utensils, and seals in ancient Greece and\h\h China. Fingerprints have also been observed on\h walls of Egyptian tombs. Craftsmen may have used\h\h fingerprints as a way to sign their work, thus\h authenticating them. Clay tablets with fingerprint\h\h patterns imprinted on them were used in ancient\h Babylon to validate business transactions, thus\h\h protecting them from forgery. As opposed to today,\h materials like paper, ink, and writing instruments\h\h were not readily available to authenticate a\h transaction by signature, so they took advantage\h\h of the fact that the patterns on your hand are\h your readymade stamps. It is quite interesting\h\h that people back then actually realized the\h uniqueness of these patterns, and used them for\h\h authenticating things. Gradually, law enforcement\h in ancient Babylon and China started using\h\h fingerprints for the identification of criminals\h as well. Documentation from the era of the\h\h Babylonian king Hammurabi indicates that records\h were kept of arrested criminals by fingerprinting\h\h them. Around 300 CE in China, fingerprints were\h authorized to be used as a piece of evidence for\h\h investigation in cases involving theft. Before\h this development, criminals were identified in\h\h most civilizations by either branding or maiming\h them, where the former involves applying hot iron\h\h on a person’s skin, while the latter involves\h cutting off the nose or hands of the criminal.\h\h Fingerprinting surely was a welcome change in this\h regard. While fingerprints were used as proof of\h\h identity in some parts of China as early as 300\h BCE, other civilizations followed suit later,\h\h such as Japan in 702 CE, and Persia in the 14th\h century, while the United States joined hundreds\h\h of years later. It was in 1902 that fingerprints\h were adopted as a means of identification in the\h\h U.S. Fingerprint science was highly developed by\h then with a proper pattern classification system\h\h in place. Let’s learn about the pioneers who made\h substantial contributions to fingerprint science.\h Nehemia Grew, an English scientist widely\h known for his work on the anatomy of plants,\h\h is widely acknowledged as the first person\h to study and describe ridges, furrows,\h\h and pores on hand and foot surfaces. In 1684\h he published accurate drawings of these skin\h\h patterns. Marcello Malpighi, who is referred to as\h the founder of microscopical anatomy, histology,\h\h and the father of physiology and embryology,\h was a contemporary of Nehemia Grew. It has been\h\h reported that both Malpighi and Grew often freely\h borrowed from one another, as they shared a common\h\h interest in microscopical studies. Malpighi\h observed fingerprints using the microscope\h\h and documented the common characteristics of\h different fingerprint patterns. With regards\h\h to his contributions, a 1.88 millimeter layer\h of human skin came to be known as the Malpighi\h\h layer. In 1823, a thesis was published by John\h Evangelist Purkinje which described a total of\h\h nine different fingerprint patterns. One\h thing to note here is that none of these\h\h early pioneers extended their studies further and\h were limited in scope. The microscope was one of\h\h the most important inventions back then, and\h fingerprints would just have been a convenient\h\h sample to observe even under lower magnification.\h Therefore, though the early pioneers recorded\h\h their observations on fingerprints, they did not\h comment on other important aspects such as their\h\h unique and permanent nature. This indicates that\h the identification of a person using fingerprints\h\h was not under scientific consideration for a long\h time in modern history. It was the early 1800s\h\h when Thomas Bewick published a book and signed it\h using his thumbprint. This gentleman was primarily\h\h a wood engraver and he started imprinting\h his other works using his thumbprint as well,\h\h believing it was unique to himself.\h Hermann Welcker, a German anthropologist,\h\h hypothesized that fingerprints do not change in\h structure throughout life. To prove this, he took\h\h his own set of fingerprints twice, forty years\h apart, once in 1856 and again in 1897. Expectedly,\h\h Welcker observed no difference between the two\h sets, following which he published a study to\h\h establish the permanent nature of fingerprints. Let's move over to the eastern part of the world.\h\h Sir William Herschel was a British administrator\h in the Bengal province in India who later went\h\h on to join the Indian Civil Services.\h While working in his official capacity,\h\h Herschel entered into a transaction with a local\h businessman and asked him to put a handprint on\h\h the back of the document. Years later, recounting\h this practice, Herschel would refer to himself\h\h as the first person to use fingerprints for\h identification. However, this claim is not true,\h\h as authorizing business transactions by signing\h them and adding a handprint or a fingerprint was\h\h fairly standard practice in that part of India.\h Nevertheless, Herschel’s contribution cannot\h\h be ignored as he was the first person to include\h fingerprints in official government transactions.\h\h He started using fingerprints as a tool to detect\h frauds and false impersonations in official\h\h work such as pension and salary distribution.\h Realizing the utility of fingerprints, Herschel\h\h tried to concretely establish their use in the\h administration. However, he was unsuccessful\h\h in convincing the officials above him and his\h vision was never fulfilled. Nonetheless, just like\h\h Hermann Welcker, Sir William Herschel went on to\h demonstrate the permanent nature of fingerprints\h\h by studying his own patterns over fifty years. In the\h following year, he visited Japan and eventually\h\h settled as a superintendent at an important\h hospital. This piqued Faulds’ interest and\h he studied fingerprints extensively to establish\h\h the unique nature of fingerprints. Soon after,\h his hospital was broken into.

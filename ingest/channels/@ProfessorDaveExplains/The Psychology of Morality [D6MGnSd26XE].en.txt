In life we are often faced with difficult\h decisions. We may come across situations where we\h\h have to ask ourselves, what is right and what is\h wrong? What is good and what is bad? While we may\h\h have some sense of how to go about answering these\h questions, how do we know that we are correct? How\h\h do we even know that there are objective answers\h to these questions? Philosophers have been\h\h wrestling with morality for a long time. There’s\h actually a whole branch of philosophy called moral\h\h philosophy. Moral philosophy has three main\h branches, each with its own specific set of\h\h questions. The first branch, known as meta-ethics,\h asks big picture questions like: What is morality?\h\h What is justice? Is there truth? Another branch is\h normative ethics. What motivates behaviors\h linked to morality, like trust and cooperation?\h Some psychologists are particularly interested\h in how morality develops. Psychologist\h\h Lawrence Kohlberg tested these ideas by\h presenting a series of moral dilemmas to\h\h participants of varying ages, and asking them\h to explain their reasoning. Here’s one example\h\h of a moral dilemma the participants might\h hear, which is called the Heinz Dilemma.\h In this scenario, a woman was dying from cancer.\h There was only one drug that the doctors thought\h\h would save her: a special form of radium that a\h druggist in the same town had recently discovered.\h\h The drug was expensive to make, but the druggist\h was charging ten times what the drug cost him\h\h to produce. Kohlberg\h asked his participants if Heinz should have\h\h stolen the drug, as well as the reasoning\h behind their decision. Kohlberg didn’t care\h\h if the participants thought Heinz was right\h or wrong. Instead, he wanted to know why they\h\h made the moral decision they did. According to\h Kohlberg’s theory, moral development can be\h\h broken down into three primary levels, each with\h two stages. At this level,\h\h a child’s moral decisions are primarily shaped\h by the expectations of adults and consequences\h\h for breaking the rules. What do the adults\h in my life think I should do? A child in\h\h the first stage of Preconventional Morality\h understands morality through obedience and\h\h punishment. Rules are fixed and absolute, and\h they need to be obeyed to avoid punishment.\h\h When they reach stage two, children are\h able to account for an individual’s point\h\h of view. This is the level\h\h where adolescents and adults are internalizing\h the moral standards they’ve learned from others.\h\h They are able to accept authority and\h the norms of the group they’re a part of.\h\h In stage three, people are focused on living up\h to social expectations and roles. There is an\h\h emphasis on being a “good boy” or “good girl”,\h being “nice,” and having the respect of others.\h\h Stage four is about maintaining social order.\h People begin to consider society as a whole\h\h when making decisions and focus on maintaining\h order by following rules and doing one’s duty.\h The final level in Kohlberg’s theory is\h Postconventional Morality. This is when\h\h people start to understand morality in an abstract\h manner. People begin at stage five to recognize\h\h that others may have differing values, opinions,\h and beliefs. Rules are important to follow,\h\h but all the members of a society should work\h together to agree on what the rules are.\h\h Finally, stage six is when people have\h internalized universal ethical principles\h\h and abstract values like dignity, equality, and\h justice. They will follow these principles even\h\h if they conflict with laws. So if we look at the\h Heinz Dilemma, a person at stage six might say\h\h that Heinz was right to steal the drug. Kohlberg also\h put a large emphasis on justice, and didn’t take\h\h into account other things that might drive moral\h reasoning, like compassion and empathy. Still,\h\h Kohlberg’s theory of moral development played\h a critical role in the emergence of moral\h\h psychology as a field.

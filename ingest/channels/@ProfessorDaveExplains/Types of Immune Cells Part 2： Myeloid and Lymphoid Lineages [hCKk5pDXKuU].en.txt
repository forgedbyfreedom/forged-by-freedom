In the previous tutorial we began to discuss\h immune cells, and we did this by going over\h\h immune cell function in a general way.\h Now it’s time to get more specific about\h\h the different types of immune cells, and which\h of these functions is performed by each type.\h Most immune cells originate in the bone marrow and\h develop from hematopoietic stem cells, which also\h\h give rise to red blood cells and platelets. To\h become a white blood cell, a hematopoietic stem\h\h cell can follow one of two major differentiation\h tracks, those being the myeloid lineage and the\h\h lymphoid lineage. Hematopoietic stem cells develop\h through these lineages into fully developed\h\h immune cells by following specific sets of\h molecular cues in the bone marrow. Historically,\h\h many of these cells were characterized based on\h how they appeared under a microscope. However,\h\h we now have molecular tools to define cells based\h on the proteins they express on their surfaces,\h\h and thus based on which genes\h are turned on inside the cell.\h Let’s start with the myeloid-derived innate immune\h cells, which include macrophages, monocytes,\h\h granulocytes, and dendritic cells. As we’ve\h mentioned previously, macrophages are the\h\h professional eaters of the immune system. They are\h very good at killing pathogens by phagocytosis,\h\h the process by which a pathogen is engulfed\h and ingested. Macrophages are also important\h\h for keeping tissues free of cellular debris\h by phagocytosing dead and dying self cells.\h\h Macrophages that live in these\h tissues under normal conditions\h\h are called tissue-resident macrophages, and\h these cells originate in the fetal yolk sac,\h\h rather than the bone marrow. Almost every\h tissue has a specific type of tissue-resident\h\h macrophage which performs important maintenance\h roles during homeostasis. During inflammation,\h\h a process we will describe at length a bit later,\h cells can also enter tissues from the bloodstream\h\h as monocytes, which then differentiate\h into macrophages. Macrophages are also\h\h important for secreting cytokines\h that can shape the immune response.\h Next, we have a family of immune cells\h called granulocytes. This name comes\h\h from the fact that these cells are preloaded\h with granules full of antimicrobial chemicals\h\h that they can release during an infection\h to kill pathogens. Neutrophils, eosinophils,\h\h basophils, and mast cells are all\h types of granulocytes. Neutrophils,\h\h eosinophils, and basophils are also called\h polymorphonuclear leukocytes, or PMNs,\h\h because of the unique, multi-lobed shape of\h their nuclei that can be seen under a microscope.\h Neutrophils are the most numerous immune cell type\h in the blood, and the first type of immune cell to\h\h arrive on the scene when tissues become infected.\h They are extremely good at phagocytosis and\h\h killing pathogens, especially bacteria, but\h they have a very short lifespan compared to\h\h other types of white blood cells. These are also\h the cells which produce the pus that we can see\h\h during an infection. Eosinophils and basophils are\h much less common in the blood than neutrophils.\h\h Eosinophils release many cytokines and other\h chemical factors that are important for promoting\h\h wound healing and tissue repair. Basophils\h are the least common, but the largest type\h\h of granulocyte in the blood. These secrete\h cytokines and other signaling molecules.\h\h Mast cells are similar to basophils, but do not\h have a multi-lobed nucleus and are usually found\h\h in connective tissue instead of circulating\h in the bloodstream. Mast cells, eosinophils,\h\h and basophils are important for killing large\h parasites that are too big to be destroyed by\h\h phagocytosis. However, these cells are also\h responsible for promoting allergies and asthma.\h Next up, dendritic cells also are capable\h of phagocytosis, but unlike macrophages\h\h and the granulocytes, they aren’t usually\h heavily involved in pathogen clearance.\h\h Instead, dendritic cells patrol tissues for\h signs of infection so they can take antigens\h\h back to structures called lymph nodes,\h which we will discuss in the next tutorial,\h\h and activate T cells that will be able to\h specifically neutralize that kind of pathogen.\h\h In this way, dendritic cells are critical\h for linking the innate and adaptive immune\h\h systems. There are also innate immune cells\h that are derived from the lymphoid lineage,\h\h and these cells are called innate lymphoid\h cells. This group of cells includes\h\h natural killer cells, which recognize and\h kill cancer cells and virally infected cells.\h\h There are many other kinds of innate lymphoid\h cells, which sense microbial cues and stress\h\h signals, allowing them to quickly respond\h to a diverse range of microbial threats.\h Innate immune cells are effective at identifying\h and clearing different pathogens, but they can\h\h only recognize generalized features of pathogens,\h not specific ones, and they can’t differentiate\h\h between good and bad microbes. First up are B cells.\h\h The main role of these is to make antibodies,\h which are incredibly specific Y-shaped proteins\h\h that can bind to, and coat pathogens\h and foreign particles. These cells\h communicate with each other through cytokines\h\h and chemokines to carry out their diverse array\h of effector functions which help destroy pathogens\h\h and heal damaged tissue.

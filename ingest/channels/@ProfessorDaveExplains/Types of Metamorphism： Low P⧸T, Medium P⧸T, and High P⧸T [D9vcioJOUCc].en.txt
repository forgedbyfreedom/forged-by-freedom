In the previous tutorial, we learned about\h how metamorphic rocks form. But there are\h\h differences in the metamorphic process that\h result in different types of metamorphic rock.\h\h More specifically, geologists group metamorphic\h rocks into “facies”, which represent a common\h\h group of minerals that form over certain ranges\h of pressure-temperature conditions. This makes\h\h it much easier to compare metamorphic rocks\h from different locations around the world,\h\h and also to relate metamorphic rocks\h to certain geologic environments.\h\h For example, the sodium amphibole glaucophane\h is only formed at low temperatures and very high\h\h pressures, and since these conditions are almost\h exclusively found in subduction zones, you can\h\h reasonably assert that any glaucophane-containing\h rock formed there. This is how geologists use\h\h metamorphic facies in a nutshell. Rock X\h contains minerals A, blast and cruise, therefore\h\h it must have formed in geologic environment Z.\h There are eleven different metamorphic facies,\h\h which can be put into three groups that\h describe the major types of metamorphism;\h\h they are the low P/T, medium P/T, and high P/T\h series, where P is pressure and T is temperature.\h The low P/T series describes contact metamorphism,\h which occurs when an igneous body intrudes into\h\h an area through numerous fractures and\h “bakes” the adjacent rock, so to speak.\h\h Contact metamorphism occurs at low pressure, since\h igneous intrusions mainly occur at shallow depths\h\h and at high temperatures, due to the heat supplied\h by the magma. A similar type of metamorphism,\h\h hydrothermal, occurs when hot aqueous\h fluids move though large areas of rock,\h\h supplying heat and chemically active ions\h that cause recrystallization. This occurs\h\h on a large scale around mid-ocean ridges where\h heated ocean water circulates through the crust,\h\h altering olivine and pyroxene to serpentine\h (Mg2SiO4 + MgSiO3 + 2H2O = Mg3Si2O5(OH)4). This\h\h is the water that is later released during\h subduction and is responsible for volcanism there.\h Generally, low P/T metamorphism occurs\h below a pressure of about 2 kilobars. We\h\h will now consider the progressive contact\h metamorphism of a mafic rock, like basalt.\h\h It should be noted that at lower temperatures,\h less than 300 degrees Celsius, recrystallization\h\h may not occur due to poor reaction kinetics. The\h lowest grade metamorphic facies is the zeolite\h\h facies, occurring below 200 degrees Celsius.\h This is our starting point. Zeolite refers to a\h\h group of framework silicate minerals that have\h large amounts of open space in their lattice.\h\h Cat litter often contains zeolites, due to their\h ability to absorb large amounts of water. Once we\h\h pass about 300 degrees Celsius, we encounter\h the albite-epidote hornfels facies. Hornfels\h\h is a general name for a rock that has undergone\h contact metamorphism, and albite-epidote refers to\h\h the stable minerals under these conditions. Albite\h is the sodium endmember of plagioclase feldspar,\h\h and epidote is a somewhat obscure iron-containing\h sorosilicate. At around 500 degrees Celsius,\h\h hornblende will begin to crystallize as we\h move into the hornblende hornfels facies.\h\h Hornblende is a calcium amphibole that appears\h over a wide range of metamorphic conditions.\h\h At around 650 degrees Celsius, it becomes too\h hot for the water-containing amphiboles to exist,\h\h and they break down into pyroxene plus quartz\h plus water; these conditions mark the pyroxene\h\h hornfels facies. Finally, at a temperature of 800\h degrees Celsius and a pressure of only 2 kilobars,\h\h we reach the sanidinite facies, where the\h potassium feldspar polymorph, sanidine,\h\h forms. However, these conditions are\h rare, since it is difficult to attain\h\h such high temperatures so shallow in the crust. The medium P/T series encompasses orogenic, or\h\h mountain-building events, where the intense heat\h and pressure associated with colliding continents\h\h metamorphoses the rock near the collision.\h Medium P/T metamorphism generally occurs at\h\h pressures between about 2 and 12 kilobars. Let’s\h now consider the progressive orogenic metamorphism\h\h of a basalt, and like before, we start in the\h zeolite facies. From there, we move into the\h\h prehnite-pumpellyite facies starting at around\h 200 degrees Celsius and 3 kilobars. The next\h\h three facies probably represent the overwhelming\h majority of all metamorphic rocks on Earth as they\h\h span a large range of P-T conditions. Between 300\h and 500 degrees Celsius is the greenschist facies,\h\h which gets its name from the green color of the\h chlorite minerals that crystallize under these\h\h conditions. Other common minerals in greenschist\h rocks are the magnesium-rich amphibole actinolite,\h\h epidote, and calcite. Between 500 and 700\h degrees Celsius is the amphibolite facies,\h\h which is unsurprisingly marked by the formation\h of amphiboles, especially hornblende along\h\h with the high-pressure mineral garnet. Common\h coexisting minerals are plagioclase and augite.\h\h Finally, we reach the granulite facies, which\h occur from 700 degrees Celsius all the way up\h\h to the onset of melting. It is characterized\h by a largely anhydrous mineral assemblage\h\h that commonly includes clinopyroxene,\h orthopyroxene, plagioclase, and garnet.\h The high P/T series mainly occurs in subduction\h zones, where cold oceanic lithosphere is brought\h\h to great depths, where the temperature would\h normally be much higher. In these environments,\h\h P/T conditions can range from 4 kilobars and 200\h degrees Celsius all the way up to 50 kilobars and\h\h 950 degrees Celsius, which is equivalent to a\h depth of 170 kilometers, meaning that pieces of\h\h oceanic lithosphere are capable of being subducted\h to this incredible depth and later returned to the\h\h surface without melting. Let’s now consider\h the progressive high-pressure metamorphism of\h\h basalt. Like with orogenic metamorphism, we begin\h with the zeolite facies and then move through the\h\h prehnite-pumpellyite facies. From here, we move\h into the blueschist facies at around 250 degrees\h\h Celsius and 5 kilobars. The blueschist facies\h gets its name from the blue color of the amphibole\h\h glaucophane, which is abundant in these rocks.\h Other minerals in blueschists are garnet, epidote,\h\h and aragonite, which is the high-pressure\h polymorph of calcite. As the pressure and\h\h temperature are increased to around 14 kilobars\h and 500 degrees Celsius, we move into the eclogite\h\h facies, which is dominated by omphacite, garnet,\h and kyanite, and which exists with glaucophane\h\h at lower pressures. Omphacite is a pyroxene\h solid solution between augite and jadeite,\h\h and kyanite is the high-pressure polymorph of\h Al2SiO5. By the time we reach about 30 kilobars\h\h and 600 degrees Celsius, amphibole completely\h breaks down and quartz transforms into coesite,\h\h its high-pressure polymorph, and at around\h 35 kilobars, graphite turns to diamond.\h\h Metamorphism that produces coesite and diamond\h is said to be ultra-high-pressure metamorphism.\h In addition to examining the progressive\h metamorphism of mafic rocks, we have also\h\h looked at metamorphic intensity using a mudstone\h as an example. Let’s talk a bit more about the\h\h metamorphism of felsic rocks. First off, the\h parent rock, or rock that is to be metamorphosed,\h\h is called the protolith. So let’s consider\h some different protoliths. Let’s start simple,\h\h with sandstone as our protolith. Since\h sandstones tend to be very rich in quartz,\h\h when they are metamorphosed the quartz just\h recrystallizes into larger crystals. This type\h\h of rock is called quartzite. The metamorphism of\h felsic igneous rocks, like granite, is also pretty\h\h straightforward and most of what will happen\h is recrystallization of the original minerals\h\h into larger crystals. High grade metamorphism\h of a granite will transform it into a gneiss.\h\h The metamorphism of limestone, which typically\h contain mainly calcite with some quartz, involves\h\h a lot of calcite recrystallization forming the\h metamorphic rock, marble. Other minerals like\h\h quartz will react with calcite to form silicate\h minerals, such as wollastonite, or CaSiO3.\h One of the most common types of rock that\h get metamorphosed in mountain building events\h\h are mudstones, as these are the most abundant\h sedimentary rock. They also show the most dramatic\h\h transformations as they are metamorphosed,\h going form slate to phyllite, to schist,\h\h and finally gneiss, as previously discussed.\h We can relate these types of metamorphic rocks\h\h to our metamorphic facies and the following are\h equivalent: slate to prehnite-pumpellyite facies,\h\h phyllite to greenschist facies, schist\h to amphibolite facies, and gneiss to\h\h granulite facies. Being rich in aluminum and\h potassium, the minerals that recrystallize\h\h during metamorphism will also be rich in these\h elements. In addition to quartz and feldspar,\h\h aluminum-rich minerals like the aluminosilicate\h polymorphs, staurolite, and cordierite,\h\h and potassium-rich sheet silicates like muscovite\h and biotite commonly occur in these rocks.\h And that concludes our introduction to metamorphic\h\h rocks, and therefore to the\h types of rocks in general.

All silicates are made of the\h\h same basic building block, the silica tetrahedron,\h which is simply a silicate, or SiO44- anion. The\h\h structure of the silica tetrahedra is a central\h silicon atom that is bonded to four oxygen atoms.\h\h You can think of it as a pyramid, with a silicon\h atom in the center and an oxygen at each corner.\h Silicate minerals are divided into subclasses\h based on their arrangement and linkage of silica\h\h tetrahedra, which can range from a single,\h isolated tetrahedron bonded to a metal,\h\h to a complex three-dimensional framework\h of silica tetrahedra. The ability of silica\h\h tetrahedra to link together, or polymerize, to\h form a diverse array of intricate structures\h\h stems from its unique mesodemic bonding, where\h each of the oxygen anions has one of its two\h\h valence charges satisfied by bonding with its\h tetrahedron’s central silicon atom, thus leaving\h\h a remaining charge of negative one at each corner\h of the tetrahedron that is available for bonding.\h\h Because of this, a given silica tetrahedron can\h bond with up to four other silica tetrahedra,\h\h or none at all. The structural complexity of\h the silicate minerals increases with the degree\h\h of polymerization, and even though quartz, or\h SiO2, is about as simple as it gets chemically,\h\h it is structurally very complex, with its\h three-dimensional framework of silica tetrahedra.\h\h We will first discuss the silicates that do\h not polymerize at all, the orthosilicates.\h Orthosilicates, also called nesosilicates,\h are the simplest subclass of silicates,\h\h as the silica tetrahedra are not polymerized,\h meaning there is no sharing of oxygen atoms\h\h between silica tetrahedra. Instead, the silica\h tetrahedra are linked by bonding with cations,\h\h like magnesium, iron, calcium, and aluminum.\h Unlike silicon, which bonds to four oxygen\h\h anions to form tetrahedra, larger cations\h like magnesium and iron bond, or coordinate\h\h with 6 oxygen anions to form octahedra. Moreover,\h even larger cations, like the alkali metals and\h\h calcium, coordinate with eight or more oxygen\h atoms, depending on the mineral’s structure.\h\h Knowing that the coordination of an ion\h is dependent on its radius, it is easy to\h\h predict which atoms can substitute for others in\h a crystal’s lattice. This brings us to the most\h\h common type of orthosilicate minerals, olivine.\h Olivine has two octahedral sites that contain\h\h medium-sized divalent cations, in addition to its\h tetrahedral silicon. In a typical olivine, the\h\h octahedral sites contain a mixture of magnesium\h and ferrous iron, with more magnesium than iron.\h\h Geologists call this mixing of different ions on\h a crystallographic site, solid solution. In the\h\h previous tutorial, we discussed endmembers, which\h are minerals that represent the chemical extremes\h\h of a group. In the olivine group, fayalite, or\h Fe2SiO4, is the iron endmember, and forsterite,\h\h or Mg2SiO4, is the magnesium endmember, with\h complete solid solution existing between them.\h\h Another important group of orthosilicates\h is garnet, which comes in a wide variety of\h\h compositions with extensive solid solution. Its\h base formula is X3Y2(SiO4)3, where the X-site\h\h is an eight-fold site that can be occupied by\h calcium, magnesium, ferrous iron, and manganese,\h\h and the Y-site is a six-fold site that\h typically contains aluminum and ferric iron.\h\h The last group of orthosilicates we\h will discuss are the aluminosilicates,\h\h which have a chemical formula\h of Al2SiO5. Unlike the others,\h\h the minerals in this group all have the exact\h same formula. Where they differ is their crystal\h\h structure. When aluminum, which\h has a charge of 3+, substitutes for silicon,\h\h which has a charge of 4+, the mineral must somehow\h balance out this gained negative charge. This can\h\h be accomplished by picking up a hydrogen ion or\h substituting a lower charged cation, like sodium,\h\h for a higher charged one, like calcium. Their name is derived from\h\h their structure, which forms rings of six\h silica tetrahedra stacked atop one another.\h\h They are a small group, with only a handful\h of commonly-occurring minerals, including\h\h the popular gemstones beryl and tourmaline, with\h beryl also being an important ore of beryllium.\h\h Let’s consider the structure of beryl,\h which has the chemical formula Al2Be3Si6O18.\h\h Between its stacked 6-membered rings of\h silica tetrahedra are sheets of laterally\h\h linked 12-membered rings composed of alternating\h aluminum octahedra and beryllium tetrahedra that\h\h cross-link the rings of silica tetrahedra.

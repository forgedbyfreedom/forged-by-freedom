In the previous tutorial we began learning\h about the most important class of minerals,\h\h the silicates, and we examined\h three of the silicate subclasses,\h\h those being orthosilicates, disilicates, and\h cyclosilicates. There are three more subclasses\h\h to go over, so let’s talk about these now. The next group of silicates, called chain\h\h silicates, or inosilicates, are divided into two\h subgroups: pyroxenes, which have single chains\h\h of silica tetrahedra, and amphiboles, which\h have double chains. Pyroxenes are composed\h\h of silica tetrahedra linked together in a zig-zag\h pattern, where one oxygen atom in each tetrahedron\h\h is shared with the adjacent tetrahedron. They are\h similar to ring silicates, in that each silica\h\h tetrahedron shares two of its oxygens with other\h silica tetrahedra, having a silicon to oxygen\h\h ratio of 1 to 3. Pyroxenes have the general\h formula XY(Si,Al)2O6 where the Y-cation is\h\h a medium-sized cation in 6-fold coordination, and\h the X cation is either a 6-fold or 8-fold cation,\h\h depending on whether the crystal structure\h is orthorhombic or monoclinic. Orthorhombic\h\h pyroxenes are called orthopyroxenes, and they\h have 6-fold cations on both their X and Y sites,\h\h making them more symmetrical than monoclinic\h pyroxenes, or clinopyroxenes, which have 8-fold\h\h cations, typically calcium, on their X-site. The orthopyroxene group only has two endmembers,\h\h the iron-rich ferrosilite, and magnesium-rich\h enstatite, with complete solid solution between\h\h them. The clinopyroxenes are more diverse\h and widespread, forming a sizeable fraction\h\h of most mafic rocks. Like orthopyroxene, there is\h extensive solid solution among the clinopyroxenes.\h\h Some endmember clinopyroxenes are the\h calcic, magnesium-rich diopside and iron-rich\h\h hedenbergite, the sodic, aluminum-rich jadeite\h and iron-rich aegirine, and the lithium-bearing\h\h spodumene. Gemstone-quality jadeite is used as\h jade and spodumene is an important ore of lithium.\h\h The most abundant pyroxene is augite, which is a\h calcic pyroxene with an intermediate composition.\h Double chain silicates, better known as\h amphiboles, combine two single chains\h\h of tetrahedra by having every other silica\h tetrahedron share three of its oxygen atoms\h\h with the adjacent tetrahedron, forming a ring-like\h structure. They have a silicon to oxygen ratio of\h\h 1 to 2.5 and always have extra anions, typically\h volatiles, like hydroxyl, chlorine, and fluorine.\h\h Amphiboles can crystallize from a magma, but they\h usually form during the hydrothermal alteration of\h\h pyroxenes, via the reaction pyroxene + quartz +\h water yields amphibole. There is an entire class\h\h of metamorphic rocks called amphibolite because\h their mineralogy is dominated by amphiboles.\h Amphiboles have the general formula,\h AB2C5(Si,Al)8O22W2, and are sometimes\h\h called the “garbage can mineral”, because of their\h ability to take in so many different elements.\h\h The A, B, and C sites are distinguished based\h on the size of cation they can accommodate,\h\h and the W-site contains hydroxyl, chlorine,\h and fluorine. The A-site can either be vacant,\h\h or contain the large cations potassium, sodium,\h and/or calcium, the B-site contains medium-large\h\h cations, such as calcium, sodium, and ferrous\h iron, and the C-site is composed of strips\h\h of octahedra that are sandwiched between the\h chains of tetrahedra, gluing them together. The\h\h octahedra are occupied by the medium-sized cations\h magnesium, iron, and aluminum. Like the pyroxenes,\h\h amphiboles are broken down into orthoamphiboles\h and clinoamphiboles, and of course, there is\h\h extensive solid solution among their respective\h endmembers. Compared to clinoamphiboles,\h\h orthoamphiboles are chemically simple, having a\h vacant A-site, and only magnesium and iron in the\h\h B-and C-sites. Clinoamphiboles are classified\h based on the dominant cation in the B-site,\h\h being either calcium amphiboles, sodium-calcium\h amphiboles, or sodium amphiboles.\h\h Blueschist, which is a class of metamorphic rock\h common in subduction zones, gets its name from the\h\h blue color of the sodium amphibole glaucophane\h (Na2[Mg3Al2]Si8O22(OH)2). The medium-grade\h\h metamorphic rock, amphibolite, typically\h contains calcium amphiboles, such as hornblende.\h Sheet silicates, or phyllosilicates, take\h double chain silicates one step further. Each\h\h silica tetrahedron shares 3 oxygen atoms with\h the adjacent tetrahedron, forming an infinite\h\h sheet of shared tetrahedra. Sheet silicates\h have a silicon to oxygen ratio of 2 to 5.\h\h The sheet of silica tetrahedra, referred to simply\h as the tetrahedral, or ‘T” sheet is stacked on top\h\h of an octahedral, or “O” sheet, which is a plane\h of divalent and trivalent cations like iron,\h\h magnesium, and aluminum. The T and O\h sheets stack together to form layers,\h\h which are bonded through strong, molecular\h bonds. They can stack in one of two ways;\h\h a T-O, or “open-faced sandwich” configuration,\h with one T-sheet bonded to one O-sheet, or a\h\h T-O-T configuration, where a single octahedral\h sheet is sandwiched by two tetrahedral sheets. The\h\h sheet silicates are a diverse group of minerals\h that include the clay minerals, the micas, the\h\h serpentine group, the chlorite group, and talc. The differences between the groups of sheet\h\h silicates are the types of bonding\h that hold together the sheeted layers.\h\h For example, in the mineral talc, Mg3Si2O5(OH)4,\h they are held together by van der Waals forces,\h\h which are relatively weak. It is no coincidence\h that talc, with a Mohs hardness of 1 is the\h\h softest mineral; so soft in fact, that it is\h used to make baby powder. On the other hand,\h\h the micas are held together by weak ionic bonds\h that arise from electrostatic interactions\h\h between large, interlayer cations, like potassium,\h and the negatively charged T-O-T layers.\h\h This structure is called T-O-T + C, with the\h “C” standing for cation. The difference between\h\h these two groups are the tetrahedral cations; in\h talc, only Si4+ occupies the tetrahedral sites,\h\h while in the micas, one Si4+ swaps for Al3+,\h creating a net negative charge among the layers,\h\h and allowing for stronger electrostatic bonds\h between the layers, with the large cations acting\h\h as the glue. The chlorite minerals have a similar\h structure, with negatively charged layers due to\h\h aluminum substitution; however, instead of being\h glued together by cations, they have an additional\h\h positively charged octahedral sheet between\h layers. This structure is called T-O-T + O.\h\h The last group has a T-O structure, and like\h talc, the layers are neutral, but are in this\h\h case held together by stronger hydrogen bonds.\h It turns out that most of the sheet silicates\h\h have a similar hardness, between about 2 and\h 3, except for the T-O-T group, which are the\h\h softest of all the minerals. The weak bonding\h between layers gives all the sheet silicates\h\h perfect basal cleavage, causing them to peel\h apart like the plies in a roll of toilet paper.\h We will now move on to the most abundant\h subclass of minerals in the Earth’s crust,\h\h the framework silicates, or tectosilicates. These\h minerals have a backbone of silica tetrahedra\h\h that share all four of their oxygens with\h neighboring silica tetrahedra. The framework\h\h silicates have a silicon to oxygen ratio of 1 to\h 2, with aluminum often substituting for silicon in\h\h the tetrahedral sites, as in the feldspar mineral\h anorthite, where half its tetrahedral sites are\h\h occupied by aluminum. The complete polymerization\h of tetrahedra creates a complex, rigid structure\h\h with large spaces in between, allowing for\h a wide range of interstitial large cations.\h The basic chemical formula for\h feldspars is (Ca,Na,K)(Si,Al)4O8,\h\h and can be separated into three\h endmembers: anorthite, CaAl2Si2O8,\h\h albite, NaAlSi3O8, and potassium feldspar,\h KAlSi3O8. There is complete solid solution\h\h between albite and anorthite, with intermediate\h compositions being called plagioclase. There is\h\h also complete solid solution between albite and\h potassium feldspar, being called alkali feldspar.\h\h For two isostructural minerals to have solid\h solution, their cations must meet one of the\h\h two following criteria: One, they have a similar\h ionic radius, or two, they have the same charge.\h\h Since potassium and calcium do not meet these\h criteria, solid solution does not occur between\h\h anorthite and potassium feldspar. Plagioclase\h is the most abundant mineral in the crust,\h\h being a main component of both the continental\h and oceanic crust. Another important framework\h\h silicate is quartz, which is composed\h entirely of silica tetrahedra, having\h\h the formula SiO2. The silicon-oxygen covalent\h bond is one of the strongest single bonds,\h\h making quartz and the other framework silicates\h very hard. Quartz is actually a polymorph of SiO2,\h\h meaning its structure changes with pressure and\h temperature. Tridymite and cristobalite are the\h\h high-temperature polymorphs, with coesite and\h stishovite being the high-pressure polymorphs.\h\h Stishovite can only be formed at pressures\h greater than 10 gigapascals and is often\h\h used to identify ancient asteroid impacts.

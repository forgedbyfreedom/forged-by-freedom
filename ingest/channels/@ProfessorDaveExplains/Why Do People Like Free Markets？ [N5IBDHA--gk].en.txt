It goes without saying that\h we want economies to grow.\h\h When economies grow, incomes increase, along\h with a higher overall standard of living.\h\h It also leads to more innovation, which in\h turn leads to even more economic growth.\h\h But which economic system leads to the most\h economic growth? Most economists argue that\h\h market economies do. However, as we will learn\h in this tutorial, they are far from perfect.\h As we learned in the previous tutorial, market\h economies are economic systems in which production\h\h and prices are determined by unrestricted\h competition between privately owned businesses\h\h and consumers. When economists talk about markets,\h they typically are talking specifically about\h\h free markets, and generally promote them\h as the best way to drive economic growth.\h Consumers and producers usually cooperate\h to give each other what they want.\h\h But why should they cooperate when we live in such\h a competitive society? According to Adam Smith,\h\h a pioneer in the field of economics who helped\h develop how we understand the subject today,\h\h competition and our own self-interest actually\h help to keep the marketplace functioning.\h\h Smith was a Scottish philosopher who noticed\h that buyers and sellers consider only their\h\h own self-interest, or personal gain,\h when making deals. In his classic book\h\h The Wealth of Nations, Smith wrote, “It is not\h from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer,\h\h or the baker that we expect our dinner, but\h from their regard to their own interest.”\h Smith also observed that people respond\h in a predictable way to both positive and\h\h negative incentives. Incentives are hopes of\h a reward or fear of a penalty. For example,\h\h consumers will buy more in response to\h the positive incentive of lower prices,\h\h or buy less in response to higher prices. Here’s\h another example. A restaurant realizes it can make\h\h more money if it just sells smoothies instead of\h a full menu of food, so it stops selling food in\h\h order to make more of a profit. Economists refer\h to the struggle among producers for the money of\h\h consumers as “competition”. While self-interest\h is the motivating force behind the free market,\h\h competition is the regulating force. According to\h Smith, voluntary trade in a free market produces\h\h huge benefits that are far greater than those of\h a regulated, planned economy. Each free exchange\h\h creates signals about which goods and services are\h desirable and how difficult they are to bring to\h\h a market. These signals, captured in the price\h system, lead to the aforementioned competition\h\h that leads to pursuing self-interest. Smith\h used a metaphor to describe this phenomenon\h\h called The Invisible Hand. He said this\h Invisible Hand guides all free markets.\h Free market economies have four distinct\h advantages. First, because free markets\h\h are self-regulating, they can respond efficiently\h to rapidly-changing conditions. Producers attempt\h\h to provide only stuff that consumers want,\h and at prices consumers are willing to pay.\h\h Second, free market economies have the\h most economic freedom of any system.\h\h Producers get to produce what they want, and\h consumers get to buy what they want. Third,\h\h because competition encourages innovation, free\h market economies encourage economic growth. And\h\h fourth, free market economies end up creating a\h wider variety of goods and services than any other\h\h economic system because consumers, in essence,\h have the power to decide what gets produced.\h\h This concept is called consumer sovereignty. As great as free market economies are,\h\h they are not perfect. Here are four\h distinct disadvantages to free markets.\h\h First, free market economies contain natural\h monopolies. A monopoly is when a single seller\h\h dominates a market. A natural monopoly is a type\h of monopoly that happens when extremely high\h\h start-up costs in a specific industry can make it\h difficult for competitors to enter the industry.\h\h For example, utilities, such as sewer or\h electricity services, require a huge supply\h\h network that would be economically inefficient,\h and not worth it for most producers to invest in.\h\h Second, free market economies can lead to fewer\h public goods and services. Remember, public goods\h\h and services describe products that no one can be\h prevented from consuming, and anyone who consumes\h\h them can do so without reducing their availability\h to others. However, in a free market, their\h\h availability is often reduced. So if a society\h decides that education is a public service,\h\h in other words, everyone deserves it, a free\h market can’t guarantee that this value is upheld.\h\h Third, free market economies can lead to negative\h externalities. When there are absolutely no\h\h regulations or restrictions, businesses may try to\h get away with anything in order to make a profit.\h\h A common example of this is that a\h business may not care about pollution.\h\h Perhaps it decides that the easiest way to get\h rid of its waste is by dumping it into a river,\h\h which can be extremely damaging to ecosystems.\h Finally, free market economies can lead to what\h\h economists refer to as a “race to the bottom.”\h In other words, as producers attempt to become\h\h as profitable as possible, they often reduce\h quality and cut corners, in order to make money.\h\h This can lead to things like unsafe\h working conditions or unsafe products.\h As we will learn over the next few tutorials,\h societies have responded to these disadvantages\h\h of free market economies in various\h ways. However, governments have had\h\h mixed success in responding to them, and\h there is a constant struggle for balance\h\h between allowing markets to be free, in order to\h reap the benefits of a free market, and regulating\h\h markets with government intervention in order to\h curb the disadvantages. This is a complicated yet\h\h crucial concept to understand, so let’s move\h forward and learn more about economic systems.

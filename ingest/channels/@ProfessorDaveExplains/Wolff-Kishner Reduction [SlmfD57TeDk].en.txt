Earlier in the series we learned about Clemmensen\h reduction, which is a way to reduce aldehydes and\h\h ketones all the way down to the alkane, thereby\h removing an oxygen atom entirely. This is\h\h performed in the presence of zinc and hydrochloric\h acid. However, there is an alternative method that\h\h will achieve this transformation under basic\h conditions, which can be a useful technique,\h\h and this is called Wolff-Kishner reduction.\h This method, which actually was developed first,\h\h was articulated independently in 1911\h and 1912 by German chemist Ludwig Wolff\h\h and Russian chemist Nikolai Kishner. The transformation was carried out with hydrazine\h\h and a strong base like potassium hydroxide at very\h elevated temperatures in a high-boiling solvent,\h\h such as ethylene glycol, which refluxes at 197oC,\h or even diethylene glycol, which boils at 245oC.\h So how is this achieved? The mechanism\h first involves the formation of a hydrazone.\h\h We have seen these types of condensation reactions\h before, which proceed with elimination of water,\h\h so beyond stating that one of these\h nitrogen atoms attacks the carbonyl,\h\h we don’t really need to illustrate this in\h detail. It is actually sometimes advantageous\h\h to pre-form the hydrazones, since they\h are usually stable, crystalline compounds,\h\h and the yield is often better if this first\h step is followed by an isolation. Alternatively,\h\h if going all at once, the water formed in\h the condensation should be distilled off.\h Now, hydrazones are in equilibrium\h with a tautomeric form, the diimide,\h\h and this equilibration is catalyzed by\h base. Notice the additional proton on\h\h this carbon in the diimide tautomer, as well\h as the double bond between the nitrogens.\h\h Then, the slow step for this reaction is the\h deprotonation of the diimide by the strong\h\h base. This leads to loss of nitrogen gas and the\h formation of a carbanion, which is an especially\h\h reactive species. The lifetime of a carbanion\h in a protic solvent is extremely short, and\h\h protonation by the conjugate acid of the base used\h in the previous step leads directly to the alkane.\h Given the utility of this transformation in\h synthesis, this method has been used often\h\h and it is still used today. It has been used on\h ton scale for the synthesis of pharmaceuticals,\h\h although liquid hydrazine, which\h is also used in rocket fuels,\h\h is a hazardous reagent at such high\h temperatures and must be handled by\h\h experts, as it detonates in the gas\h phase even in the absence of oxygen.\h Given the drastic conditions used in the original\h Wolff-Kishner protocol, it is not surprising that\h\h many workers have endeavored to develop milder\h conditions, and this has given rise to a host of\h\h modifications of the reaction. A complete\h survey of all the methods inspired by Wolff\h\h and Kisher’s original work is beyond the scope\h of this tutorial. However, just to give you a\h\h flavor of where modern synthesis can lead, let’s\h mention the improvement made in 1962 by American\h\h chemist Donald Cram, Nobel Prize winner for his\h work in supramolecular chemistry. His group found\h\h that if you use potassium tert-butoxide as a\h base and dimethyl sulfoxide as the solvent,\h\h these are exceptionally basic conditions and the\h reaction can then be run at room temperature.\h\h There are other modifications which now allow the\h use of this reaction even on sensitive substrates.\h To show you an example of a complex\h strategy involving Wolff-Kishner reduction,\h\h suppose you have to carry out the following\h transformation, the alkylation of pyrrole at\h\h this particular carbon. It turns out that this\h is a difficult operation. You may suggest a\h\h Friedel-Crafts alkylation, but this does not work\h cleanly, and one obtains a mixture of two isomers\h\h plus dialkylated products. Conditions to do\h this cleanly have not been found. However,\h\h it turns out that pyrrole 2-carboxylic acid is\h readily available, and this can be converted for\h\h protection to the ethyl thioester by simple means. These thioesters were shown to be excellent\h\h substrates for Friedel-Crafts\h acylation, though not alkylation,\h\h at C-4. The reaction occurs exclusively at that\h position. The reason for this is easy to see\h\h by drawing the Wheland intermediates leading\h to all the three possible isomeric products.\h\h Of the three isomeric intermediates, A and C place\h a positive charge next to the electron-withdrawing\h\h thioester group, and are therefore disfavored,\h whereas isomer B has the positive charge\h\h in a position that is not conjugated with the\h thioester and is therefore much more stable.\h Under Wolff-Kishner conditions, the acyl group at\h C-4 is reduced to an alkyl group, the thioester is\h\h hydrolyzed to a carboxylic acid, and it is known\h that pyrrole 2-carboxylates readily decarboxylate,\h\h so they lose CO2 under basic conditions, which\h leaves us with the desired product we mentioned\h\h earlier. This three-step procedure is still\h the best way to prepare 3-alkyl pyrroles,\h\h and it highlights the utility of the Wolff-Kishner\h reduction in situations where a carbonyl is\h\h needed to carry out a specific transformation\h efficiently, but must then be removed to prepare\h\h a target which is not accessible directly. Such\h strategies are common in organic synthesis. In\h\h summary, the Wolff-Kishner reduction is still\h the most direct way to reduce ketones to alkanes,\h\h and it is important for all organic\h chemists to be familiar with it.

You've heard of spin, a quantum property related to angular momentum. We're used to thinking of spin as a kind of intrinsic angular momentum that a particle has, even if it isn't literally spinning in space. But in quantum field theory, spin comes from symmetries. The standard model is built on the principle of Lorentz invariance. This invariance comes from Einstein's reworking of gravitation, and it means the laws of physics have to look the same to all observers, regardless of how fast they're moving or in which direction. The group of transformations that corresponds to this symmetry is called the Lorentz group. When you classify all the possible types of particles allowed by this Lorentz invariance, you find that each one is labeled by a number that describes how it transforms under Lorentz transformations. That number is what we call spin. So to put it simply, spin is a label that tells us how a particle responds to fundamental symmetries. There are other spin-like symmetries. Isospin was originally introduced to explain a puzzle in nuclear physics. Protons and neutrons are not the same particle. One is charged, the other is neutral. However, they behave almost identically under the strong force. To account for this, physicists proposed that protons and neutrons are just two states of a more fundamental object, which they called a nucleon. Isospin treats this like a kind of symmetry, similar to how spin treats up and down states. The proton has I equals one-half, or isospin up, and the neutron has I equals negative one-half, or isospin down. This is an approximate symmetry that holds well within strong interactions. However, other particles have isospin values. Another important quantity, hypercharge, is a quantum number that accounts for isospin, electric charge, and flavor. All of these symmetries are essential parts of formulating the theories behind electrodynamics, chromodynamics, and the weak interaction that we'll discuss over the next few tutorials.

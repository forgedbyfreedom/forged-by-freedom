 what is the relationship between intermittent fasting and disordered eating? First off, intermittent fasting is a popular dietary trend where you alternate periods of fasting with periods of eating. Less than two months ago, I made a video sharing a new systematic review which found no significant differences in multiple health outcomes between intermittent fasting and continuous energy restriction. Another new study published this year finds that individuals engaging in intermittent fasting report elevated eating disorder symptomatology, including heightened weight and shape concerns, greater binge eating behavior, and even self-induced vomiting and laxative use compared to community norms. Over 60% of participants cited weight loss or fat loss as their number one reason for intermittent fasting. They also reported a daily intake of 1100 to 1200 calories on their fasting days, which is indicative of how restrictive many of them are. It is important to note that these findings were based on a survey and that no causal relationship can be established between intermittent fasting and ED symptomatology. It is possible that one, those already engaging in disorder eating behaviors or more prone to disorder eating or eating disorders are more likely to adopt intermittent fasting. Two, intermittent fasting triggers or exacerbates ED symptoms. Or three, some other third variable is at play. When we talk the health benefits of popular eating strategies, we too often overlook the psychological implications of dyes that restrict what, how much, or when we eat. Given the apparent increased association with ED symptomatology, extra attention should be paid to how any kind of restrictive eating may impact your relationship with food.

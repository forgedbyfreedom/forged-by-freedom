I'm Dr. Brian Chuck, a UCLA health urologist and kidney cancer specialist. When we find a mass on the kidney, we face a critical decision. Do we treat it or do we observe it? Some tumors are benign and pose no threat. Some tumors can be life-threatening. If we can figure out which type it is, we can determine the best course of action. Using nuclear medicine imaging to characterize kidney masses can provide crucial information to help guide us. Nuclear medicine uses small amounts of a radioactive material to create detailed images of internal organs. This can tell us whether a tumor is likely benign or malignant. It can also tell us if the cancer has spread which is a clear sign of malignancy. Different tracers tell us different things. The spec using technesium 99 cestibi is a method that helps us characterize kidney tumors. This tracer highlights cells rich in mitochondria. High uptake often suggests a benign tumor or a low risk cancer. No uptake suggest a malignant tumor. FDG PETCT uses a radioactive sugar that cancer cells may absorb. Many kidney cancers do not take up this substance. It's difficult to use to evaluate kidneys because kidney tissue and urine also appear bright, making interpretation difficult. A new tool, Amunopet CT with TLX 250 targets CA9. CA9 is a marker found in 95% of clear cell renal cancers. TLX 250 is accurate. 94% of positive scans indicate clear cell kidney cancer. It's a great tool when we want to confirm if treatment is the best option. Our goal is to treat aggressive tumors and to avoid unnecessary treatment for benign tumors. Nuclear medicine imaging is a safe way to guide these decisions and impact patient outcomes.

Death was present every minute, and people lay infected with measles, especially the most defenseless children, for lack of something as essential as a vaccine. I didn't choose to be a nurse; nursing chose me. I started in the world of nursing when I was 16 years old. I had dropped out of high school at 15 due to financial problems. In 1989, the medical intern invited me to a community called Loreto. We were 12 hours away. We rode horses and left at 4 in the morning. We arrived in Loreto as it was getting dark. It was incredibly hot, very sunny, very hot. My arrival in this town was to see extreme poverty, and people lay infected with measles. Mothers cried for their children, for their grandchildren who were dying or who had already died. It was my first time seeing death, and knowing that a vaccine could save their lives was something that troubled me and made me fall in love with it, and to this day, it has kept me here. For 25 years, I dressed as I traveled to the indigenous and mestizo communities, sometimes walking, but also on horseback, carrying the thermos. My father lent me a horse named Pajarito, and that's the one I rode to the indigenous communities to vaccinate the children. The children would run and say, "Here comes Julia!" They ran because they were afraid of me, but at the same time, they knew I was bringing hope. They knew they would be cured, that they wouldn't get sick. There shouldn't be deaths from vaccine-preventable diseases; that shouldn't be allowed. In 30 years, I saw how the vaccines reached the most remote communities. I saw how people were convinced to get vaccinated. I saw how people stopped dying from measles. I would like to be remembered with a thermos of vaccine, with a thermos of vaccines, walking the streets. That's how I would like to be remembered, talking to people and telling them that vaccines save lives.

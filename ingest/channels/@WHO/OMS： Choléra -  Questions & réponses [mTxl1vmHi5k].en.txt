Cholera is an acute diarrheal disease that progresses very rapidly and is transmitted through water. You become infected either by drinking water contaminated with Vibrio, the bacterium responsible for the disease, or by consuming fresh fruits or vegetables contaminated with the virus. It's a transmission known as oral transmission. Treatment is very simple in theory; in any case, it's enough to rehydrate the patient, initially with oral rehydration sachets, and then in severe cases, intravenous rehydration. In severe cases, antibiotics are also quite often given. First and foremost, access to clean drinking water is crucial. If we give populations universal access to clean drinking water, we will solve the cholera problem; that's very clear. Furthermore, in case of illness, it's essential to be able to treat patients as quickly as possible. As I was saying, it's a disease that progresses very rapidly, so the vaccine is a supplementary tool to either prevent the onset of the disease or... For example, during humanitarian crises or in response to an epidemic, by targeting groups with insufficient access to clean water and sanitation who are considered particularly vulnerable to cholera, there are currently two cholera vaccines prequalified by the WHO. These two vaccines are identical and are administered orally. Since a stockpile was established three years ago, approximately 5 million doses have been distributed. No significant side effects have been observed, so these vaccines are considered safe and do not cause side effects. They have an efficacy of around 65% at 5 years and are therefore quite easy to administer, as highly qualified personnel are not required. The only difficulty, therefore, lies in the fact that the two doses necessitate a second visit a few weeks after the first dose to ensure maximum protection.

Some cautionary words have endured for millennia. There is a human right to health, and we are all working towards it, including this September as  One of the Sustainable Development Goals is to reaffirm the right to health within the UN framework, which is why the German G7 Presidency has placed a strong emphasis on health, and we will continue to address this issue throughout our presidency. Firstly, because the human right to health can only be realized if a sustainable healthcare system exists or is being developed in every country of the world. Secondly, because we recognize that global interconnectedness makes us all dependent on the health of one country being the health of another. This means that the effectiveness of a global healthcare system determines the health of other countries, just as it determines security and stability. National responsibility and global shared responsibility are two sides of the same coin. Germany has placed three health-related topics on its agenda: firstly, due to current events, what lessons should we learn from the Ebola catastrophe? Secondly, how can we better combat neglected and poverty-related tropical diseases? Thirdly, and this concerns both industrialized and developing countries equally, what can we do about increasing antibiotic resistance? All three topics have a common thread: they can only be successfully addressed if all countries...  To ensure compliance with the standards of the health system, these health regulations, the International Health Regulations, were developed by the WHO ( World Health Organization), and the implementation process must become binding for everyone. These are not recommendations; these guidelines are so important that they must be implemented. Why are the G7 countries now addressing what has already been internationally agreed upon? Because we are economically strong, because we as G7 states possess a high level of scientific expertise, and because we share common values stating that the dignity of every human being is indivisible and therefore cannot be limited to the G7 countries. I am pleased to be here today as a guest at the World Health Organization, together with the German Minister of Health, to report on our plans. The Ebola catastrophe in West Africa has painfully demonstrated once again how urgently international action is needed in crisis situations. 26,000 people have contracted the virus, there are over 11,000 deaths, and we are witnessing the significant destabilization of the affected countries. We have achieved initial successes: Liberia is Ebola-free. We hope this will also be the case for the other two countries. But a victory has not yet been won. The fight is only truly won when there are no new cases of illness, but it's only really won when we are prepared for the next crisis. That means when we have learned the lessons from this crisis, and the lesson we all must learn is that we should have reacted sooner. Therefore, we must ask what can be done. We need a global disaster preparedness plan, and the World Health Organization plays a central role here. What do we expect from the World Health Organization, what must it achieve, and what must the member countries contribute? In my view, the World Health Organization is the only international organization that enjoys political legitimacy in global health issues, and therefore it's crucial to make its structures efficient. Certainly, it's an advantage of the World Health Organization that it has 150 country offices, six regional offices, and a central office. This decentralized structure is important, but let's be honest: decentralization often leads to indecisiveness and inability to act. Therefore, the advantage of the decentralized organization must be utilized in such a way that the three levels at which the World Health Organization operates are linked with clear lines of authority and operational structures, so that ultimately everyone knows who is supposed to report what to whom and when.  What this entails is certainly easier said than done, but I believe that, especially now, as we seek to learn from this experience, it is crucial that everyone steps outside their comfort zone and accepts this task so that we can improve together. While World Health Organization holds central legitimacy in health policy, it cannot be the sole organization responsible for a globally oriented disaster preparedness plan. This means we must achieve effective collaboration between the WHO, the UN system as a whole, and the World Bank. To examine this interconnectedness more closely, the Prime Minister of Norway, President Garnas, and I have asked the Secretary-General of the United Nations to establish a high-level panel. This panel, chaired by President Kikete of Tanzania, has now commenced its work and is expected to submit a report by the end of the year outlining what we need to learn from international organizations, which organizations can contribute what, and how we can be better prepared for future health crises as well as natural disasters. I am convinced that if we react faster, if we act more quickly, and if we have clear and decisive action plans, then we will also succeed in combating a crisis like Ebola more effectively next time. However, one thing we must not forget, despite all this...  We must not lose sight of international cooperation. The starting point must be that every country develops and can develop its own health system as sustainably as possible, and therefore the development of health systems is a central task. Germany is taking on this task; we will provide 200 million euros in the affected countries this year and next, million of which will go to West Africa alone, to promote the sustainable development of structures in the sense of helping people to help themselves. Ladies and gentlemen, a second topic on our G7 agenda is neglected and poverty-related tropical diseases. This is related to the fact that many countries are simply too poor to be able to build their own health systems today, which is why we must help them. With regard to neglected tropical diseases, the word "neglected" should n't really be taken literally; 1.4 billion people are affected, and the interesting thing about these diseases is that often with relatively little material expenditure, the suffering of hundreds of millions of people can be combated and they can be made healthy again. Here, on the one hand, we need the long-term development of health systems, on the other hand, of course, the results of the pharmaceutical industry, and thirdly, and this is important...  Logistics of distribution, and therefore it is very important to work closely with the affected countries. We have very different results on the African continent, for example, regarding these logistical capabilities. Therefore, this all connects with the development of a healthcare system, but not only with the development of a healthcare system, but also with the development of sound administrative structures. The third topic that we want to address from the German side during our G7 presidency is sustainability. This is the issue antibiotic resistance. The national academies of sciences have given us guidance on this, as well as on the issue of neglected military diseases. And I believe here we come to an issue of crucial importance for humanity as a whole, both in developed and less developed countries. It is about ensuring the effectiveness of existing antibiotics and restricting their use to strictly necessary medical applications, not only for humans but also for animals. Here, we want to agree on the strictest possible standards among the industrialized countries at our G7 meeting. The active ingredients must be used restrictively because once they become resistant, it is different than, for example...  It is much, much more difficult to research new active ingredients for neglected tropical diseases. The pharmaceutical industry is important as a partner in the fight against diseases, but it cannot achieve anything that research cannot. I have been told how difficult it is to research new active ingredients for antibiotics that can replace the old ones. Therefore, we must proceed carefully and have an approach that is a health-oriented approach for both humans and animals in order to make progress here. I am very pleased that the World Health Assembly has committed itself to developing a global action plan for antimicrobial resistance for the first time, and every country should have such a plan. Germany adopted such a plan in its cabinet a few days ago, and there is also very close cooperation between human and veterinary medicine. Ladies and gentlemen, let me conclude by thanking all the staff of the World Health Organization, as well as the representatives of the other UN organizations, the representatives of the non-governmental organizations, and the representatives of the foundation. I believe that when we think about the Ebola crisis, we know what non-governmental organizations and local aid workers on the ground accomplished. I want to emphasize the German, not the international, aspect here. I'll mention the Red Cross and Doctors Without Borders, but only as an example of the many, many others who show endless dedication, together with the staff of international organizations. Therefore, I have one final request, and we will also advocate for this at the G7: everyone is important in the fight for the human right to health. The structures have changed over the last few years. When the World Health Organization was founded, there was this organization; now there are foundations. There are very good, dedicated non-governmental organizations. Everyone fighting for the same goal is equally important. Let's find a way to work together and not against each other. The task is so great and the efforts are so important that every helping hand is needed. And therefore, in conclusion, once again a heartfelt thank you for your work on behalf of the people. Germany, in its G7 presidency, will try to support and advance your work together with the other G7 partners. Heartfelt Thank you

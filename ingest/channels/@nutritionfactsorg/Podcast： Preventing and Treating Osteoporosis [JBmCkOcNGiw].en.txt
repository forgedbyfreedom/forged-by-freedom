Today, we look at bone mineral density screening, the safety and efficacy of oral and injective medications, which foods may protect our bones, and more. Nearly one in five adults in the world may&nbsp; have osteoporosis—that's hundreds of millions&nbsp;&nbsp; of people. The word "osteoporosis"&nbsp; literally means porous bone. Now,&nbsp;&nbsp; most of our bone is actually porous to begin&nbsp; with. Bone mineral density is considered to&nbsp;&nbsp; be the standard measure for the diagnosis of&nbsp; osteoporosis, although the bone mineral density&nbsp;&nbsp; cutoff for an osteoporosis diagnosis is kind&nbsp; of arbitrary. Using the standard definition,&nbsp;&nbsp; osteoporosis may affect about one in 10&nbsp; women by age 60, two in 10 by age 74,&nbsp;&nbsp; and six or seven out of 10 by age 90. Osteoporosis&nbsp; is typically thought of as a disease in women,&nbsp;&nbsp; but one-third of hip fractures occur in&nbsp; men. The lifetime risk for osteoporotic&nbsp;&nbsp; fractures for 50-year-old white women and men&nbsp; is 40% and 13.3% respectively. The good news&nbsp;&nbsp; is that osteoporosis need not occur. Based on a&nbsp; study of the largest twin registry in the world,&nbsp;&nbsp; less than 30% of osteoporotic fracture risk is&nbsp; heritable, leading the researchers to conclude&nbsp;&nbsp; prevention of fractures, even in the oldest&nbsp; elderly, should focus on lifestyle interventions.&nbsp;&nbsp; This is consistent with the enormous variation&nbsp; of hip fracture rates around the world,&nbsp;&nbsp; with the incidence of hip fracture varying&nbsp; tenfold or even a hundredfold between countries,&nbsp;&nbsp; suggesting that excessive bone loss is not&nbsp; just an inevitable consequence of aging. The United States Preventive Services Task&nbsp; Force, an independent scientific panel&nbsp;&nbsp; that sets evidence-based clinical prevention&nbsp; guidelines, recommends osteoporosis screening,&nbsp;&nbsp; such as DEXA bone mineral density scan, for all&nbsp; women aged 65 and older, and potentially starting&nbsp;&nbsp; even earlier than 65 for women at increased&nbsp; risk, such as those with a parental history&nbsp;&nbsp; of hip fracture, smokers, excessive alcohol&nbsp; consumers, or those with low body weight. What should you do if you're diagnosed,&nbsp; or more importantly, what should you do&nbsp;&nbsp; to never be diagnosed? Before exploring&nbsp; the drugs offered to treat osteoporosis,&nbsp;&nbsp; there are some drugs that may cause it. So&nbsp; let's start there. Stomach acid-blocking&nbsp;&nbsp; proton pump inhibitor drugs, so-called&nbsp; PPIs with brand names like Prilosec,&nbsp;&nbsp; Prevacid, Nexium, Protonix, and AcipHex, are&nbsp; among the most popular drugs in the world,&nbsp;&nbsp; raking in billions of dollars a year. But then&nbsp; in 2006, two observational studies out of Europe&nbsp;&nbsp; suggested an association between intake of&nbsp; this class of drugs and increased risk of&nbsp;&nbsp; hip fracture. By 2010, the growing evidence&nbsp; forced the US Food and Drug Administration&nbsp;&nbsp; to issue a safety alert implicating PPI use with&nbsp; fractures of the hip, wrist, and spine. By now,&nbsp;&nbsp; there have been dozens of such studies involving&nbsp; more than two million people that overall show&nbsp;&nbsp; higher hip fracture rates among both long-&nbsp; and short-term users at all dose levels. The irony is that most people taking these&nbsp; drugs shouldn't even be on them in the first&nbsp;&nbsp; place. These PPIs are only FDA approved for&nbsp; 10 days of use for the treatment of H. pylori,&nbsp;&nbsp; up to two weeks for heartburn, up to 8 weeks for&nbsp; acid reflux disease, and for two to six months for&nbsp;&nbsp; ulcers. Yet, in a community survey, most users&nbsp; remained on these drugs for more than a year,&nbsp;&nbsp; and more than 60% of patients were taking them for&nbsp; inappropriate reasons, often wrongly prescribed&nbsp;&nbsp; for "indigestion," for instance. Calls to stop&nbsp; this massive overuse from regulatory authorities&nbsp;&nbsp; have fallen on deaf doctor ears, and now&nbsp; that they're available over the counter,&nbsp;&nbsp; the problem of overuse may have just gotten&nbsp; even worse. They can be hard to stop taking&nbsp;&nbsp; since many patients experience withdrawal&nbsp; symptoms that can last for weeks. In fact,&nbsp;&nbsp; if you take normal healthy volunteers without&nbsp; any symptoms and put them on these drugs for&nbsp;&nbsp; 2 months and then covertly switch them&nbsp; to a placebo without their knowledge,&nbsp;&nbsp; all of a sudden they can develop acid-related&nbsp; symptoms such as heartburn or acid regurgitation,&nbsp;&nbsp; ending up worse off than they were before&nbsp; they even started taking the drugs. In addition to bone fractures, this class&nbsp; of drugs has also been linked to increased&nbsp;&nbsp; risk of other possible long-term&nbsp; adverse effects, such as pneumonia,&nbsp;&nbsp; intestinal infections, kidney failure, stomach&nbsp; cancer, and cardiovascular disease. In fact,&nbsp;&nbsp; the blood vessel effects could explain&nbsp; the case report of abrupt onset profound&nbsp;&nbsp; erectile dysfunction in a healthy young man&nbsp; after initiating over-the-counter Prilosec&nbsp;&nbsp; and also premature death. There are individuals&nbsp; with conditions like Zollinger-Ellison syndrome,&nbsp;&nbsp; stricken with tumors that can cause&nbsp; excess stomach acid secretion,&nbsp;&nbsp; for whom the risk versus benefit of long-term use&nbsp; may be acceptable. But that's a far cry from the&nbsp;&nbsp; hundred million PPIs prescribed annually in the&nbsp; United States alone to deal with acid reflux. Without drugs, recommendations include weight&nbsp; loss, smoking cessation, avoiding fatty meals,&nbsp;&nbsp; especially within 2 to 3 hours of&nbsp; bedtime, increased fiber consumption,&nbsp;&nbsp; and an overall more plant-based&nbsp; diet because non-vegetarianism&nbsp;&nbsp; is associated with twice the odds of&nbsp; acid reflux-induced inflammation. Other&nbsp;&nbsp; classes of drugs associated with hip&nbsp; fracture risk include antidepressants,&nbsp;&nbsp; antiparkinson's drugs, antianxiety drugs,&nbsp; oral corticosteroids, and the other major&nbsp;&nbsp; class of heartburn drugs, the H2 blockers&nbsp; such as Pepcid, Zantac, Tagamet, and Axid. Excessive bone loss is not just an&nbsp; inevitable consequence of aging,&nbsp;&nbsp; and one of the things we can do to lower&nbsp; our risk and that of our patients is to&nbsp;&nbsp; take into account the potential skeletal side&nbsp; effects of the drugs we take and prescribe. What about the drugs prescribed to treat&nbsp; osteoporosis? First, let me address efficacy.&nbsp;&nbsp; Drug therapy for osteoporosis is recommended&nbsp; for those 50 and older with a history of past&nbsp;&nbsp; hip or spine fractures, those with hip or spine&nbsp; T-scores of -2.5 or less, and postmenopausal&nbsp;&nbsp; women or men aged 50 and older who don't meet that&nbsp; cutoff but have an estimated 20% or higher risk of&nbsp;&nbsp; major osteoporotic fracture over the subsequent&nbsp; decade or an estimated 3% or greater risk of hip&nbsp;&nbsp; fracture. Specifically, a T-score is a measure of&nbsp; how dense your bones are compared to a 30-year-old&nbsp;&nbsp; white woman, since that's the standard. We tend&nbsp; to lose bone as we age. You can be labeled as&nbsp;&nbsp; having osteoporosis even if you have completely&nbsp; normal bone density for your age. Of course,&nbsp;&nbsp; just because your bone density may be normal&nbsp; doesn't mean it's necessarily optimal,&nbsp;&nbsp; which is why the National Osteoporosis&nbsp; Foundation set out these guidelines for&nbsp;&nbsp; drug treatment. Though another reason, perhaps,&nbsp; is because it gets substantial funding from the&nbsp;&nbsp; drug industry which rakes in literally billions&nbsp; of dollars in profits from osteoporosis drugs. What does the science say? The primary drug class&nbsp; used to treat osteoporosis is the bisphosphonates&nbsp;&nbsp; sold under brand names such as Fosamax, Actonel,&nbsp; Boniva, and Reclast. They're most effective at&nbsp;&nbsp; reducing vertebral fractures, cutting the&nbsp; risk in postmenopausal women from 2.8% down&nbsp;&nbsp; to 1.44%. That's a relative risk reduction of&nbsp; 50% but an absolute risk reduction of only 1.4%,&nbsp;&nbsp; meaning you'd have to treat 71 women to&nbsp; prevent just one woman from getting a&nbsp;&nbsp; vertebral fracture. Unfortunately, the diagnosis&nbsp; of vertebral fracture is kind of wishy-washy,&nbsp;&nbsp; and depending on how you define the changes on&nbsp; an x-ray, the prevalence of vertebral fractures&nbsp;&nbsp; can vary as much as 3% to 90% in the same elderly&nbsp; population—almost none to almost all. They're all&nbsp;&nbsp; so poorly predictive of back pain or disability.&nbsp; Vertebral fractures can certainly lead to back&nbsp;&nbsp; pain and reduced physical function, but only&nbsp; about a third are symptomatic at all. The most&nbsp;&nbsp; harmful fractures are hip fractures. Despite most&nbsp; of the clinical trials for osteoporosis treatment&nbsp;&nbsp; being funded by the drug companies themselves, no&nbsp; primary prevention benefit from bisphosphonates&nbsp;&nbsp; has been found for hip fractures. In other words,&nbsp; taking these drugs has not been convincingly shown&nbsp;&nbsp; to prevent fracturing your hip in the first place.&nbsp; However, having a prior fracture doubles your risk&nbsp;&nbsp; of breaking another bone, so for those at high&nbsp; risk, these bisphosphonate drugs may decrease&nbsp;&nbsp; the odds of hip fracture by 25%. So, the absolute&nbsp; risk reduction is again only about 1%. It may take&nbsp;&nbsp; treating 91 people for 3 years to prevent one&nbsp; hip fracture. An overconfidence in the power of&nbsp;&nbsp; pills and procedures for disease prevention may&nbsp; be one of the reasons doctors and patients alike&nbsp;&nbsp; may undervalue diet and lifestyle approaches. In a&nbsp; study of those undergoing bone density scans, the&nbsp;&nbsp; average yearly fracture risk that would motivate&nbsp; most participants to consider preventive medicine&nbsp;&nbsp; was 50% to 60%, much higher than their actual&nbsp; risk. Most patients want to be told the truth;&nbsp;&nbsp; they want to be told what the chances are that&nbsp; the drugs will actually benefit them. But there's&nbsp;&nbsp; a tension between the patient's right to know&nbsp; and the likely reduction in their willingness&nbsp;&nbsp; to take the drug if they knew the truth. In&nbsp; the bone density scan survey, participants&nbsp;&nbsp; greatly overestimate their own personal risk for&nbsp; hip fracture, thinking it was around 19% over&nbsp;&nbsp; the next 5 years, which is more than 10 times&nbsp; higher than their actual risk of 1.4%. Rather&nbsp;&nbsp; than disabusing them of this overestimate, some&nbsp; suggest physicians should do the opposite—stoke&nbsp;&nbsp; fear in patients who refuse to comply with&nbsp; medication recommendations by increasing their&nbsp;&nbsp; perceived susceptibility to fragility fractures&nbsp; as well as their perceived severity of suffering&nbsp;&nbsp; from a fragility fracture. Since emotion can&nbsp; be more motivating than reason and anecdotal&nbsp;&nbsp; evidence can be more effective than evidence, a&nbsp; graphic scenario of suffering and incapacitation&nbsp;&nbsp; after hip fracture will enhance emotional&nbsp; perception of this threat. But hip fractures&nbsp;&nbsp; can indeed be devastating and are associated&nbsp; with a significant risk of ensuing death. So, what about all the lives these drugs must be&nbsp; saving? Well, only about a quarter of the deaths&nbsp;&nbsp; following fractures may be attributable to the&nbsp; fracture itself, so most of the mortality risk may&nbsp;&nbsp; just be a consequence of the comorbidities—that&nbsp; is, the other diseases and poor health status—of&nbsp;&nbsp; people who tend to fracture their hips. So, they&nbsp; might have died anyway in that same timeframe&nbsp;&nbsp; even if they hadn't broken any bone. This may&nbsp; help explain why osteoporosis drug treatments,&nbsp;&nbsp; particularly bisphosphonates, fail to&nbsp; significantly reduce overall mortality. Okay, so the efficacy of the primary&nbsp; drug class used to treat osteoporosis&nbsp;&nbsp; is so slight on an individual basis that&nbsp; most patients, if truly fully informed,&nbsp;&nbsp; likely would not take them. And that's&nbsp; before even talking about the side effects,&nbsp;&nbsp; which I'll turn to right now. Potential lack of&nbsp; efficacy is not the only reason why most people&nbsp;&nbsp; prescribed bisphosphonate drugs for osteoporosis&nbsp; like Fosamax, Actonel, Boniva, and Reclast,&nbsp;&nbsp; may stop taking them within a year. They're too&nbsp; rare but devastating side effects—osteonecrosis&nbsp;&nbsp; of the jaw and atypical femur fractures—that&nbsp; contributed to around A-50% drop in the use&nbsp;&nbsp; of this class of drugs when they came to light.&nbsp; The New York Times article noting the decline&nbsp;&nbsp; explained the reasoning: reports of the drugs&nbsp; causing jawbones to rot and thigh bones to snap&nbsp;&nbsp; in two have shaken many osteoporosis patients&nbsp; so much that they say they would rather take&nbsp;&nbsp; their chances with the disease. The jaw rot&nbsp; syndrome that prevents many from initiating&nbsp;&nbsp; treatment for fear of their jaw falling off can&nbsp; severely impact many aspects of quality of life&nbsp;&nbsp; but is exceedingly rare, affecting at most one&nbsp; in a thousand patients treated for osteoporosis. Atypical femur fractures can occur more frequently&nbsp; though, as many as one in 300 users treated for 3&nbsp;&nbsp; years. They're called atypical because&nbsp; they occur not after a fall or trauma,&nbsp;&nbsp; but just during routine activities&nbsp; like walking, twisting at the waist,&nbsp;&nbsp; or even just standing still. Your femur, your&nbsp; thigh bone, the biggest bone in your body,&nbsp;&nbsp; just breaks in half—too cruel an irony from&nbsp; a drug that's supposed to protect your bones. These bisphosphonates work by inhibiting&nbsp; the action of a type of bone cell called&nbsp;&nbsp; osteoclasts. Your entire skeleton is constantly&nbsp; being remodeled, with bone added in some spots&nbsp;&nbsp; and taken away in others to conform to changing&nbsp; demand. The cells that are continually laying&nbsp;&nbsp; down new bone are called osteoblasts,&nbsp; and the ones that chisel away old bone&nbsp;&nbsp; are called osteoclasts. It makes sense then that&nbsp; curbing the osteoclasts could prevent bone loss,&nbsp;&nbsp; but by reducing the active remodeling process,&nbsp; the bisphosphonates can freeze the skeleton,&nbsp;&nbsp; allowing for the accumulation of microcracks&nbsp; over time, resulting in stress fractures. Other osteoporosis drugs, like denosumab, sold as&nbsp; Prolia, prevent the same formation of osteoclasts&nbsp;&nbsp; in the first place and have been plagued by the&nbsp; same kinds of rare but disturbing side effects. As&nbsp;&nbsp; with anything in life, it all comes down to risks&nbsp; versus benefits. I mean, how many hip fractures&nbsp;&nbsp; are prevented for every femur that snaps? It&nbsp; depends on your race and how long you're on&nbsp;&nbsp; these drugs. After 5 years, in white women, 36&nbsp; hip fractures are prevented for every atypical&nbsp;&nbsp; femur fracture. Hispanic women only get half&nbsp; the benefit, about 18 to 1, and for Asian women,&nbsp;&nbsp; it's only about five hip fractures prevented for&nbsp; every femur fracture caused. The study claims it&nbsp;&nbsp; failed to accrue enough data on black women. At&nbsp; 10 years of drug exposure, the ratios get worse—16&nbsp;&nbsp; to 1 for white women, 5 to 1 for Hispanic&nbsp; women, and only 1.5 to 1 for Asian women,&nbsp;&nbsp; meaning that the devastating fractures prevented&nbsp; and caused in Asian women are nearly comparable. A nationwide survey of resident physicians&nbsp; found that knowledge regarding osteoporosis&nbsp;&nbsp; diagnosis and treatment was poor, with a&nbsp; particularly striking lack of knowledge&nbsp;&nbsp; regarding the serious drug side effects. The&nbsp; good news is that after stopping the drugs,&nbsp;&nbsp; the risk of femur fracture rapidly dropped by&nbsp; 70% within a year, leading to the suggestion&nbsp;&nbsp; that a drug holiday be considered after a few&nbsp; years on the drug to help mediate the risk. How crazy is that atypical femur fracture data,&nbsp; right? I just cannot get over the irony of it all.&nbsp;&nbsp; Okay, so between the lack of sufficient efficacy&nbsp; and these rare but devastating side effects,&nbsp;&nbsp; what can we do personally and how can we counsel&nbsp; our patients to protect their skeletons? In 12&nbsp;&nbsp; short years, government panels have gone from&nbsp; suggesting widespread calcium supplementation&nbsp;&nbsp; may be necessary to protect our bones&nbsp; to "do not supplement." What happened? It all started with a 2008 study in New&nbsp; Zealand. Short-term studies had shown that&nbsp;&nbsp; calcium supplementation may drop blood pressure&nbsp; by about a point. The effect appears transient,&nbsp;&nbsp; disappearing after a few months—better than&nbsp; nothing. And excess calcium in the gut can&nbsp;&nbsp; cause fat malabsorption by forming soap&nbsp; fat, reducing saturated fat absorption,&nbsp;&nbsp; and increasing fecal saturated fat content.&nbsp; And indeed, if you take a couple Tums along&nbsp;&nbsp; with your half bucket of KFC, up to twice&nbsp; as much fat could end up in your stool.&nbsp;&nbsp; And with less saturated fat absorbed into&nbsp; your system, your cholesterol might drop. So, the New Zealand researchers were expecting&nbsp; to lower heart attack rates by giving women&nbsp;&nbsp; calcium supplements. To their surprise, there&nbsp; appeared to be more heart attacks in the calcium&nbsp;&nbsp; supplement group. Was this just a fluke? All&nbsp; eyes turned to the Women's Health Initiative,&nbsp;&nbsp; the largest and longest randomized control&nbsp; trial of calcium supplementation. The name&nbsp;&nbsp; might sound familiar—that's the study&nbsp; that uncovered how dangerous hormone&nbsp;&nbsp; replacement therapy was. Would it&nbsp; do the same for calcium supplements? The Women's Health Initiative&nbsp; reported no adverse effects. However,&nbsp;&nbsp; the majority of participants were&nbsp; already taking calcium supplements&nbsp;&nbsp; before the study started. So effectively, the&nbsp; study was just comparing higher versus lower&nbsp;&nbsp; dose calcium supplementation, not calcium&nbsp; supplements versus no calcium supplements. What if you go back and just see what happened to&nbsp; the women who started out not taking supplements&nbsp;&nbsp; and then were randomized to the supplement&nbsp; group? Those who started calcium supplements&nbsp;&nbsp; suffered significantly more heart attacks&nbsp; or strokes. Thus, high dose or low dose,&nbsp;&nbsp; any calcium supplementation seems to&nbsp; increase cardiovascular disease risk. So, researchers went back digging through&nbsp; other trial data for heart attack and stroke&nbsp;&nbsp; rates in women randomized to calcium&nbsp; supplements with or without vitamin D&nbsp;&nbsp; added and confirmed the danger. And most&nbsp; of the population studies agreed—users of&nbsp;&nbsp; calcium supplements tended to have increased&nbsp; rates of heart disease, stroke, and death. The supplement industry was not happy,&nbsp; accusing the researchers of relying in part&nbsp;&nbsp; on self-reported data—like they just asked&nbsp; people if they had a heart attack or not,&nbsp;&nbsp; rather than verifying it. And indeed, long-term&nbsp; calcium supplementation causes all sorts of&nbsp;&nbsp; gastrointestinal distress, including twice the&nbsp; risk of being hospitalized with acute symptoms&nbsp;&nbsp; that may have been confused with a heart&nbsp; attack. But no, the increased risk was seen&nbsp;&nbsp; consistently across the trials, whether&nbsp; the heart attacks were verified or not. Okay, but why do calcium supplements increase&nbsp; heart attack risk but not calcium you get in&nbsp;&nbsp; your diet? Perhaps because when you take&nbsp; calcium pills, you get a spike of calcium&nbsp;&nbsp; in your bloodstream that you don't get just&nbsp; eating calcium-rich foods. Within hours of&nbsp;&nbsp; taking supplemental calcium, the calcium levels&nbsp; in the blood shoot up and can stay up as long as&nbsp;&nbsp; 8 hours. This evidently produces what's called&nbsp; a hypercoagulable state—your blood clots more&nbsp;&nbsp; easily, which could increase the risk of&nbsp; clots in the heart and brain. And indeed,&nbsp;&nbsp; higher calcium blood levels are tied to&nbsp; higher heart attack and stroke rates. So, the mechanism may be calcium supplements&nbsp; leading to unnaturally large, rapid,&nbsp;&nbsp; and sustained calcium levels in the blood, which&nbsp; can have a variety of potentially problematic&nbsp;&nbsp; effects. Calcium supplements have been widely&nbsp; embraced on the grounds that they are natural and&nbsp;&nbsp; therefore a safe way of preventing osteoporosis&nbsp; fractures. But it's now becoming clear that&nbsp;&nbsp; taking calcium in one or two daily doses is not&nbsp; natural and that it does not reproduce the same&nbsp;&nbsp; metabolic effects as calcium in food the way&nbsp; nature intended. And furthermore, the evidence&nbsp;&nbsp; is also becoming steadily stronger that calcium&nbsp; supplementation may not be safe. That's why most&nbsp;&nbsp; organizations providing advice regarding&nbsp; bone health now recommend that individuals&nbsp;&nbsp; should obtain their calcium requirement&nbsp; from diet in preference to supplements.
